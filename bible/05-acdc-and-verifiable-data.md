# 05 — ACDC and Verifiable Data {#acdc}

## Thesis {#thesis}

An Authentic Chained Data Container (ACDC) is not a "credential format" in the W3C-VC or SD-JWT sense; it is a fragment of a globally distributed, cryptographically self-addressed property graph whose every node commits to its own content by a SAID, whose edges are typed and operator-bearing, and whose authority is bound not to a signing key but to an issuer's evolving key state anchored in a KEL or TEL. This chapter sets out the machinery that makes that claim precise: SAIDs and the single canonical "most compact form" digest algorithm; the mutually exclusive attribute (`a`) and aggregate (`A`) sections and the partial-vs-selective disclosure split they encode; the edge section and the unary/m-ary operator families that turn a bag of credentials into a reasoned graph of authority; the Ricardian-contract rule section; the graduated-disclosure ladder from compact through metadata, partial, nested-partial, selective, and full disclosure; contractually-protected disclosure (chain-link confidentiality and contingent disclosure); TEL-based registries and the observer/registrar split that refuses phone-home revocation; the IPEX exchange state machine; and the applied corpus (SEDI, GCD, vLEI, the Dossier) that shows what real schemas actually enforce. Throughout, the reference implementation (`keripy`) is treated as ground truth against the spec's prose, and where the two diverge — most sharply in the edge-operator family — that gap is stated plainly rather than papered over.

Adversarial reviewers should carry one framing into every section: ACDC's design goal is not data privacy per se but "the more general goal of protection from the unpermissioned exploitation of data" (ACDC spec > Disclosure Mechanisms, L1659). Privacy mechanisms are means, not ends. A critique that measures ACDC against a pure-privacy or pure-ZK yardstick is measuring the wrong thing.

## Status {#status}

Markers are carried where the tier is load-bearing, which in this chapter means the edge-operator family above all, because that is where the four sources genuinely disagree and where a reviewer is most likely to be misled by whichever one they happened to read.

## 1. What an ACDC fundamentally is {#what-an-acdc-is}

An ACDC is modeled abstractly as an ordered nested field map (`key:value`), serializable in JSON, CBOR, MGPK, or CESR, in which every field map MUST contain a SAID field — making each map a SAD (Self-Addressed Data structure): "One important feature of the fields maps used by ACDC is that they all MUST include a field with a SAID" (ACDC spec > ACDC Message Fields, L2982). The container carries no top-level type field, because "the Schema, `s`, field itself is the type field" (ACDC spec > Type-is-schema, L182); this is the recurring "type-is-schema" principle, whose corollary is that "type information is metadata, not data" (L182). Insertion-ordered field maps MUST be used for canonical serialization — never lexicographic (ACDC spec > Ordered Nested Field Maps, L10) — which is the design's direct answer to the JSON-canonicalization and JSON-LD term-redefinition fragilities that let a signed VC's meaning shift without breaking its signature (papers `keri-primer.md` > 4.1 The engineering bottleneck: text vs. binary, citing W3C vc-data-integrity issue #272).

A set of ACDCs "as nodes connected by edges forms a labeled property graph," each node "universally uniquely identified by the SAID of its ACDC," yielding "securely attributed fragments of a globally distributed property graph... a global verifiable knowledge graph that crosses trust domains" (ACDC spec > ACDCs as secure graph fragments, L1060-1068). No shared or trusted relationship between controllers and verifiers is required (ACDC spec > AID Fields, L95). In Hardman's own framing, ACDC is "not just a format — they're a *methodology* for creating verifiable evidence" (papers `x509-prob.md`), and the container is "not a mutable document; it is a crystallized fact" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3.2 SAIDs).

#### Top-level field order (MUST) {#field-order}

The top-level field order is `[v, t, d, u, i, rd, s, a, A, e, r]` (ACDC spec > Field Ordering, L32); the required fields are `[v, d, i, s]` (ACDC spec > Required Fields, L36). The hardest structural invariant: an ACDC MUST NOT have both a non-empty `a` and a non-empty `A` value (L106, L110) — attribute and aggregate sections are mutually exclusive.

#### Field label restrictions {#field-label-restrictions}

[N1.1] Field labels are about to become constrained, and the constraint is backward-incompatible. A `### Field Label Restrictions` section exists in `v1.1` @`2362e48a1` and nowhere in `main` @`f0bd097de`. Two rules: "A field label in an ACDC MUST NOT, therefore, contain the character `-`" (ACDC spec > Field Label Restrictions, L63 @`2362e48a1`), because the compact CESR serialization of a disclosure path replaces the `/` delimiter with `-` and the result must stay pure Base64; and a lone underscore is reserved, since "The label `_` MUST therefore be reserved as a virtual path component denoting that hop" across an edge (L65), narrowly — "Only that label is forbidden; `_` MAY appear within a longer label, such as `first_name`." The restrictions do not reach the Schema Section's labels, which come from the JSON Schema vocabulary and are never path-descended (L69). A reviewer should register the migration gap rather than the rule. Any already-issued ACDC carrying a label with a hyphen, or a lone `_`, becomes non-conforming the moment `v1.1` lands; `v1.1` argues the cost is low — most languages disallow `-` in an attribute name anyway — and says nothing whatever about migrating the ACDCs that are already in the world. That silence is the open question, not the rule itself.

These restrictions exist to serve the disclosure-path construct, `dp`, which is not an ACDC field at all: "The `dp` field is not an ACDC field. It appears in the messages that negotiate a disclosure, not in the ACDCs that are disclosed" ([N1.1], ACDC spec > Disclosure Paths, L1813 @`2362e48a1`). It is a negotiation construct living in IPEX `exn` messages, and [chapter 08, section 4](08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md#sec-4) owns it. Nothing in this chapter should be read as putting `dp` in a container.

## 2. SAIDs and SAIDification {#saids}

A SAID is "a special type of cryptographic digest of its encapsulating field map (block)" (ACDC spec > SAID Fields, L77). Its load-bearing property is commitment equivalence: "A digital signature on a SAID makes a verifiable cryptographic non-repudiable commitment that is equivalent to a commitment on the full serialization of the associated block from which the SAID was derived" (L81), requiring collision resistance and ~128-bit strength. Because a SAID may itself digest a block containing further SAIDs, the commitment is hierarchical — a single top-level commitment binds an entire tree.

The derivation mechanism is the dummy-fill-then-digest algorithm, and the reference implementation is the ground truth here. `Saider._derive` (keripy `core/coring.py`, `Saider._derive`, L4058-4096): copy the `sad`, fill the id field with the dummy character `#` to the exact full size of the digest (`sad[label] = Dummy * Matter.Sizes[code].fs`, keripy `core/coring.py`, L4085; the dummy "must not be a valid Base64 char", keripy `core/coring.py`, L3949), recompute the version-string size over the dummied serialization if versioned, digest the serialization, then inject the result back into the field. Verification (keripy `core/coring.py`, `Saider.verify`, L4117-4160) re-derives with dummy fill and compares `qb64b`; any exception returns `False` — fail-closed. So the SAID is "a tamper-evident commitment to exact serialized content at exact size"; change any non-ignored byte and verification fails. This is exactly the "44 `#` chars for Base64 256-bit, hash, overwrite" story in the papers (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3.2 SAIDs), made mechanical.

Two subtleties matter for reviewers. First, there MUST be one and only one unambiguous way to compute the SAID of a compactifiable section: the most compact form SAID, computed by depth-first search — SAID the expanded leaves, compact them, ascend computing enclosing-block SAIDs up to the trunk (ACDC spec > Most compact form SAID, L130-151). keripy invariant J3 states this at the credential layer: "An ACDC SAID is computed over its most-compact form, not its uncompacted serialization, so the SAID is stable across compaction/disclosure" (keripy-knowledge `30-invariants.md` > J3; keripy `vc/proving.py`). This stability is what makes graduated disclosure verifiable: "enables verification of portions of a set of nested compactifiable subblocks against their SAIDs without requiring that the whole tree be exposed. This is essential to Graduated Disclosure" (ACDC spec > Most compact form SAID, L136).

Second, the schema SAID uses the field label `$id`, not `d` — a repurposed JSON Schema keyword whose value MUST be the bare schema SAID, not a URI (ACDC spec > Schema ID Field Label, L186-210). In the applied corpus this is stated as a hard rule: "`$id` is a SAID... Edit content -> the SAID changes... Never invent or hand-patch a SAID" (bakobo/schema `KNOWLEDGE_TRANSFER.md`).

#### The UUID field as a blinding factor {#uuid-blinding}

The UUID `u` field is a blinding factor, not decoration. Without it, "an adversary may be able to reconstruct the block contents merely from the SAID... and the Schema... using a rainbow or dictionary attack," because the power set of schema-allowed field values may be far smaller than the digest strength (ACDC spec > UUID Fields, L89). Sufficient entropy in `u` "ensures that the cardinality of the power set allowed by the schema is at least as great as the entropy of the SAID digest algorithm." This is the crisp correction of a common outsider assumption: compactness is not privacy. A public (no-`u`) compact ACDC is still rainbow-attackable — "It only provides compactness, not privacy" (ACDC spec > Targeted Public-attribute, L501). The presence or absence of `u` at top level is precisely what separates a public ACDC (no `u`) from a private one (`u` with entropy), and an empty `u` marks the metadata variant (ACDC spec > ACDC Variants, L120-174).

#### A naming change that is a naming change {#uuid-renamed-ue}

`v1.1` renames the field throughout: `### Universally Unique Identifier (UUID) Fields` on `main` becomes `### Unique Entropy (UE) Fields`, the field tables retitle `u` from "UUID" to "UE", and the same substitution runs through the Edge, Edge-group and Rule sections. The two paragraphs of substance — the rainbow/dictionary attack over the power set of schema-allowed values, `u` as blinding factor, a top-level `u` blinding the whole ACDC — are word-for-word identical on both branches once the term is swapped. This is recorded as terminology and deliberately not as doctrine: nothing above changes, and a reviewer who reads the rename as a change of meaning has read a change that is not there. The name is nonetheless worth knowing, because a `v1.1`-era reader searching for "UUID" will find nothing. This chapter keeps saying UUID, because the quotes it cites are from `main` and say UUID; a citation to `v1.1` says UE.

## 3. Static, immutable schema {#static-schema}

Schemas MUST be completely self-contained and statically fixed: "dynamic Schema references or dynamic Schema generation mechanisms MUST NOT be used" (ACDC spec > Static Schema, L194). The spec names two attacks this prevents. The schema revocation attack: an attacker who can mutate a dynamic schema resource makes validation fail on every ACDC using it, "effectively revok[ing] all the ACDCs that use that dynamic Schema reference" (L196). The semantic malleability attack (a transaction-malleability variant): an attacker "shift[s] the semantics of the dynamic Schema" so the ACDC still validates but downstream behavior changes (L198). Prevention: "all Schemas MUST be static, i.e., Schemas MUST be SADs and therefore verifiable against their SAIDs" (L200). The `$schema` dialect URI "is simply an identifier"; dereferencing it for validation code "would be an attack vector" — the Validator MUST control the tooling dialect (JSON Schema 2020-12) (ACDC spec > Schema dialect, L226). This is the ACDC answer to the JSON-LD `@context` link-rot and term-redefinition problems (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md` > Loss 6: Schema Stability).

A composable schema uses `oneOf` to bundle compact and expanded variants: "a verifiable bundle of metadata (composed) about content that can then be verifiably unbundled (decomposed) later. The Issuer makes a single verifiable commitment to the bundle" (L246). The Validator uses the composed base schema before disclosure and the decomposed schema (compact option removed) after, to force full disclosure. Two schema "versions" coexist and must not be confused: the `$id` SAID is "the normative determiner" used in validation; the semver `version` field is "informative" and is NOT used in validation (ACDC spec > Schema Versioning, L230). Availability is orthogonal to security: "Unavailable does not mean insecure or unverifiable. ACDCs MUST be verifiable when available" (ACDC spec > Schema Availability, L242).

## 4. Attribute section `a` vs aggregate section `A` {#attribute-vs-aggregate}

The two disclosable payload sections encode two different disclosure mechanisms, and choosing between them is the single most consequential schema-design decision.

The attribute section `a` is a labeled field map. Reserved nested labels are `d, u, i, dt` (ACDC spec > Attribute Section, L260). Its presence-of-`i` distinguishes Targeted (an Issuee AID is present — "a provably controllable identifier that is the Target," making the ACDC usable as a Verifiable Credential, L312) from Untargeted ("issued 'to whom it may concern'", verifiable authorship only, no counterparty, no delegation, L330). Crucially the Issuee `i` sits nested in `a`, not at top level, so it is "partially disclosable / correlatable only after Chain-Link Confidentiality [is] accepted" (L316). Because field labels are public (they live in the schema), the attribute section supports partial disclosure — each nested block carries its own `d` + `u`, and a withheld block travels as its bare SAID (bakobo/schema `docs/choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md`).

The aggregate section `A` (capital) is categorically different: "its compact value is an aggregate / AGID (Aggregate ID), NOT a SAID" (ACDC spec > Aggregate Section, L661). It is an ordered array of blinded blocks, each with its own `d` + `u`; "the block labels live *inside* each block, so they're blinded; array position is arbitrary" (bakobo/schema `docs/choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md`). This gives selective disclosure: field labels and positions are hidden, so `anyOf` ordering "is not correlated to the actual order" and cannot leak information by position (ACDC spec > Aggregate Section, L694). The AGID is computed as `AGID = H(C(a_i for i in {0..N}))`: the zeroth list element is dummied with `#` chars, the remaining N elements are block SAIDs, the list is serialized and digested (Blake3-256), and the digest replaces the dummy (L712-747). Its security rests on "sufficient cryptographic entropy of the blinding factors, collision resistance of the digests, and unforgeability of the non-repudiable digital commitments," and the digest-of-ordered-concatenation is "not subject to a birthday attack" (L688, L728). An inclusion proof discloses the detailed block at index j, the full list, the compact ACDC, and the issuance seal; the Disclosee recomputes the block, checks membership, recomputes the AGID, and confirms the KEL seal (L734-750).

#### The deciding question {#deciding-question}

[V] The deciding question, from the applied corpus, is exact: "Does hiding the field labels or positions buy you anything?" (bakobo/schema `docs/choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md`). The aggregate provides label/position blinding, valuable in exactly two cases — the presence/identity of a field is itself sensitive, or the field set is variable/homogeneous. Otherwise the aggregate is "pure cost, because you lose label-based pathing": graduated-disclosure negotiation (apply/offer path lists via keripy's `Pather`) "wants to name a field as `a/residenceState`. In an aggregate you can't." One further caveat worth quoting: "Attribute-form partial disclosure forces the issuee (`a.i`) and the `a`-section metadata to be revealed on any disclosure; an aggregate can disclose one element with no issuee." (bakobo/schema `docs/choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md` > Caveats worth remembering, L65-66 @`cfcbdcc7a`). That caveat is true of the mechanism and, at keripy `80f77b73b`, not true of a registry-backed credential presented through keripy's IPEX — see [section 4.1](#age-credential).

The distinction from partial disclosure is subtle and load-bearing: a partially-disclosed block, when fully disclosed, "exposes, at the very least, the labels of other fields in its enclosing block," whereas a selectively-disclosed block "does not expose any information about other yet-to-be-exposed fields, including their labels" (L2733). Selective disclosure is "separating a 'stew' of 'ingredients' into its constituent ingredients without correlating them via the stew."

#### Where the aggregate's guarantee stops {#aggregate-guarantee-limit}

[N] And there the guarantee stops, by the spec's own account. The worked selective-disclosure example added to `main`'s annex states the residue without hedging: "The verifier does still learn how many elements the section has and which were withheld — the count and positions are structural, not blinded" (ACDC spec > Graduated Disclosure, L5051-5053), and "only the withheld values themselves are protected." The mechanism blinds values and labels, not cardinality and shape. That matters for exactly the schemas the aggregate is good for: an age vector leaks how many thresholds exist and which of them were withheld (eleven thresholds in Sam's current Age schema, six in the Bakobo profile this sentence was first written against), and a schema whose element count varies per holder leaks a per-holder fingerprint before any value is disclosed. A design whose privacy argument depends on the verifier not knowing how many claims are in the set needs a different mechanism — a DAG of separately-issued ACDCs, per the chaining argument at L2739 — not a bigger aggregate.

### 4.1 The age credential, the reference implementation, and two findings (added 2026-10-03) {#age-credential}

The age credential is where the aggregate's design case meets running code, and at keripy `80f77b73b` the two do not agree. Both findings below are recorded in `raw/11` section 1 and section 9 items 1 and 8 with the full chain of citations, and both carry the same boundary: no test in keripy at `80f77b73b` presents an Age credential — `test_ipex_sedi.py` does not import `AgeSchema` at all (keripy `tests/sedi/test_ipex_sedi.py`, L39-50) — so nothing in keripy exercises either collision. They are findings from reading the code paths, not from running them. Both also sit under [section 5.1](#verifying-ground-truth)'s scoping caveat: the IPEX path in question is `acdc/ipexing.py`, which keripy's own CLI does not call.

#### Finding 1: no selective Age presentation through IPEX grant {#age-finding-grant}

[K] Finding 1: an Age credential cannot be presented selectively through keripy's IPEX grant. The chain has four links. Sam's Age schema requires a top-level `rd` (keripy `tests/sedi/test_sedi.py`, L1180), and the fixture issues Age into the state agent's registry (`regid=sueRids[4]`, L4397), so every conforming Age is registry-backed. keripy represents a withheld aggregate element as its SAID string (keripy `core/mapping.py`, `Aggor.disclose`, L1899-1900), which is the shape of the Age schema's withheld arm. For a registry-backed ACDC, `_presentationRegistries` refuses any aggregate entry after the AGID that is not a mapping — "Every inspected aggregate entry must be a mapping." (keripy `acdc/ipexing.py`, `_presentationRegistries`, L1458-1460) — and the code gives the reason: "A verifier cannot distinguish an omitted requirement from an undisclosed one using only its digest." (L1414-1415). That refusal fails the node, and the node fails the grant (`_verifyPresentationAuthGraph` returns `None` at L1508-1512, and the grant handler returns `False` on it at L610-617). So a holder who wants to prove over-21 through this path must disclose every element, including over-40 and over-62, which brackets the age into a band. The rule is not arbitrary: it exists so a hidden element cannot conceal a presentation-registry declaration (see [chapter 10](10-presentation-registries.md)). But it collides with the age credential's central use case, and it contradicts three statements in the applied corpus — the page's "The holder can disclose the over-21 block without disclosing a birth date" (bakobo/schema `sedi-age/index.md`, L3, true of the birth date but not of the other thresholds), bakobo's `prove-over-21` example, which "discloses only its issuee and over-21 blocks" (bakobo/schema `sedi-age/index.md`, L5), and the design doc's "an aggregate can disclose one element with no issuee" ([section 4](#attribute-vs-aggregate)). The schema bakobo validates and the protocol keripy enforces disagree. `raw/11` section 9 item 8 sketches possible resolutions; none is proposed anywhere read, and this chapter does not choose one.

#### Finding 2: the aggregate issuee is read from a fixed position {#age-finding-issuee}

[K] Finding 2: keripy reads an aggregate's issuee from one fixed position, and the schema does not fix it. The library resolves an aggregate ACDC's issuee "for aggregative ACDC .sad["A"][1]['i']" (keripy `core/serdering.py`, L2830), inside a bare `except` that returns `None` (L2837-2844). Sam's Age schema has no `prefixItems`, `minItems` or `contains` on `A` (keripy `tests/sedi/test_sedi.py`, L1204-1493), so nothing requires the issuee block to sit at index 1 or to be disclosed. An Age whose element 1 is withheld or misplaced therefore reads to keripy as untargeted. Bakobo's decision record had already named the consequence from the schema side — the shape "lets a holder withhold element 1 and present a targeted credential as an untargeted affidavit" (bakobo/schema `this.i` > `@lkfnoess`, L2144-2147 @`cfcbdcc7a`) — and sequenced the fix upstream-first. What closes the hole on the IPEX path at this pin is Finding 1: a rule that forbids withholding any element also forbids withholding element 1. The hole is closed by also closing selective disclosure, which is not a fix either finding can live with for long.

#### Sam's sparse-Merkle-tree proposal {#age-smt-proposal}

[P] Sam's mechanism of record for age is something else again. Discussion [#1098](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1098) proposes a static sparse-Merkle-tree predicate rather than a vector of booleans. It opens on staleness — "a static a priori issuance of a given proof of age could become invalid as the individual's age changes." The construction: "The issued ACDC includes an attribute that is the Root of the SMT," with one leaf per date on which a predicate is true, and "A proof-of-age is given by an inclusion proof of a given predicate leaf in the SMT." Leaves are salted from a secret shared at issuance, so "only 2nd parties to a disclosure can construct a valid query of the SMT." Its case against zero-knowledge predicates is detectability, not cost: "ZKPs are not a viable solution to the proof-of-age problem with KERI/ACDC-based proof-of-age entitlements," because with unlinkable ZKPs "the Issuer of the credential is unable, by virtue of that unlinkability, to detect that a credential has been fraudulently issued," and on the holder's side, "Likewise, the key compromise of an Issuee (Holder) can be made detectable using a presentation registry. Which would also be defeated by an unlinkable ZKP." The verdict: "a static SMT predicate proof preserves KERI's security properties, whereas a dynamic ZKP predicate proof defeats them." The discussion is candid about its own limit — "age-range predicate proofs provide only weak protection when used in isolation" — and reframes the problem: "In this light, birthdate privacy is relieving the symptom, not curing the disease," the disease being birthdates used as shared-secret authenticators. This is the same contracts-plus-detection posture as [section 8](#contractual-disclosure), applied to a cryptographic choice, and it is Sam's assessment in a design discussion, not a surveyed result. Provenance caution: the body was last edited 2026-09-26, and without its edit history the presentation-registry sentence is evidence of his position at that date, not of when he first held it (`raw/16` section 1a).

#### Fixture versus proposal {#age-fixture-vs-proposal}

The fixture and the proposal differ in kind, and nothing read says which is the plan. This comparison is interpretive. The fixture's booleans are point-in-time — "Over21 True if age>=21 else False" (keripy `tests/sedi/test_sedi.py`, L1385) is false for a twenty-year-old and stays false after the birthday — which is exactly the staleness #1098 opens by naming; the fixture's remedy is its expiration-date block, which means reissuance. #1098's leaves are dated predicates and stay correct for life. Whether the fixture is an interim stand-in for the SMT, a deliberate alternative, or a demo convenience is not stated anywhere this pass read.

## 5. Edges, chaining, and the operator families {#edges}

Edges connect ACDCs into a DAG: the near node is the enclosing ACDC, the far node is the ACDC referenced by `n` (a SAID) (ACDC spec > Edge Section, L1172). Reserved edge labels are `[d, u, n, s, o, w]` in fixed order (L1149). An edge's `s` field is an additional constraint — the far node MUST validate against both its own schema and the edge's `s` (L1176) — which is what the vLEI ladder uses to const-pin each hop (see [section 11](#applied-patterns)). This is a genuine capability W3C VCs lack: RDF "cannot attach properties to edges directly," forcing reification/"triple bloat," whereas ACDC edges are first-class property-graph objects carrying native `w`/`o`/`s`/`n` (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md` > Loss 3: Edge Weights and Conditional Logic).

#### The edge schema constraint is enforced {#edge-schema-enforced}

[K] The `s` constraint is enforced, and the semantics the code chose are "satisfy", not "equal". `processCredential` — not `verifyChain` — checks an edge's declared far-node schema on a stated reading of the spec: the edge `s` "is a schema the far node must" "*satisfy*, not a SAID that must equal the far node's own schema" SAID (keripy `vdr/verifying.py`, `processCredential`, L196-197 @`80f77b73b`, the code citing Sam Smith and keripy issue #1534). A direct SAID match short-circuits, since the far node already validated against its own schema; where the edge declares a different schema the far node is validated against it, and failure raises `MissingChainError`. A schema not yet cached escrows and cues a query rather than failing, so transient unavailability is distinguished from a real violation (the whole block is L195-230 @`80f77b73b`; the per-branch hints L211-218 and L219-227 are as of `b8f60166b`). This landed between `60ab9e08d` and `b8f60166b` and is unchanged at `80f77b73b`. It means the vLEI const-pinning pattern of [section 11](#applied-patterns) has a verifier behind it rather than only a schema convention — and it changes what an unpinned edge schema means, which matters now that one schema family has stopped pinning ([section 11](#applied-patterns), telco).

#### The m-ary operators {#m-ary-operators}

The m-ary operators (on edge-groups, via `o`): `AND` (default; group valid iff all members valid), `OR` (one member valid), `NAND`, `NOR`, `AVG` (arithmetic average of a member property), `WAVG` (weighted average using `w`) (ACDC spec > Edge Section, L1099-1116). Default when `o` is missing is `AND`; validity logic is EGF-dependent. The table is identical on both spec branches. [K] Two of the six are implemented, a prose-to-code movement first recorded at `b8f60166b`: `EdgeGroupOps = ("AND", "OR")` (keripy `acdc/ipexing.py`, `EdgeGroupOps`, L45), defaulting to `AND` (`groupOp = group.get("o", "AND")`, L870) and reduced at L903 as `return any(results) if groupOp == "OR" else all(results)`, with an empty group treated as malformed (L899-900) — quotes and hints as of `b8f60166b`. By `80f77b73b` 913 lines of the file had changed (+729/−184, `git diff --numstat b8f60166b 80f77b73b`; corrected 2026-10-03 by verification from "grew by 913 lines") and the m-ary AND/OR reduction (now L1020-1053) is unchanged in substance (`raw/09` section 7b). `NAND`, `NOR`, `AVG` and `WAVG` still have no code path anywhere. So "m-ary edge-group operators are read but unused" — the corpus's standing formulation, from keripy landmine L16 — is no longer accurate as written; the accurate replacement is that the two boolean group operators evaluate and the four others do not exist in the implementation. Read the scoping caveat in [section 5.1](#verifying-ground-truth) before leaning on this: the module it lives in is not wired into keripy's own CLI.

#### The unary operators {#unary-operators}

The unary operators (on individual edges, via `o`) are the family reviewers most need to understand, because the gloss and the enforced behavior diverge:

| Op | Spec gloss | Default | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| `I2I` | Issuer-To-Issuee: this ACDC's Issuer AID MUST be the Issuee AID of the far node | Yes (targeted far node) | [N] |
| `NI2I` | Not-Issuer-To-Issuee: no such requirement | Yes (untargeted far node) | [N] |
| `DI2I` | Delegated-Issuer-To-Issuee: this Issuer MUST be the Issuee AID or a delegated AID of the Issuee | No | [N] |
| `NOT` | logical NOT — inverts far node validity | No | [N] |
| `E1E` | IssueE-To-IssueE: "The Issuee AID of this ACDC MUST be the Issuee AID of the node this Edge points to" | No | [N1.1] (and [K]; proposed in [P] #1515) |

#### E1E, the fifth unary operator {#e1e}

The family is no longer four, and the fifth member is not a variation on the first four. `E1E` appears in `v1.1` @`2362e48a1` (ACDC spec > Edge, L1206 @`2362e48a1`) and occurs zero times in `main` @`f0bd097de`; it is [N1.1] and must not be quoted as normative. What makes it worth a reviewer's attention is that it constrains a different thing than its siblings do: "This is an identity relation on the two ACDCs' Issuees and places no constraint on either ACDC's Issuer" (L1206). `I2I` and `DI2I` are delegative — they bind this ACDC's Issuer to the far node's Issuee — whereas `E1E` binds the two Issuees to each other and leaves both Issuers free. Both ends must therefore be Targeted: "both the ACDC in which the Edge resides and the node to which the Edge points MUST be Targeted ACDCs (each MUST have an Issuee), and the Edge is valid when, and only when, those two Issuee AIDs are equal" (L1223). The motivating case is same-subject, different-issuer — a core identity credential and a separate entitlement credential issued to the same Issuee by different Issuers, where "`E1E` binds them as being about the same subject, a relationship the delegative `I2I` Operator cannot express" (L1223), because `I2I` would instead demand that the entitlement's Issuer be the identity credential's Issuee. That is exactly the shape [section 5.2](#same-holder-binding)'s SEDI material used to work around by binding at presentation rather than by edge, and now binds by edge. `E1E` is never a default.

#### Where `E1E` came from, now cited directly {#e1e-origin}

The proposal is Sam Smith's, in keripy discussion [#1515](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1515), New Unary Edge Operators to Support SEDI (opened 2026-07-17): "E1E which means near side IssueE AID must equal far side IssueE AID." [P]. The opening post proposed a larger family — `I1I`, `I1E`, `E1I`, a "3" equivalence class, and `DI1I` — and Sam narrowed it in discussion to `E1E` alone: "The only concrete use case we have is for E1E, and we want to avoid it falling into the unknown operator trap." (keripy discussion #1515, comment of 2026-07-18). So `E1E` is a discussion proposal that keripy implemented and `v1.1` defines; `raw/09` reads the sequence as implementation-led (`[P]` → `[K]` → `[N1.1]`), and nothing read contradicts that, though this chapter has not dated the `v1.1` commit that added it. (Discussion quotes keep Sam's wording as written.)

#### `I1I` and `DI1I`, proposed 2026-10-01 {#i1i-di1i}

[P] Two more operators were proposed on 2026-10-01, and nothing implements them. In a #1515 comment headed "Concrete Use Case in SEDI for I1I and DI1I operators", Sam argues that a SEDI leaf credential's edge to Core should assert issuer identity as well as issuee identity: "the leaf edge to the core is both E1E and I1I  that is issuee is issuee and issuer is issuer." When `I1I` fails, "an alternate edge providing the chain of authority for the issuer is required," and `DI1I` is the delegated variant: "if the delegation edge for the core is DI2I then the for leaf to core edge should have DI1I instead of I1I." Neither appears in either keripy operator table or anywhere in `src/keri` at `80f77b73b`; the words occur only in comments in `tests/sedi/test_sedi.py` (L32-33, L67-68), as design notes in a test file (`raw/09` section 10 item 7). Until one does, an edge carrying `o: "I1I"` is silently dropped to the default operator by `vdr/verifying.py` and rejected as malformed by `acdc/ipexing.py` — [section 5.1](#verifying-ground-truth)'s disagreement, in a case that is about to matter. This chapter's reading, not the discussion's: `DI1I` would inherit `DI2I`'s problem, since it is defined against a delegation that no keripy code path evaluates ([section 5.1](#verifying-ground-truth)). One sentence in that comment also says "I2I" where its context seems to call for "I1I"; `raw/09` section 11 item 8 records it as a probable slip and does not correct it, and neither does this chapter.

The spec's default inference: a targeted far node appends `I2I`, an untargeted far node appends `NI2I`; on a conflict list "the latest Operator... in the list takes precedence" (L1186). A chain of I2I-linked targeted ACDCs "can be used to provide a chain-of-authority... a delegation chain for authorization" (L1203). The gate that triggers the default differs between the branches, and both forms are given here rather than one overwriting the other. On `main` @`f0bd097de` L1197 it fires when `o` "is missing or empty or is present but does not include any of the `I2I`, `NI2I` or `DI2I` Operators"; on `v1.1` @`2362e48a1` L1209 the same sentence reads "any of the `I2I`, `NI2I`, `DI2I`, or `E1E` Operators". The defaults themselves — `I2I` for targeted, `NI2I` for untargeted — are unchanged on both branches. The practical difference is narrow, real, and this chapter's own reading rather than anything either branch states: an edge whose only operator is `E1E` suppresses the default under `v1.1`'s gate and would not under `main`'s, so an implementation that accepts `E1E` while leaving `main`'s gate in place would silently append a delegative `I2I` alongside it and reject exactly the same-subject/different-issuer case `E1E` exists to permit. keripy, as it happens, behaves like the `v1.1` gate — see [section 5.1](#verifying-ground-truth) — which is a coherence worth noting and not a licence to read `v1.1` as settled.

#### NI2I is a deliberate no-op {#ni2i-no-op}

NI2I skips the issuer/issuee binding entirely; it only requires that the node credential exists and is in issued state — a non-authority reference. It is a no-op binding check by construction, not an unimplemented one, and the distinction matters when counting what is enforced.

#### The two modules disagree on three axes {#module-disagreement}

The two modules disagree about edge operators, and the disagreement is exactly the kind of seam this chapter exists to surface. At `b8f60166b` this chapter recorded one axis of disagreement. Widened 2026-10-03: at `80f77b73b` there are three (`raw/09` section 7e, section 9, section 11 item 1; [chapter 08, section 5](08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md#sec-5) and its [section 12](08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md#sec-12) ground-truth matrix carry the detail, and [chapter 07, section 10.5](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#sec-10.5) the shibboleth). In brief:

- Unrecognized tokens. `vdr/verifying.py` drops them and falls through to the default if nothing recognized remains (L435); `acdc/ipexing.py` treats any unrecognized element as malformed and fails closed — `if any(operator not in UnaryEdgeOps for operator in recognizedOps): return None` (keripy `acdc/ipexing.py`, L921-922 @`80f77b73b`), where `None` means malformed and malformed always fails.
- Conflicting delegative operators in a list. `vdr/verifying.py` resolves them latest-wins (L436); `acdc/ipexing.py` deleted its delegative subset and conjoins every listed operator, so `["I2I", "NI2I"]` is `NI2I` to one module and `I2I` to the other.
- An absent `o`. `vdr/verifying.py` infers `I2I` or `NI2I` (L443); `acdc/ipexing.py` applies no relation check at all (L910-911).

The second and third axes matter more than the first, because they give two different answers for edges a conformant issuer may legitimately write. The same edge can pass credential processing and fail IPEX grant verification, or the reverse. The SEDI fixtures use `["E1E", "NI2I"]`, which both modules evaluate identically, so keripy's tests do not exercise the divergence. Neither file acknowledges the other, and neither branch of the specification says what a validator should do with an operator it does not recognize. Silently substituting a default for an operator you failed to parse remains the weaker behavior, and it is still the one in the module that actually ships.

The DAG acyclicity gap survives, and is now a confirmed finding rather than an untested assumption. `_walkGraph` (keripy `acdc/ipexing.py`, `_walkGraph`, L627-719 @`b8f60166b`; L780-872, with the `seen`-set BFS at L808-820, @`80f77b73b`) is a breadth-first walk with a `seen` set, so a cycle among disclosed nodes terminates the walk instead of triggering a rejection — the set is a termination guard, not a validity check, and no code path reports a cycle. `vdr/verifying.py`'s edge walk does not recurse at all; `verifyChain` resolves one hop. ## 6. Rules and Ricardian contracts {#rules}

The rule section `r` is a Ricardian Contract (RC): "both human and machine-readable and referenceable by a cryptographic digest," with the section's top-level SAID `d` providing the digest that "supports the bow-tie model of RC" (ACDC spec > Rule Section, L1239). Rules are terminal (MUST have a legal `l` field); rule-groups are intermediate and may nest; reserved labels are `[d, u, l]` and a Rule MUST NOT have other fields (L1265-1300). A rule with both `d` and high-entropy `u` is private — protected "from discovery via a rainbow table attack merely from its SAID and subschema" (L1313). The spec's worked examples are `warrantyDisclaimer`/`liabilityDisclaimer` and a `permittedUse` clause ("MAY only use this ACDC for non-commercial purposes", L1512).

[V] The applied GCD corpus turns the rule section into a governance surface with five required disclaimers, each quotable doctrine: `noRoleSemanticsWithoutGfw` ("the role field has no enforceable semantics unless the gfw field is also defined"), `issuerNotResponsibleOutsideConstraints`, `noConstraintOutsideConstraints` ("enforceable constraints exist only inside the constraints container, or in the role field when gfw is defined." … "an unrecognized key inside the constraints container MUST be treated as fail-closed."), `useStdIfPossible`, and `onlyDelegateHeldAuthority` ("Issuers agree to only delegate authority that they reasonably believe they hold.") #### Rules are not machine-enforced {#rules-not-enforced}

Load-bearing caveat for reviewers: rules are disclosure and accountability, and in keripy the disclosure/rule layer is largely not machine-enforced by the credential `Verifier`. The keripy crosswalk flags this explicitly: "disclosure rules not enforced in `Verifier`" (keripy-knowledge `20-crosswalk.md`). A rule clause is a commitment a human or an application-layer policy engine must honor; it is not a predicate the reference verifier evaluates. GCD's own doctrine embraces this: "Duties are disclosure and accountability -- a stranger-verifier does not gate on them -- so they live here in r, not in a.constraints." (bakobo/schema `gcd.schema.json`, `r.duties`, L526 @`cfcbdcc7a`; quote corrected to the source wording 2026-10-03). As of GCD 3.1.0 the same division reaches inside `constraints` itself — see [section 11](#applied-patterns).

## 7. The graduated-disclosure ladder {#graduated-disclosure}

"Graduated Disclosure" is recursive least disclosure: "disclose enough to enable more disclosure, which in turn may enable even more disclosure" (ACDC spec > Graduated Disclosure, L1747). (Note the deliberate vocabulary choice: "selective disclosure" is avoided as a top-level term because it "has a long and unpleasant history in financial regulation," per papers `sdjwt-acdc.md` > 1. Similarities.) The ladder's rungs (L1749-1774):

1. Compact Disclosure — a SAID commitment via `oneOf` compact/full; compactness only, not privacy.
2. Metadata Disclosure — the metadata ACDC (empty `u`) lets a Discloser commit to metadata (Issuer, Schema, Edges, Rules) of a yet-to-be-disclosed private ACDC "without providing any point of correlation to the actual top-level SAID." Critically, "When metadata ACDC is disclosed, only Discloser commitments attach, NOT Issuer commitments" — so the Issuer's commitments do not become a correlation point until after the Disclosee agrees to the Rules (ACDC spec > Metadata ACDC, L168-172).
3. Partial Disclosure — SAID + salty nonce; SAID + schema is not enough to discover content.
4. Nested Partial Disclosure — each nested block carries its own `d` + `u`, with per-level `oneOf`, so branches disclose at different nesting depths (L633).
5. Full Disclosure — no hiding.
6. Selective Disclosure — `anyOf` + `oneOf`, order-independent, membership via the SAID set (the aggregate mechanism of [section 4](#attribute-vs-aggregate)).
7. Bulk-issued Instance Disclosure — many non-correlatable instances (see [section 9](#registries)).

"All the Graduated Disclosure mechanisms MAY be used in combination" (L1775). The keripy reference `tests/acdc/test_clc_disclosure.py` is the executable exemplar cited by the SEDI corpus (bakobo/schema `sedi-id/index.md`, L167-168 @`cfcbdcc7a`; the citation moved off `sedi-present-age-portrait/index.md`, and the test itself has not been re-read). keripy's SEDI IPEX tests now carry their own worked graduated disclosure — Residence disclosed as state only, then county and postcode, with both compacted forms asserted to keep the issued SAID (keripy `tests/sedi/test_ipex_sedi.py`, L1494-1496 @`80f77b73b`).

#### Worked examples in the standardizing branch {#ladder-worked-examples}

[N] The ladder now has worked examples in the standardizing branch, and their framing is the useful part. A Graduated Disclosure subsection in `main`'s annex (@`f0bd097de`, L4988-5098) presents two flows explicitly as "how an issuer *plans* for disclosure by the way it structures the credential, and how a holder *performs* disclosure at presentation while a verifier checks it" (L4991-4993). That split — planning at issuance, performance at presentation — is the thing most outsiders miss, because in the SD-JWT and BBS+ worlds disclosure is a runtime capability of the signature scheme rather than a schema-design decision made months earlier. In ACDC the issuer's structural choices fix what can ever be disclosed separately, which is why [section 4](#attribute-vs-aggregate)'s attribute-versus-aggregate question is consequential and irreversible. The first flow walks selective disclosure via an aggregate section: every attribute gets its own element with its own `u` and `d`, the AGID is element 0, the holder "discloses only the chosen elements as full blocks and leaves the rest as their bare SAIDs" (L5031-5032), and the verifier "recomputes the AGID over the mix of disclosed blocks and undisclosed SAIDs and confirms it still equals the committed AGID" (L5048-5049) — with the count-and-position leak recorded in [section 4](#attribute-vs-aggregate). The second walks partial disclosure via compaction, and states the invariant that makes the whole ladder coherent: "Because the top-level SAID is computed over the most compact form, it is identical whether the credential is held compact or expanded" (L5058-5060), so "Every disclosure level... verifies to the same top-level SAID, so what the credential commits to never changes as disclosure progresses" (L5095-5098). It also shows the chain-link-confidentiality ordering concretely rather than abstractly: "Under Chain-Link Confidentiality the rule (terms-of-use) section is disclosed first — so a potential Disclosee can agree to the terms" (L5078-5080) while the attribute section is still a bare SAID. These examples are new to this chapter but not new to `main` — they may read as forward-branch material and are not; they are substantively identical on `v1.1` apart from the UUID-to-UE substitution of [section 2](#saids).

An important disambiguation for reviewers who arrive from the Merkle-tree / transparency-log world: ACDC graduated disclosure and Merkle Tree Certificate inclusion proofs share hash vocabulary but discharge different obligations. ACDC's proof obligation is semantic — "that a particular view corresponds to a specific underlying claim" — motivated by correlation-minimization; MTC's is historical/membership — "is this object a member of a committed set" — motivated by efficiency. "A Merkle inclusion proof establishes placement in a history. A SAID establishes identity of content" (papers `acdc-and-mtc.md` > 3.2 Trees, structure, and meaning). They are orthogonal, not competitive.

## 8. Contractually-protected disclosure {#contractual-disclosure}

Two mechanisms attach obligations to disclosed data (ACDC spec, L1783-1792):

#### Chain-Link Confidentiality (CLC) {#chain-link-confidentiality}

In Chain-Link Confidentiality the offer is a partial disclosure of metadata plus terms; full disclosure follows only after the Disclosee agrees — "permissioned disclosure" in which "the disclosed data has 'strings attached.'" CLC chains Disclosees (distinct from the edge-chaining of ACDCs): "each Disclosee... in turn, is the Discloser to the next," and terms-of-use MUST propagate, imposing "conditions and limitations on the further disclosure and/or use." The SEDI governance framework operationalizes this as the `chainLinkConfidentiality` rule: the verifier MUST NOT "assimilate, aggregate, correlate, sell, or otherwise combine disclosed attributes... for any purpose beyond the disclosed purpose," and "This binds each downstream recipient reached through the edge section of a presentation." (bakobo/schema `sedi-id/rules.json`, L3 @`cfcbdcc7a`). At `cfcbdcc7a` the named clauses are carried as text inside a single `l` string, because "Sam's r object permits only d and l" (bakobo/schema `this.i` > `@2vqmhxai`, L2235). Sam's own fixtures go further: every rule section in them is empty, `{"d": "", "l": ""}` saidified (keripy `tests/sedi/test_sedi.py`, e.g. L4381-4393 @`80f77b73b`), so the chain-link-confidentiality language is Bakobo's profile, not the spec author's.

#### Contingent Disclosure {#contingent-disclosure}

Contingent Disclosure is an obligation in the Rules to disclose when a contingency is met (e.g. breach); the responsible party may be the Discloser or an escrow agent, and the rule references a private ACDC. This enables latent accountability: "Recourse via Full Disclosure of PII is latent... but never realized until the conditions of the contingency is satisfied," limiting PII exposure to a "just-in-time, need-to-know basis" (L1792).

The doctrine that governs when to reach for expensive cryptographic unlinkability instead is blunt: cryptographically-provable correlation resistance is unlinkability, but "this is a weak form... as it does not prevent statistical correlation from contextual information." "There is no cryptographic mechanism that precludes statistical correlation among a set of colluding Verifiers" (ACDC spec > Bulk-issued, L2785). Consequently, advanced cryptographic unlinkability can be "an exercise in diminishing returns" without contractually-protected disclosure. This is a deliberate, load-bearing design position: ACDC leans on contracts-plus-detection where a ZK-maximalist would spend cryptography.

## 9. Registries and TELs {#registries}

A TEL (Transaction Event Log) is "a hash-chained data structure of sealed transaction events" tracking an ACDC's issuance/revocation/transfer state; TEL events are sealed/anchored in a KEL via seals (a seal is an event SAID, optionally plus sequence number). Because they are anchored to a KEL, TELs are "also securely attributable to the KEL's controller" (ACDC spec > TEL Overview, L1918). The persistence property is central: "the verifiability of transaction events in the TEL persists in spite of changes to Key States in the sealing KEL" (L1920). TEL events need not themselves be signed — "the digest in the seal in the KEL is cryptographically equivalent to signing the transaction event itself" (L1946).

#### The registry-dependent issuance lifecycle {#registry-lifecycle}

[N] The spec now walks the whole registry-dependent lifecycle end to end, filling a gap its earlier examples left. The Registry-Dependent Issuance Lifecycle section (`main` @`f0bd097de`, L4852-4986) opens by naming the gap: "The examples above reference each issuer's registry by its identifier (for example `regAmy`) as though it already existed" (L4854-4856). The five steps are `rip` → `acm` → `bup` → verify → revoke, with Amy a school issuing to Bob a student. Two of them carry doctrine a reviewer can use directly. The registry inception is derived, not chosen — "The registry identifier is the SAID of this event, so it is *derived* from the event rather than chosen" — and it is deliberately contentless: "The inception commits to no credential state: it is a vacuous placeholder that reveals nothing about what will later be issued" (L4865-4867). The same self-derivation applies one layer down at issuance: "Because the SAID is derived from the credential's content, it is not known in advance; it is produced by issuance, not assumed" (L4890-4892), which is the mechanical refutation of every design that wants to mint a credential identifier before the credential exists. The `bup` then carries a blinded state block `{d, u, td, ts}` blinded by a nonce derived from a salt Amy shares with Bob and the update's sequence number, and "Only the blinded block's SAID travels in the `bup`; neither the credential SAID nor the word `issued` appears" (L4917-4918). Revocation is a second `bup` whose nonce is independent of the first, so a verifier that once confirmed `issued` "cannot later read the `revoked` state by watching the registry: it would need the salt" (L4979-4981).

#### The lifecycle's own boundary {#lifecycle-boundary}

The example states its own boundary, and the boundary is the quotable part. "The registry *events* remain public and chained (the `rd` and prior links are in the clear); it is the state *content* each event commits to that is protected" (L4981-4983), and therefore: "This is confidentiality of registry state, not anonymity of the credential — issuer, registry, and issuance chronology remain linkable, which is the intended, auditable trade-off" (L4983-4985). A reviewer who reads blindable state as an anonymity mechanism is reading past the spec's own disclaimer. Note also what the lifecycle is not: it is Issuer-controlled end to end — Amy incepts the registry, Amy issues, Amy appends every update, and Bob's only role is holding the shared salt. There is no Issuee-controlled registry anywhere in it, on either branch, which is the load-bearing negative for [chapter 10](10-presentation-registries.md).

Terminology a reviewer should keep straight: ACDCs generically are Verifiable Containers; as entitlements they are Verifiable Credentials; a VCR (Verifiable Credential Registry) is a type of TEL, which is a form of VDR (Verifiable Data Registry); a TEL tracking issuance/revocation is a Revocation Registry (ACDC spec, L1948). keripy's `Tever`/`Tevery` implement this, with registry-inception `vcp` and issue/revoke events `iss`/`rev` (backerless) or `bis`/`brv` (backer) (keripy `vdr/eventing.py`).

The anti-phone-home stance is a deliberate rejection of OCSP/CRL. A Registrar (under the Issuer) maintains and publishes the registry; an Observer (under one or more Validators) caches the registry so Validators "validate the state... without exposing a point of validation (PoV)." "It can mask the usage of a given ACDC from the Issuer." Observer-to-Registrar sync happens on state changes, NOT at the point of validation, which "protects against forced validator-to-issuer correlation of ACDC usage, i.e., no forced phone home validation" (ACDC spec > TEL Registrars and TEL Observers, L1693). Race conditions on rare state changes are handled by "timed grace periods on revocations" (L1695). In Hardman's framing, "A registry consulted at verification time is a phone-home in disguise" (papers `sda.md` > 7. Open loop, not closed); the corollary invariant is that "verification must succeed from the credential and key-state alone, and any registry or log is an issuance-time and audit-time convenience, never a verification-time dependency."

#### Registrar and Observer as a governance split {#registrar-observer-governance}

The split is a governance decision, not a caching optimization — a point the spec never makes but Sam states directly. At KERIcon 2026 he presented Registrar/Observer as a deliberate replay of the witness/watcher structure one layer up: "notice that this models the witness watcher governance structure. So, we learned a lesson. We shouldn't have shared governance. So, we have witnesses that are controlled by controllers, watchers that are controlled by verifiers. We split the verification between two places. We do the same thing with ACDCs" (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 16:48; `raw/14`). The Observer's remit is correspondingly thin — "All our observer is doing is it's keeping track of the registry state" (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 32:13) — and it is layered on, not substituted for, KEL assurance: asked why Observers and watchers must be separate components, "they have different purposes... They depend on watchers, but they aren't watchers" (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 52:25). The cleanest one-line distinction in the corpus is from the same Q&A: "watchers watch the KEL... looking for duplicity in your KEL. Observers are watching the state of issuances that are anchored to the KEL... it's a TEL observer." (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 52:00). Caution on provenance: these are hand-edited auto-captions, `[SAM-DIRECT]` in substance but not verbatim in wording — see `raw/14` section 0 before quoting any of it to Sam or into a spec issue.

#### The split's proposed third leg {#proposed-third-leg}

The split has a proposed third leg. Registrar (Issuer-controlled) and Observer (verifier-controlled) leave the Holder as the one party to a transaction running no state infrastructure of its own. A presentation registry — an Issuee-controlled blindable state registry that a `grant` must be anchored in — closes that, giving the Issuee the same detect-and-recover capability the Issuer already has against impersonation. It is pre-normative and unimplemented; see [chapter 10](10-presentation-registries.md), and note that its origin document calls it an Issuee usage registry (keripy #1095).

#### Bulk updates as a privacy requirement {#bulk-updates}

Bulk updates are a privacy requirement, not a performance one. The spec mentions "optimized batch synchronization" in a performance register (L1695); the talks make batching load-bearing. "if you update it instantly, then the observer could correlate a change to some registries and not others. But if all the registries, 100% of them update at the same clock time... then there's [synchronization] in the time of update" (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 32:48), generalized to a design law in the same talk: "if you want to make it so that it's not statistically correlatable, you have to do it in a bulk or herd privacy protected way." (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 33:14). The batch window closes correlation in both directions — backward from a point of validation to the state change that preceded it, forward from a state change to the validations that follow. The residual weakness is named by Sam himself, and a reviewer should quote it rather than overclaim: "if an observer is malicious and the issuer is malicious, they can collude... but there's no phone home here. The issuer can't force an observer... There's no technical thing that makes it impossible in this, but what it does is it means that there's no easy way for them to do it" (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 34:46). The claim is that collusion is unforced, out-of-band and legally repressible — the same technical-plus-legal posture as the rest of the ACDC privacy story ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#sec-4), the three-party exploitation model), not a cryptographic impossibility.

#### Usage registries {#usage-registries}

Usage registries are where presenter-side compromise detection actually lives, and they are the thing most often misattributed to Observers. Observers cache state; they detect nothing. Detection of a presenter's key compromise comes from a second registry controlled by the issuee, in which every presentation is anchored: "if somebody steals my private keys as a presenter, I know that they stole them because the only way that they can gain access is to anchor it in my registry that I control" (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Future of KERI, 13:39). The verifier then checks two registries, the issuer's and the presenter's, "so that both the issuer and the user can detect if either one has been compromised and the verifier knows it." (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Future of KERI, 15:15). It is opt-in and it costs ephemerality — "Makes the presentation less ephemeral" (KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Future of KERI, 13:28) — and it is the mirror of the issuer-side anti-forgery story at L1687-1689, applied at the other end of the presentation. Tier this carefully: it is talk-only. The sole trace in the specification is the `rd` field description listing registry kinds as "Issuance and/or revocation, transfer, retraction, or usage registry" (ACDC spec > Top-Level Fields, L48); there is no usage-registry section, and Sam's own framing of it is a novelty claim ("a new thing that nobody else does", KERIConf26 caption, Sam Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 14:38).

#### Implementation status of Registrar and Observer {#registrar-observer-status}

Implementation status (2026-09-01): neither component exists. `keri-foundation/observer` and `keri-foundation/registrar` are public placeholder repos holding a LICENSE and a one-line README; `src/keri/acdc/registraring.py` in keripy is a docstring-only stub Sam created 2026-07-02, and keripy has no observer module at all. What is implemented is the layer beneath — the blindable `bup` update event and `BlindState` structures (keripy `core/structing.py`; keripy `acdc/messaging.py`, `blindate()`) and the `RegBaser`/`WebRegBaser` registry stores. Nothing anywhere specifies the Observer wire protocol, how a validator discovers or pools Observers, or how the batch-window-versus-revocation-latency trade should be set. A reviewer should treat Registrar/Observer as architecturally committed and operationally unbuilt, and should not accept "the Observer handles that" as an answer to a live design question.

#### Binding ACDC state to issuer key state {#key-state-binding}

Binding ACDC state to issuer key state is the core anti-forgery doctrine, and it is where ACDC most sharply corrects the W3C-VC/SD-JWT prior. ACDCs are "not directly signed by the Issuer... bound to the Issuer's Key State... and the Issuer's Key State is signed. This enables the Key State of the Issuer to change independently of the ACDC state" (ACDC spec > Binding to Key State, L1673). The explicit anti-pattern: in "other verifiable credential schemes, where the credentials are signed directly... a key rotation forces all the credentials signed with a given set of keys to be revoked; otherwise, a key compromise would enable the compromiser to issue... forged [credentials]" (L1675). This is the retrograde attack defense (papers `was.md`): a bare signature "provides no assurance that the private key was used to sign the message at that time" (NIST SP 800-102, via papers `keri-primer.md` > 3.3 Anchored vs. paired signatures), so ACDC uses anchored rather than paired signatures — validity is judged against issuer key state "AS OF its KEL anchor's sequence position, not current key state or wall-clock" (keripy invariant H1, keripy-knowledge `30-invariants.md` > H1; enforced in `Tever.verifyAnchor`).

#### The Dossier specification on anchoring {#dossier-anchoring}

[N] A second standardizing specification now says this in its own words, and it is the better citation to hand an outsider. The Dossier specification at `main` @`b9227753c` (2026-08-25) swept its whole text to distinguish anchoring from signing, adding a normative section titled "How a Dossier Is Issued: Anchoring, Not Attached Signatures" that opens with a flat denial — "A dossier is not a signed document. The issuer does not compute a signature over the dossier ACDC and attach that signature to it as proof of authenticity" (Dossier spec > How a Dossier Is Issued: Anchoring, Not Attached Signatures, L13-14) — and locates the signature precisely: "The signature exists on the anchoring key event, not on the dossier" (L27-28). It reaches the verifier as an obligation: "A verifier MUST NOT treat an attached signature as equivalent to an anchor when evaluating a dossier as of any referenceTime other than the present" (L77-79). What makes this the citation of choice is not that it is stronger but that it is a correction — a working spec author found the signing/anchoring conflation dangerous enough to sweep an entire document for it and define three terms to stop it recurring — and that it argues from key rotation rather than from ACDC internals, which is the argument an arrival from the VC world can follow.

#### Two refinements {#anchoring-refinements}

Two refinements guard against reading that too absolutely, and a reviewer should carry both. First, the partition is drawn on lifespan, not on artifact kind. The spec partitions its own artifacts explicitly: "key events are signed, and so are the short-lived transactional objects described under *Dossiers and Derivative References*. Dossiers, endorsements, and other ACDCs are anchored." A token "is one of the few artifacts in this specification that genuinely bears an attached signature... it can afford one because its lifetime is measured in seconds and it is never expected to outlive a key rotation" (L473). Second, an attached signature is explicitly permitted in the ephemeral case: a dossier that lives only for one interaction and will never be verified after the issuer's next rotation MAY be authenticated by an attached signature, at the stated price that it "ceases to be verifiable once the signing key is no longer part of the current key state" and that the issuer's commitment "becomes repudiable in practice." So the shibboleth "ACDCs are never signed" overstates the case. The accurate form — and the one a reviewer should use, because it survives the obvious counterexample — is that nothing expected to outlive a key rotation is authenticated by an attached signature.

#### Recon hypothesis: anchors not re-verified on presentation {#verifyanchor-hypothesis}

A serious caveat, flagged as a keripy recon hypothesis (L15, HIGH severity, medium confidence). The credential `Verifier.processCredential` checks registry existence, TEL state, freshness, schema, and chain edges, but recon "found no call to `Tever.verifyAnchor`" in that path — anchor validation appears to happen only when a TEL event is ingested by `Tevery`, "not when a credential + proof is verified." If confirmed, "a credential presented with a forged `(seqner, saider)` proof could pass schema + TEL-state checks without the anchor being confirmed against the issuer's actual KEL at that sn — undermining invariant H1" (keripy-knowledge `31-landmines.md` > L15). A reviewer relying on anchor-time-of-signing verification must confirm the ingest path actually ran `verifyAnchor` on the events in question; do not assume the credential-verification call re-checks it.

#### Revocation is a state, not a deletion {#revocation-state}

Revocation is a state, not a deletion. The keripy verifier explicitly saves a revoked credential rather than raising: "Log this and continue instead of the previous exception so we save a revoked credential" (keripy `vdr/verifying.py`, L129-132). State is queried from the TEL (`vcState`), not from a central revocation list. This is the direct machine-level contradiction of the "revocation = CRL/blocklist" mindset.

#### Blindable state (BLID) {#blindable-state}

Blindable state (BLID) extends registries to private/blinded state: a blinded attribute block `[d, u, td, ts]` whose SAID is computed over a fixed-field concatenation, derived from an Issuer+Discloser shared salt via HD derivation keyed by sequence number, so "an observer cannot correlate state to event updates" (ACDC spec > Blinded State Disclosure, L2043-2135). The Discloser recovers state by brute-forcing the small combination space of possible `td` x `ts` until the BLID matches (only ~6 combinations in the revocation example, L2288). Placeholder decorrelation (publishing empty state before any real ACDC exists) and continued blind updates after revocation "decorrelate the time of revocation" (L2324). Herd privacy can be strengthened with Sparse Merkle Trees amalgamating all seals into one KEL root, giving `O(log N)` inclusion proofs where "a given seal in the KEL... no longer provides a point of correlation to any other transaction event" (L2914).

## 10. IPEX — the issuance and presentation exchange {#ipex}

The ACDC spec's IPEX section is explicitly non-normative (ACDC spec, L1795), but the framing is doctrine: "all exchanges (both issuance and presentation) MAY be modeled as the disclosure of information by a Discloser to a Disclosee. The difference between exchange types is the information disclosed, not the mechanism" (L1797). IPEX rides routed KERI `exn` messages, with routes:

| Route | By | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `apply` | Disclosee | initiate; defines wanted disclosure (schema, label list, aggregate elements, signature) |
| `offer` | Discloser | initiate; proposes acceptable disclosure (Metadata ACDC or SAID, partial disclosure, signature) |
| `agree` | Disclosee | accepts the offer |
| `grant` | Discloser | initiate; discloses the agreed content (Full or Selective Disclosure ACDC, signature) |
| `admit` | Disclosee | confirms receipt |
| `spurn` | either | rejects apply/offer/agree/grant |

The keripy implementation is a strict state machine, not a message log, and this is where prose and code agree cleanly. `Ipexage(apply, offer, agree, grant, admit, spurn)` with a legal-predecessor table `PreviousRoutes` (keripy `vc/protocoling.py`, `PreviousRoutes`, L17-23): `offer` <- `apply`; `agree` <- `offer`; `grant` <- `agree`; `admit` <- `grant`; `spurn` <- any of the four. `IpexHandler.verify` enforces it: `apply` may only start an exchange (empty prior `p`); `offer`/`grant` may start or must reference a legal predecessor; `admit`/`agree`/`spurn` may never start; and there is no double-response — "reject if a response to that prior already exists" (keripy `vc/protocoling.py`, `IpexHandler.verify`, L60-113). Unmatched routes fail closed. This corrects the outsider assumption that "peer disclosure messages get logged like credentials / like a VC exchange endpoint" — out-of-order or duplicate-response messages are rejected, not merged (`raw/09` section 4).

The commitment mechanics that make IPEX safe across variants: a commitment (signature or KEL-anchored seal) to any variant commits to the shared top-level section fields of all variants, because each section value is SAD-or-SAID. "A signature on any variant MAY be used to verify the Issuer's commitment to any other variant... on a top-level section-by-section basis." The variants form a hash tree, and "A commitment to the top-level SAID of the compact version... is equivalent to a commitment to the hash tree root," so the Issuer MUST provide a signature or seal on the SAID of the most compact form variant (ACDC spec > Issuer Commitment Rules, L1843). Mutual anchoring — issuer logs "I issued," holder logs "I accepted" — "prevents spam (you can't force a credential into someone's wallet) and provides non-repudiation of receipt" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3.1 Issuance model).

#### Bespoke and disclosure-specific ACDCs {#bespoke-acdcs}

A Discloser may issue its own ACDC referencing another via an edge, naming the Disclosee as Issuee, to attach context-specific obligations and enable "rich presentation" (combining attributes from multiple edge-referenced ACDCs) "without requiring any new tooling" (ACDC spec, L1860). The SEDI corpus refines this. ~~"'Bespoke' describes an *issuance pattern*... not a credential *type* — so there is no useful generic 'bespoke' schema. What *is* reusable is a **named verification pattern**," and "The club's accepted shape and the holder's issued presentation are one schema viewed from two sides" (`sedi-present-age-portrait/index.md`).~~ Re-cited 2026-10-03: that wording is no longer on the page; the doctrine stands in Bakobo's decision record, where "Bespoke" "names an ISSUANCE PATTERN, not a credential TYPE" and "the club's accepted-shape and the holder's issued presentation conform to that ONE schema viewed from two sides" (bakobo/schema `this.i` > `@sdp3wk`, L1950-1960 @`cfcbdcc7a`). The page itself now says the presentation "is a named presentation pattern, not a Sam Smith schema." (bakobo/schema `sedi-present-age-portrait/index.md`, L3). One reversal belongs here: the presentation, once deliberately unregistered so it would not be logged, now requires `rd` — the holder's presentation registry, which Sam's Core names in `a.rd` as the "Issuee Presentation Registry SAID" (same page, L5). ## 11. Real-world credential patterns and what schemas enforce {#applied-patterns}

#### vLEI {#vlei}

vLEI is the production proof point (ISO 17442-3, finalized Oct 2024): GLEIF -> Qualified vLEI Issuers -> legal entities -> role credentials -> individuals, each hop a SAID-linked, schema-pinned edge. "A verifier receiving an employee's credential can traverse the chain back to GLEIF without consulting a trust registry. The entire graph is self-certifying" (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md` > Real-World Validation). [V] Concretely, the Legal Entity vLEI carries edge `qvi` -> the QVI credential with `s` const-pinned to the QVI schema, and the ECR Authorization vLEI carries edge `le` -> the LE vLEI with `s` const-pinned to the LE schema SAID (public-schema `vLEI/acdc/legal-entity-vLEI-credential.json`, L99, and `ecr-authorization-vlei-credential.json`, L114, re-verified @`95a07e544`; the `vLEI/` tree is unchanged since `8db57eb75`). The chaining "eliminates the need for trust registries" whose rise "is proof that this conceptual gap needs plugging" (papers `x509-prob.md`).

#### Telco schemas stopped pinning far-node schemas {#telco-unpinned}

[V] One schema family stopped pinning far-node schemas, and that is not the same as weakening the graph. Between public-schema `8db57eb75` and `95a07e544`, Provenant removed the SAID-valued `const` from the edge `s` fields of its telco credentials — commit `2c8e834e2`, "remove the const schema SAID constraints from the vvp-dossier schema" — so that the `tnalloc` edge's `s` is now only `"SAID of TN allocation schema"` with `"type": "string"` (public-schema `vvp-dossier/vvp-dossier.schema.json`, L180), and no edge in the current telco schemas carries a SAID-valued `const`. The intent is written down for one edge: tn-alloc's `issuer` edge is "Intentionally generic — does not constrain the schema of the identity credential." (public-schema `tn-alloc/index.md` > Edge structure, L51). Read together with [section 5](#edges)'s `[K]` paragraph, what moved is who restricts the edge schema, not whether it is checked: keripy still validates the far node against whatever `s` the issued edge carries, and what the schema no longer does is restrict which `s` an issuer may write, which leaves that to verifier policy. That reading is interpretive; neither repo states the reason. It runs opposite to the vLEI ladder and to bakobo's SEDI presentations, which still pin.

#### SEDI {#sedi}

[V] SEDI (Self/State-Endorsed Digital Identity) applies KERI's root-of-trust to civil identity: "The state endorses an identity; it does not create one." — withdraw the endorsement and the identity persists, "because the holder, not the state, holds the key." (bakobo/schema `sedi-id/index.md` > Purpose, L15-17 @`cfcbdcc7a`). The family was rebuilt between this chapter's passes, so read what follows as current state with its history marked. At `cfcbdcc7a` the state-issued credentials are Sam Smith's own — IAR, Core, Residence and Age, pinned to his keripy test module at `80f77b73b` (bakobo/schema `README.md` > Status, L65) — and `sedi-id` is "Superseded for new issuance" while it "remains resolvable for existing credentials" (bakobo/schema `sedi-id/index.md`, L3). The presentation, guardian, ward and authorization schemas "are Bakobo profiles for the November SEDI Summit; they are not Sam's schemas or statements of Utah policy." (bakobo/schema `README.md` > Status, L65). Nothing in the active family contradicts the founding inversion: "The state issues Core to the citizen." (bakobo/schema `sedi-core/index.md`, L3).

~~Schema enforces minimalism: exactly four attributes (name, birth date, image, Utah residence).~~ Superseded 2026-10-03 for the active family. That was `sedi-id`, and it is still on that historical page. Sam's Core does not hold to four: its required blocks include given, middle and family name, name suffix, birth date, facial image proof, legal presence status, issued and expiration dates, and a bare boolean `primary` (bakobo/schema `sedi-core.schema.json`, `a.required`, L67-81) — and `primary` is not a blinded block, so it cannot be withheld while `a` is expanded (keripy `tests/sedi/test_sedi.py`, L587 @`80f77b73b`). Residence left Core and is its own credential, every block of which has a compact arm. So the decomposition principle survives one level up: residence is split from identity at the credential level, still because "a single residence block would force over-disclosure on every verifier who only needs jurisdiction." (bakobo/schema `sedi-id/index.md`, L70-71, historical but still the clearest statement). The image is still a commitment rather than a locator — Core's image block is "Facial Image Proof Value as SAID of typed media block" (bakobo/schema `sedi-core.schema.json`, L293) — and the old reasoning for that stands on the `sedi-id` page: a download URL is "phone-home by another name" and an availability chokepoint. Whether Core's `legalPresenceStatus` is an endorsed attribute rests on relayed correspondence and contradicts the governance artifact's `noOverAssertion` clause; `raw/11` section 9 item 3 holds that tension, and this chapter does not resolve it.

#### The Utah delegation chain {#utah-delegation-chain}

The Utah "delegation chain" is a chain of credentials, not KERI delegation. Sam's fixture heads it "Create State's three level delegation chain AIDs" / "Root AID -> Org Unit (division/department/program) -> Issuing Agent" (keripy `tests/sedi/test_sedi.py`, L3165-3166 @`80f77b73b`), but every one of those AIDs is made with plain `incept`, not `delcept`; a search of the file for `delcept`, `dip` and `delpre` finds nothing. The chain is ACDCs: the root issues an Organizational Unit credential, the unit issues an Issuing Agent credential whose `orgUnit` edge is `"o": "I2I"` (L3598-3605), and the agent issues each citizen's Core with a `utahAgent` edge, also `I2I`, to its Agent credential (L3997-4004). Leaves — Residence, Age — reach the chain only through their `coreIdentity` edge to Core. A reviewer who reads "delegation" here as KEL delegation, with its cooperative `dip`/`ixn` binding and superseding recovery, has read a different mechanism. This is fixture evidence of the spec author's intended design, and it is the same move GCD made below: authority evidenced by chained credentials, with KEL delegation optional. The fixture also defines a "SEDI AID Replace Schema" (L200) that names an obsolete AID and its replacement — AID replacement by credential, for a holder who has lost control of an identifier, which KERI rotation does not cover; the fixture does not say when it is used rather than rotation.

~~`sedi-guardian` enforces the anti-impersonation invariant: the guardian holds the credential and the ward is named only by a `subject` edge — "Collapsing the two (guardian-as-subject) is the classic impersonation/commingling failure the whole field warns against" — and it is registry-bound (`ri` mandatory) because guardianship "terminates dynamically... a verifier MUST check current status, not just the signature and dates."~~ Superseded 2026-10-03. Version 3.0.0 "names the guardian as its issuee (`a.i`) and the ward by AID in the separately disclosable `a.ward` block", and "There is no guardian-to-ward edge." (bakobo/schema `sedi-guardian/index.md`, L3); the reason given is that "This avoids a cyclic pair of edges and allows the ward identity to be withheld independently." (bakobo/schema `this.i` > `@6ge27cya`, L2254-2255). The quoted "classic impersonation/commingling failure" sentence is absent from the whole tree at `cfcbdcc7a`. The invariant itself survives in the schema — `a.i` is "Issuee = the GUARDIAN's AID (holder != subject)" (bakobo/schema `sedi-guardian.schema.json`, L81) — and so does registry binding, now in the v2 envelope: `rd` is "MANDATORY; guardianship terminates dynamically" (same, L43). The link back runs from the ward: Sam's Core carries an optional `guardians` edge group, but in his fixture it is schema-only — its operators are unconstrained strings, and no ward Core is issued at `80f77b73b` — so "a verifier must hold the group to `AND` or `OR` and each member to the guardianship schema and `NI2I`" (bakobo/schema `sedi-core/index.md`, L3).

#### GCD {#gcd}

[V] GCD (Generalized Cooperative Delegation) is the applied delegation model, at version 3.1.0 on bakobo/schema `cfcbdcc7a`. ~~The delegation relationship itself is "not embodied in a credential" but in the cooperative two-way AID binding (delegate inception + delegator interaction).~~ Superseded 2026-10-03: that wording is still on public-schema's GCD page, but Bakobo removed it in GCD 2.0.1. KERI cooperative delegation is now "the exemplar these credentials were named for" (bakobo/schema `gcd/index.md` > Purpose, L7), "**But it is not a precondition.**" (L9): "A GCD credential is a standard targeted ACDC; its authorization and constraint mechanics do not depend on the issuee being KEL-anchored to the issuer." (L9). The decision record states the precision that keeps this from weakening GCD — what is decoupled is delegated authority, which GCD documents, from the cooperative-delegation KEL binding, "a stronger, optional fact"; dip+ixn "is now the EXEMPLAR, not the gate" (bakobo/schema `this.i` > `@n8wq3d`, L258-263). What survives intact is GCD's job: "Whichever binding is used, it only proves the *state* of the delegation relationship and defines how it is controlled." (bakobo/schema `gcd/index.md`, L11), and GCD says what actions are expected and what constrains them.

Two further changes matter to a reviewer. `rd` is required from GCD 3.0, overriding the spec's default on purpose: "the ACDC spec makes `rd` optional for correlation minimization, but a GCD is authority evidence, so secure discovery of its revocation registry wins here." (bakobo/schema `gcd/index.md` > Schema, L37-40). And the `constraints` container no longer enforces fail-closed in the schema. ~~Its `constraints` container is fail-closed (`additionalProperties: false`; an unrecognized key MUST deny), while its descriptive `facet` container is open/forward-compatible — "the open-vs-closed choice tracks descriptive-vs-gating."~~ Superseded 2026-10-03: as of 3.1.0 the container is `"additionalProperties": true` (bakobo/schema `gcd.schema.json`, L130), so that custom constraints named by a governance framework can be expressed at all. The deny-unknown rule is unchanged, but it moved from the schema to the verifier: "This container remains FAIL-CLOSED, but the obligation runs to the verifier, not to schema validation" (L128), and "Validating against this schema is not an authorization decision" (bakobo/schema `gcd/index.md` > Custom constraints, L102). The descriptive-versus-gating distinction now lives in the verifier obligation and the `noConstraintOutsideConstraints` clause ([section 6](#rules)), not in `additionalProperties`. The accepted cost is stated: "a verifier that gates on schema validation ALONE loses a free backstop" (bakobo/schema `this.i` > `@vy7qoj`, L360-361). This is the general shape the applied corpus keeps arriving at — the schema checks shape, the verifier decides — and it is the same move as the telco edge schemas above. The `acts` grid crosses five effects (observe/create/modify/preserve/destroy) with six state-kinds (info/record/commitment/authority/resource/relationship): "the two axes of one coordinate, neither meaningful alone." A consequence reviewers must note: "these credentials do not support graduated disclosure. All constraints must be disclosed every time a verifier is evaluating delegated authority" — because within a field values are ORed and across fields ANDed, so partial disclosure would silently loosen the gate (bakobo/schema `gcd/index.md`, L86). This is the general rule: authority/legal credentials are disclosed whole; identity/attribute credentials support graduated disclosure. Qualified 2026-10-03: `sedi-guardian` 3.0.0, an authority credential, now carries its ward as "Ward AID in an individually disclosable block" (bakobo/schema `sedi-guardian.schema.json`, L101), so the guardian can withhold whose guardian it is — the rule bends where the subject's identity is itself the sensitive datum.

#### The Dossier {#dossier}

The Dossier is itself a valid ACDC used as a curated bundle of evidence: "The primary payload of a dossier is not a set of direct claims, but rather a graph of references to external evidence." ~~What the issuer's signature attests is composition/integrity, "NOT veracity."~~ Superseded 2026-09-15 at Dossier `main` @`b9227753c`: the veracity half stands, the signature half does not, because there is no issuer signature on a dossier at all. What attests composition is the issuer's anchor: "The issuer of a dossier is the entity that curates the collection of evidence and attests to its composition by anchoring the container" (Dossier spec > The Role of the Issuer, L83), while the veracity boundary is unchanged — "The issuer does not necessarily attest to the veracity of the claims within the evidence, but rather to the integrity and composition of the collection itself" (same). See [section 9](#registries) for the general doctrine this is an instance of, and for the two refinements that keep it from being overstated. This encodes the authenticity-vs-veracity distinction — authenticity is mechanizable, veracity "inherently require[s] subjective assessments of reputation" (papers `authenticity-vs-veracity.md`). The Dossier adds joint-issuance threshold operators (`MxN`, `RMxN`, `MxQ`, `RMxQ`) in an edge-group's `o`, weighted so Endorsed slots sum to >= unity — "the same fractionally weighted threshold KERI uses for key-event signing thresholds (`kt`)." Its key contrast with group multisig: joint issuance "relies on logic within the ACDC layer," letting members "anchor their endorsements of a dossier at different times and via different channels, each in their own KEL, without immediate impact on a shared one" — a wording the same 2026-08-25 sweep sharpened rather than weakened, since members are not adding signatures to a shared object at all but each writing to a log they alone control. Validity is thereby decoupled from synchronous key-event coordination. The sweep had a wire-format consequence a reviewer must not file as editorial: rewriting the two normative schemas' prose descriptions in anchoring rather than signing terms changed their SAIDs — the base dossier schema from `ECpZgR2Y…` to `EKuLzS_oN_mIao5og1NOujtF2QMXloiMCZP7xuAR5tY7`, and the endorsement schema from `EAfn0gRM…` to `ECRjgun8t3cay_YBiVxEY-qXKCz37oUrvE5GvmySwxCh`. Because a dossier schema is identified as one by an `allOf` `$ref` naming the base schema's SAID, and because a joint-issuance slot names the endorsement schema by SAID in its `s` field, any schema or slot still naming an old SAID no longer resolves. This chapter never quoted either value, so nothing here was stale; anything built against the pre-August schemas is. Foreign material (W3C VC, ISO mDL, PDFs, X.509) is bridged via a Foreign Artifact wrapper ACDC, never referenced directly — "Direct reference to non-ACDC material without a wrapper is NOT RECOMMENDED." The FAA corpus makes the trust stance explicit: a bridge issuer's `art_posture` and `rev_latency` fields let "verifiers to treat the FAA as a proxy for the foreign credential, assuming they trust the FAA issuer" — trust in a bridge is "explicit and issuer-scoped, not systemic."

#### A deployed dossier schema's evidence rule {#deployed-dossier-schema}

[V] A deployed dossier schema states the evidence rule outright (added 2026-10-03). Provenant's revised dossier base at public-schema `95a07e544` (`dossier-base/dossier-base.schema.json`, `$id` `EHxI61CGs7FRuStz8P6QWJyMK4u2bGH1LQqIXsA4bT7q`) puts the division of labour between sections in its top-level description: "A dossier SHOULD NOT include an issuee; all evidence MUST be carried in e, never in a." (public-schema `dossier-base/dossier-base.schema.json`, L5), with `a` as metadata that "MUST NOT carry primary evidence." (L54). Type membership is structural, as in the specification: "A schema S IS-A dossier iff S's top-level allOf array contains a $ref to this schema's SAID, or recursively to another schema that IS-A dossier." (L5). It also states a reissuance doctrine for evolving evidence — "When phase transitions, a new dossier version SHOULD be issued." (L127) — so a dossier is a point-in-time artifact, not a live document. Two cautions. Its base-schema SAID is not the Dossier specification's (`EKuLzS_…`, above), and bakobo/schema still publishes an older base (`EFf_1u…`), so three dossier bases are now in circulation; this chapter's comparison of the values is its own, and whether either schema repo intends to track the specification exactly was not checked (`raw/11` section 9 item 9). And Provenant's one derived dossier, `vvp-dossier` 2.0.0, inherits that base while still requiring the v1 `ri` where the base offers `rd`.

#### The negative corpus {#negative-corpus}

[V] The negative corpus (what real schemas reject in CI) is the clearest statement of schema teeth, re-checked against directory listings at bakobo/schema `cfcbdcc7a` (fixture bodies not opened): GCD rejects a missing `rd`, `terminatingEvents` without a `validUntil` backstop, and two-sided braces in `acts`; the historical `sedi-id` rejects a block missing its blinding nonce, a missing issuee, or an image missing its digest; `sedi-present-age-portrait` rejects a non-I2I edge operator and a presentation missing `rd`. Two entries reversed, and both reversals are doctrine. ~~GCD rejects an unknown key inside `constraints` (fail-closed)~~ — that fixture was deleted at 3.1.0, because "an unknown key inside `constraints` is now *valid*" (bakobo/schema `gcd/index.md`, L143-144), and fail-closed moved to the verifier as above. ~~`sedi-present-age-portrait` rejects a registry-bound (`rd`) presentation~~ — inverted to `missing-rd`, with the presentation-registry reversal ([section 10](#ipex)). These rejections are where schema stops being documentation and becomes enforcement; the two reversals are where a schema author decided enforcement belonged somewhere else.

## 12. What ACDC corrects, and what remains uncertain {#corrections-and-uncertainty}

#### Where ACDC corrects outsider priors {#outsider-priors}

ACDC explicitly corrects these outsider priors (well-established, spec-grounded): direct-signing of credentials (rejected — forces mass revocation on rotation, L1675); phone-home revocation via OCSP/CRL (rejected via Observer/Registrar split, L1693); dynamic/URL-dereferenced schemas (rejected — schema-revocation and semantic-malleability attacks, L194); treating compact form as private (a public compact ACDC is still rainbow-attackable, L501); bundled multi-claim credentials (refactorable into a graph of separately-disclosable chained ACDCs, "obviating the need for Selective Disclosure," L2739); centralized namespace registries (unnecessary — content-address plus content-addressable schema is the namespace, L2936); over-investing in cryptographic unlinkability ("diminishing returns" without contractually-protected disclosure, L2785); and privacy-as-end-goal (reframed to exploitation-protection, L1659).

#### Where the ground truth is softer than the prose {#softer-ground-truth}

Where the ground truth is softer than the prose, restated at keripy `b8f60166b` (2026-09-14), which moved several of these. ~~"The DI2I operator is unimplemented (`NotImplementedError`); m-ary edge-group operators and DAG acyclicity are read but not enforced; all re-confirmed as of `60ab9e08`."~~ Superseded 2026-09-15; [section 5.1](#verifying-ground-truth) holds the detail and the reasons. At the new pin: `DI2I` and `NOT` are still unimplemented but now fail closed by design with a `ValidationError` rather than crashing, which is a better failure and not an implementation; four of the six m-ary edge-group operators still have no code path, while `AND` and `OR` do, in a module nothing in `src/keri` calls; DAG acyclicity is confirmed still unchecked, `_walkGraph`'s `seen` set being a termination guard rather than a validity check; a missing edge operator is still silently inferred; two modules disagree about what to do with an operator they do not recognize; and disclosure and rule clauses remain commitments for application-layer or human enforcement rather than predicates the reference verifier evaluates. Moving the other way, the edge `s` constraint is now enforced and `E1E` is implemented ahead of the standardizing branch.

#### Re-checked 2026-10-03 {#rechecked-2026-10-03}

Re-checked 2026-10-03 at `80f77b73b`. Every item in the paragraph above still holds at the new pin, with two widenings and one addition ([section 5.1](#verifying-ground-truth), [section 4.1](#age-credential)). The module disagreement grew from one axis to three — unrecognized tokens, conflicting delegative operators in a list, and an absent operator — and the silent default now applies in one module while the other checks nothing. The operator family has two more proposed members, `I1I` and `DI1I` ([P], #1515, 2026-10-01), with no code. And the age credential, the aggregate section's showcase, cannot be presented selectively through keripy's IPEX grant, because a registry-backed aggregate must disclose every element; keripy also reads an aggregate's issuee from a position the schema leaves free. No keripy test presents an Age credential, so both are findings from reading code paths, not from running them.

#### Not re-examined: the verifyAnchor hypothesis {#verifyanchor-not-reexamined}

One item on the old list was not re-examined this pass and must not be read as re-confirmed. The recon hypothesis that the credential `Verifier` may not re-run `verifyAnchor` on presentation, potentially undermining the anchor-position (retrograde) defense (landmine L15, HIGH severity, medium confidence, [section 9](#registries) above), still stands at the older `60ab9e08d` pin; the 2026-09-15 keripy pass read `vdr/verifying.py` in full but recorded no finding on it, and this chapter under-claims rather than assuming it survived unchanged. Treat it as an open question at an older commit, not as a fact at the new one.

None of these are protocol defects — they are implementation-maturity gaps on the keripy 2.1-dev line (`__version__ = '2.1.0-dev1'` at `b8f60166b`, unchanged at `80f77b73b`; the corpus's older material was tagged against 2.0.0-dev6, and nothing here is a release). The honest posture is Hardman's own: ACDC's constraints today "are constraints of *youth*, not of *architecture*" (papers `wtbo.md` > 6. The Transition Problem: A Comparison of Trajectories). A reviewer who conflates the two — who reads an unimplemented operator as a design flaw, or an unenforced rule as a broken guarantee — is making the category error the whole corpus is built to prevent. The month's movement is evidence for that posture rather than against it: the gaps that closed closed because someone implemented them, not because the design changed. One item added on 2026-10-03 may be an exception, and it should be carried as one: the age-credential collision of [section 4.1](#age-credential) is two deliberate rules meeting — hide nothing that could conceal a presentation-registry declaration, and disclose one threshold without the others — and resolving it may take a design decision about where such a declaration may live, not just more code. Which kind of gap it is has not been settled by anyone read here.
