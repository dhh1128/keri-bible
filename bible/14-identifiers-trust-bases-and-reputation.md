# 14 — Identifiers, Trust Bases and Reputation {#identifiers}

## Thesis {#thesis}

KERI secures identifiers, not identities, and the order matters. An AID proves who controls it and nothing else; it says nothing about which person or organization stands behind it, and nothing about whether what that controller says is true. Sam Smith's identifier theory supplies the missing layer and gives it a structure. A trust basis is what an identifier's security rests on, and there are three kinds: autonomic (a self-certifying root of trust), algorithmic (a ledger) and administrative (a trusted entity). A trust domain is the set of statements verifiably issued under one AID, and inside it any human-meaningful name can be made secure by an anchored authorization, which turns it into a legitimized identifier (LID) written as the couplet `aid|lid`. Everything an AID cannot prove about its controller is reputation: which entity it belongs to, whether that entity's statements are reliable, whether it is the same entity as another AID. Reputation is measured behavior, it is contextual, it needs secure attribution before it means anything, and it is what makes an AID worth trusting, because a bare AID is a cheap pseudonym that anyone can mint and abandon. Identity assurance is the most widely used form of that reputation.

The practical consequence for a reviewer is a strict separation of questions. Who controls this AID is answered by its key event log, with no authority anywhere. Which entity this AID belongs to, and whether to rely on what it says, is answered by reputation, which does involve vouching, by assurers, by issuers in a credential chain, and by the relying party's own judgment. A critique that asks who vouches for an AID's control has imported a prior KERI removed; a design that assumes control settles identity has skipped a layer KERI's own author says is required.

## Status {#status}

Almost nothing in this chapter is normative, and it should be read that way. The identifier theory, the reputation doctrine, the trust-basis taxonomy, LIDs, the PAC trilemma and the cheap-pseudonymity argument are all [proposal]: Sam's whitepapers, slide decks and one GitHub discussion. None of these constructs appears in the KERI or ACDC specification at the pins in [Sources](#sources), and no keripy code computes or stores reputation. The specifications are cited only for the few places where they touch the theory: the KERI specification uses the phrase "trust basis" twice and applies the authenticity-versus-veracity split once ([section 2](#trust-bases), [section 5](#two-kinds-of-trust)), and the ACDC specification uses "trust domains" twice in passing. Searched at those pins and absent from both: "legitimi", "human-meaningful", "human meaningful", "trilemma", "pseudonym", "Zooko" and "identity assurance". The only "reputa" in the KERI specification is the different word "non-reputable" in a sentence about receipts. So where this chapter says that KERI holds something, it means that KERI's author argues it, unless a [spec] marker says otherwise.

Three provenance cautions. First, the slide decks are slides, extracted to text with diagrams flattened; their quotes are headline bullets, not argued paragraphs, and are cited by slide title. Second, some lineage papers are co-authored, and their sentences are not Sam's alone (the Sources entries name the authors). Third, conference captions appear here only for framing, never as evidence of a design, and two of them are from speakers other than Sam. The did:webs specification is a Trust Over IP specification in its own standards track, outside the KERI, ACDC and CESR lines this corpus tracks; following `raw/20` section 6 it is marked [proposal] here, which under-claims a document that is itself a specification. The [architect] marker is used for Sam's spoken words only.

What has changed recently, and might change again: discussion #1598 (2026-08-09) is the first written statement of the cheap-pseudonymity argument and of identity assurance as a form of reputation, which `raw/20` had found only in speech; and UIT v1.40 (2025-09-14) added KERI's choice within the PAC trilemma and stopped describing AIDs as a type of DID. A later revision of either could move this chapter.

## 1. Identifier first, identity second {#identifier-first}

Universal Identifier Theory (UIT) opens by choosing its subject: "We use the term identifier system instead of identity system" (UIT > 1.1 Unified Model of Identifiers, p.1 @b47f21597) [proposal]. The reason given is that the identifier's properties determine the identity system's, and the definition of identity is deliberately thin: "We formally define a (digital) identity to be a (digital) identifier plus attributes." (same) [proposal]. The attributes may themselves be identifiers, so an identity is in general a graph.

The ordering is a security claim. UIT says its model "takes a security-first approach" and rests on "the recognition that all other properties of an identifier are of little value" unless the identifier is securely controlled (UIT > 1.1 Unified Model of Identifiers, p.2 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Security means a mechanism by which an entity can prove control, and UIT's answer to which mechanism is self-certification (UIT > 1.2 Identifier Control and Trust Basis, p.2-3) [proposal], which is the derivation [chapter 01, section 2](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#autonomic-identity) describes from the specification.

This framing is why the rest of the chapter can exist. If an identity were the identifier, reputation would be a property of the key. Because an identity is an identifier plus attributes, the attributes, including which real entity the identifier belongs to, can be asserted, contested and accumulated separately, in a layer above key state. A reviewer should hold the two definitions apart. KERI's core proves control of an identifier ([chapter 01, section 3](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#root-of-trust)). The identity that hangs off it is attributes, and attributes are claims.

UIT v1.40 also moved KERI off any particular namespace. Where v1.32 said "AIDs may be used as a type of DID." (UIT v1.32 > 1.3 Self-Managing Self-Certifying Identifiers (AIDs), p.6 @416b7ac), v1.40 says "A given DID may be a type of AID but not all DIDs are AIDs." and "KERI itself is name space agnostic" (UIT > 1.3 Self-Managing Self-Certifying Identifiers (AIDs), p.6 @b47f21597) [proposal]. The identifier is primary; DID syntax is one way to write it.

## 2. Three trust bases {#trust-bases}

UIT classifies identifier systems by what their security finally rests on, which it calls a trust basis [proposal]. The 2025 SEDI deck states the term in one line, "A trust basis binds controllers, identifiers, and key-pairs.", and the dependent term in the next, "A trust domain is the ecosystem of interactions (functions) that rely on a trust basis." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Trust Basis of a Trust Domain @b47f21597) [proposal]. There are three.

- Autonomic. The root of trust is a self-certifying identifier and its own key event log. The deck's one-line characterization: "root-of-trust in verifiable cryptographic proofs of infrastructure with no shared governance" (same slide) [proposal].
- Algorithmic. The root of trust is a network running a Byzantine fault-tolerant, totally ordering consensus algorithm, a ledger: "root-of-trust in verifiable operational infrastructure with shared governance" (same slide) [proposal].
- Administrative. The root of trust is a trusted entity, such as DNS plus certificate authorities: "root-of-trust in non-verifiable operational infrastructure with opaque governance" (same slide) [proposal].

The paper's argument against the second is the sharpest one-line statement of KERI's objection to ledgers in the corpus. A ledger may be very secure, UIT concedes, but its identifiers are bound to that instance of infrastructure and are not portable: "Should the ledger go away, so does the root of trust." Its summary: "One may characterize a ledger as a centralized logical construct that is governed in a decentralized manner." (UIT > 1.2 Identifier Control and Trust Basis, p.4 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Against the third it is blunter: "Security may be dependent on something as nebulous as employee morale." (UIT > 1.2 Identifier Control and Trust Basis, p.5) [proposal]. [Chapter 07, section 4.1](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#global-consensus) and [chapter 07, section 2.5](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#administrative-bottom) carry the corresponding shibboleths; the taxonomy is what lets a reviewer name which basis a design has silently adopted.

The KERI specification uses the term without defining it. KERI's overlay "provides the trust basis for a universal AIS" (KERI spec > End-verifiable, L89 @fbdd4a615), and witness designation matters because "the identifier’s trust basis is not locked to any given witness or set of witnesses" (KERI spec > Advantages, L1840 @fbdd4a615). The second sentence is the portability argument against the algorithmic basis, stated for witnesses: a controller's choice of infrastructure does not become its root of trust. That is as far as the specification goes. The three-way classification is UIT's.

Two refinements matter in review. First, the deck says a concrete interaction is overlaid per identifier, not per protocol: each identifier in a message has its own controller and so its own concrete trust basis (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Overlay: Protocol Abstraction vs. Concrete Interaction) [proposal]. A system can therefore mix bases in one exchange, and the weakest basis among the parties a decision depends on is the one that bounds it. Second, the deck frames cross-domain trust as appraisal: "Maximal trust transitivity happens in an interaction when the trust bases of all parties are mutually appraisable" at comparable trustability and low appraisal cost (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Trust Domain Appraisability @b47f21597) [proposal]. This is the theory's account of why interoperating with a poorly appraisable basis costs something even when the protocols interoperate. It is stated as a question in the deck, not as a result.

## 3. The trust domain and the legitimized identifier {#trust-domain}

Once an AID's control is established, everything it signs can be verified to it. UIT names that set: "The trust domain of an AID is the set of statements that may be securely verified as being issued by the controller of that AID" (UIT > 1.3 Self-Managing Self-Certifying Identifiers (AIDs), p.6 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Authorizations inhabit the trust domain and inherit its security, and a verifier checks each one against the keys authoritative when it was issued, which is the anchoring rule of [chapter 03, section 11](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#as-of-anchor). UIT gives the rule a name, the persistent authorization model: "Any signed statement that is signed with the set of authoritative keys at the time of its signing is valid until revoked" (UIT > 4.1 Authorization Models, p.20) [proposal].

The trust domain is the construct that lets a human-meaningful name become secure. On its own such a name has nothing to secure it: "there is no inherent security property of a human-meaningful identifier. This makes them insecure by default." (UIT > 1.4 Legitimized Human Meaningful Identifiers (LIDs), p.6 @b47f21597) [proposal]. But the controller of an AID, and only that controller, can issue a verifiable authorization that legitimizes a name within its trust domain. UIT calls the result a legitimized human-meaningful identifier, LID, and writes the pair as the couplet `aid|lid`, where the bar is the legitimizing authorization (UIT > 1.4, p.6-7) [proposal]. The AID supplies the security and the LID the meaning.

The couplet is UIT's claimed resolution of Zooko's triangle, which says an identifier can be at most two of human-meaningful, secure and decentralized. Global scarcity disappears, because each AID may legitimize its own copy of any name: "Scarcity only occurs within the trust domain of each AID but is completely managed by the controller of that trust domain (AID)." (UIT > 1.4, p.7) [proposal]. A reviewer should notice the move UIT is making. It objects to ledger-based resolutions because there "not one identifier but a combination of identifiers is needed to solve the trilemma", which "hints that the identifier model therein employed is incomplete" (UIT > 1.1 Unified Model of Identifiers, p.2) [proposal]. The couplet is itself a combination of two identifiers. UIT's claim therefore amounts to saying that a combination is principled when the joining element is a verifiable authorization in a self-certifying trust domain, and unprincipled when it is a registration on someone else's ledger. That is Sam's argument. The corpus holds no outside assessment of it.

### 3.1 Primary, secondary and tertiary identifiers {#identifier-classes}

UIT sorts all identifiers into three classes [proposal]. AIDs are primary, self-certifying to a cryptographic root of trust. LIDs are secondary: "Secondary identifiers are LIDs and are not self-certifying but are secured under the aegis of an associated primary identifier's trust domain." Everything else is tertiary, and "tertiary identifiers are unsecured and may only be used internally or where security is unimportant" (UIT > 1.4 Legitimized Human Meaningful Identifiers (LIDs), p.7 @b47f21597). A secondary identifier never stands alone: it appears with its primary, or in a context where the primary is known.

The classification is older than KERI. The 2016 Identity System Essentials paper, by Smith and Khovratovich, already has the alias as a secondary identifier, a string that "is a human friendly globally unique string of text that may be associated with an identity in addition to its primary cryptonym", and states "It is a secondary identifier." (ISE > Aliases, p.4 @b47f21597) [proposal]. What UIT added is the security condition. In 2016 an alias's uniqueness could be kept by an external authority such as DNS; in UIT a secondary identifier is secure only when an AID's controller has authorized it.

### 3.2 LIDs in practice {#lids-in-practice}

UIT's worked examples are industry identifier schemes. "GLEIF's Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) may be viewed as a type of LID" (UIT > 1.7 Examples of LIDs, p.10 @b47f21597) [proposal], legitimized by a GLEIF root AID, and GS1's GTIN, SGTIN and GLN codes are treated the same way (UIT > 1.7, p.11). The paper is candid about where these come from: "Usually, the issuance of an LID uses an administrative trust basis." (UIT > 1.7, p.10) [proposal]. The couplet secures the binding of the name to the issuing AID. It does not make the issuer's vetting cryptographic, and a reviewer should not read a verifiable LEI as a claim that legal-entity verification has become autonomic. What changes is that the vetting's output is anchored, attributable and checkable without a callback, which is the vLEI arrangement described in [chapter 06, section 2](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#arbitration).

The cleanest working instance in the corpus is did:webs [proposal]. A did:webs identifier is a web host and path joined to an AID, and the specification requires the controller to legitimize that name in its own trust domain: on creation it "MUST authorize the full `did:webs` identifier as a designated alias ACDC anchored to the KERI AID" (did:webs spec > Create, L287 @2d84ef2b0). The host-and-path is the human-meaningful part; the anchored designated-aliases ACDC is the bar. The specification's example credential also states the limit of what the bar asserts, in its rule text: "It does not assert that the controller of this AID has control over the infrastructure or anything else related to the namespace" (did:webs spec > Full Example, L1565 @2d84ef2b0). That is the LID's boundary stated exactly. The authorization proves the AID's controller designated the name. It proves nothing about who runs the web server, which the specification treats as an untrusted host of content. [Chapter 01, section 7](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#did-current-state) faults DID methods that resolve only current key state; did:webs is the KERI-side answer to that critique, and chapter 06 is the place for its mechanics.

### 3.3 A LID is not an alias {#lid-not-alias}

[Chapter 07, section 9.4](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#aliases) says an alias is a memory aid, not evidence, and that only AIDs may drive system logic. In UIT's terms that is the rule for tertiary identifiers, and it is right. A LID is a different object: a name made verifiable by an anchored authorization from a known AID. Two errors are possible and a reviewer should catch both. One reads the alias rule as forbidding LIDs, and so rejects a did:webs host name or a verifiable LEI as mere petnames. The other treats a bare alias as if it were a LID, and so keys logic on a name nobody legitimized. The test is whether a verifiable authorization from an identified AID binds the name. If it does, the name is a secondary identifier, and logic may key on the couplet. If it does not, the name is tertiary, whatever it looks like.

Even a LID inherits only the security of its binding, not the reputation of the name. A LID legitimized by an AID nobody recognizes is a secure name in a domain nobody trusts. Which AID's legitimizations to accept is the question the next section takes up.

## 4. Establishing a root AID by well-known reputational association {#well-known-association}

Every chain of trust in UIT starts at a root AID, and a root AID has a problem a CA certificate does not. It is unique, but it is a random string, so nothing about it says whose it is. UIT confronts this directly: "The process that establishes an AID as the root identifier with an organization may be called a well-known reputational association." Its security condition is social: "Once this connection between root AID and organization becomes common knowledge, then it is secure." (UIT > 1.6 Root AID Establishment via Well Known Reputational Association, p.9 @b47f21597) [proposal].

The mechanism is redundancy across channels the entity already controls or that already know it, such as RFC 8615 `/.well-known/` paths on its domains or registrations with vetting bodies (UIT > 1.6, p.9-10). "The strength of the well-known establishment is multiplied by the number of such associations, i.e., it provides a reputational source of trust." (UIT > 1.6, p.9) [proposal]. And: "Consistent common knowledge is the root of reputational trust" (UIT > 1.6, p.10) [proposal]. After the root is established, every further link can be a signed statement under the root's keys, so the reputational step happens once, at the root, and everything below it is cryptographic.

This is the conceptual ancestor of the KERI specification's OOBI-with-MFA pattern, in which a controller publishes its AID at several independently controlled `/.well-known/oobi/{aid}` locations and a recipient bootstraps only on threshold agreement [spec] ([chapter 06, section 2](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#oobi-mfa-arbitration)). The specification draws a boundary UIT does not draw as clearly: the pattern "does not change the trust model of an OOBI", and it "governs only whether the recipient is willing to bootstrap from the OOBIs at all" (KERI spec > OOBI with MFA, L2838 @fbdd4a615) [spec].

### The tension with the OOBI shibboleth {#well-known-tension}

Read side by side, UIT and [chapter 07, section 2.6](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#multiple-well-knowns) appear to disagree. UIT says the strength of an association is multiplied by the number of well-knowns. Chapter 07 says that publishing the same AID at several well-knowns does not strengthen the AID binding, and names as the tell "any sentence in which the count of well-knowns appears as an input to an authenticity judgment". Both are right, about different bindings, and the resolution is the point of this chapter.

There are two bindings in play. One is between an AID and its keys and endpoints: which keys control this AID, and where its log is served. That binding is cryptographic, OOBI resolution verifies it in the usual way, and no number of well-knowns improves it, which is what the specification and chapter 07 say. The other is between an AID and an entity: whether this AID is the one GLEIF, or Acme, or the State of Utah, actually uses. Cryptography cannot establish that binding at all, because an entity is not a key. It is a reputational fact, and redundancy across independent channels is exactly how reputational facts are made credible. UIT is answering the second question. So the doctrine survives intact and gains a qualifier: the count of well-knowns is never an input to an authenticity judgment about an AID's key state, and it is a legitimate input to a reputational judgment about which entity an AID belongs to. A sentence that uses it for the first is the chapter 07 tell. A sentence that refuses it for the second is asking cryptography to answer a question that is not cryptographic.

This chapter's synthesis, not a source's: a reviewer who meets a design that bootstraps a root AID should ask which binding each step is establishing, and should expect the reputational step to be explicit, redundant and done once.

## 5. Two kinds of trust, and the order between them {#two-kinds-of-trust}

UIT divides trust into two types [proposal]. The first is trust in the cryptographic root of trust: we can trust who said something, and prove whether they were consistent. "But we may not trust in the veracity of what was said. For this, we need the second type of trust." (UIT > 1.5 Reputational Trust, p.8 @b47f21597). The second is reputational trust, trust that an entity will make accurate statements, and UIT defines reputation narrowly: "Reputation is a measure of accumulated consistent behavior." (same). This is the authenticity-versus-veracity split the bible already rests on through Hardman's paper ([chapter 06, section 1](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#authenticity-veracity); [chapter 07, section 9.2](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#authenticity-veracity)), in an earlier written form: the UIT text dates from 2020.

UIT adds something the bible's version does not state: an order. "First, authenticity (AID), and then veracity (LID)." (UIT > 1.5, p.8) [proposal]. The argument is that the value of information depends on its veracity, veracity depends on the issuer's reputation, and reputation can only be associated with the information if the issuer's authenticity is established first. Reputation is built from consistent behavior, which needs provably consistent attribution, so "Provable trust in the AID mechanism enables provable trust in the entity behind the LID mechanism." (UIT > 1.5, p.9) [proposal]. Hardman calls the two axes orthogonal; UIT makes the second depend on the first. These are compatible. The judgments are independent, since a perfectly authentic statement may be false, while the usefulness of a veracity judgment depends on authenticity, since a reputation attached to an unattributable statement attaches to nothing. The bible should cite Hardman for the analysis and UIT for the dependency.

The KERI specification states the same split once, about certificate authorities rather than about KERI: a CA certificate "merely provides evidence of the authenticity of the assignment of the mapping but not evidence of the veracity of the mapping" (KERI spec > Overcoming existing security overlay flaws, L81 @fbdd4a615) [spec]. The did:webs specification draws it as a definition: "the term decentralized trust includes verifiability, confidentiality, and privacy, but excludes veracity of the content." (did:webs spec > Introduction, L81-82 @2d84ef2b0; the source italicizes the term) [proposal], and assigns veracity to "(personal) evaluation of available reputational data and verifiable credentials (VCs)" (same, L83-84). That last clause is the whole of this chapter in a parenthesis: what decentralized trust cannot reach, reputation must.

UIT's pairing of veracity with the LID is looser than its pairing of authenticity with the AID. A LID is a name, and the veracity at stake is that of the authorizer's act of legitimizing it, which UIT says outright: "we rely on the reputational integrity of the controller to trust that a given LID has been authorized correctly" (UIT > 1.5, p.9) [proposal]. A reviewer should read "veracity (LID)" as shorthand for the reliability of the legitimizing authority, not as a property of the string.

## 6. What reputation is {#reputation-defined}

The definition has been stable for a decade. Sam's reputation decks from 2017 to 2022 carry the same usage line: reputation is "A considered evaluation (measure) of past behavior used to predict future behavior." with the qualification "Confidence improves with contextual similarity." (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slide Reputation @b47f21597; the 2017 deck has the same lines, misspelling the last word) [proposal]. The 2025 SEDI deck compresses it: "Reputation is a behavior-based contextual predictor that enables an interaction." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Reputable Authenticatable Pseudonymity (RAP) @b47f21597) [proposal]. Three properties are fixed across the versions: reputation is about behavior, it predicts, and it is bounded by context. The same decks rank predictors along those axes: "Contextual predictors are more powerful", "Behavior based predictors are more credible", and "Transitive predictors are more portable" (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slide What is reputation AI?) [proposal].

### 6.1 Reputes, not ratings {#reputes-not-ratings}

The design rule from the Open Reputation period is that reputation is computed from recorded behavior, not from scores people give each other. "Computational generation of a reputation is to aggregate relevant instances of behavior." and "Instances of behavior = reputational events or reputes for short" (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slide Computational reputation @b47f21597) [proposal]. The reason is that a repute can be reinterpreted and a rating cannot: "Reputation from reputed behavior allows for re-scoping, re-weighting, re-combination, and re-evaluation of collected reputes (not so for ratings)" (same slide) [proposal]. A rating has already collapsed the evidence into one judgment in one context; a record of behavior can be re-read for a new context. This is the reputation-side version of the bible's general preference for evidence over verdicts, the same preference that makes a watcher report duplicity rather than declare an identifier distrusted ([chapter 07, section 8.2](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#watcher-enforcement)).

### 6.2 Reputation is not a set of credentials {#not-credentials}

The commonest outsider prior is that a credential is a reputation. Sam's 2017 deck answers it in one line: "Reputation is not a set of verifiable claims but a set of verifiable claims can contribute to a reputation" (ReputationIIW2017 deck > slide Issues @b47f21597; the extracted text renders "fi" as a ligature) [proposal]. A credential is an issuer's attributable assertion. A reputation is a relying party's evaluation, which may count that assertion as one input among others, weighted by the issuer's own reputation and by how close the credential's context is to the one at hand. The distinction keeps reputation where [section 5](#two-kinds-of-trust) puts it, on the relying party's side of the transaction, and keeps a credential from being read as a verdict.

### 6.3 Why reputation is the motive {#reputation-motive}

Reputation is not a late addition to KERI's program. It is the reason the program exists, on its author's account [proposal]. A 2018 note puts the dependency in one line: "decentralized identity is essential to decentralized reputation which is essential to decentralized 2 sided networks." (IdentityAndReputation.md, paragraph 2 @b47f21597). The decks give the economics: "Curation is applied reputation" and "Portable Reputation is a potential meta-platform" (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slides Curation and Meta-platform) [proposal]. The founding sentence that keri.one's About page carries has its earliest written form in a 2019 paper by Shea, Smith and Stöcker: "a reputation is meaningless without an underlying identity and an identity is valueless without a credible reputation." (CooperationBeatsAggregation.md > Identity and reputation systems as trust brokerages, L132 @b47f21597) [proposal]. The same section says a decentralized identity and reputation system "enables trust to be transitive between contexts to some degree" (same, L136; the source bolds the middle phrase).

This motive explains a design choice that otherwise looks like a privacy concession. If identity existed to be hidden, a long-lived, correlatable identifier would be a defect. If identity exists so that behavior can be attributed and accumulate into reputation, then persistence across key rotation is the point, since an identifier that dies with its first key cannot accumulate anything. [Chapter 01, section 2](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#autonomic-identity) gives the mechanism; this section gives the reason the mechanism was wanted. The deck's own words for it: within a context, "the secure attributability of an AID, together with its perfect correlatability, enables secure reputational trust (good or bad) in that AID" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Relationship Properties @b47f21597) [proposal]. Correlatability inside a context is the feature; correlatability across contexts is the hazard, and that is where the PAC trade of [section 9](#pac-trilemma) begins.

## 7. Reputation by reference and by behavior {#reference-and-behavior}

Two routes to reputation run through the material, and the 2026 talks gave them names. Phil Windley, at the KERI conference, called the first reputation by reference, "and the reason I'm going to talk about it first is because it's essentially how we bootstrap things" (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Windley, Digital Identity Needs a Legal Foundation, 23:02), illustrated by a diploma chained through the university to its accreditors and to the state. The second is reputation by behavior, accumulated from direct interaction. The captions are framing only. Sam's own spoken list of UIT's contributions includes "reputation by reference" (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 30:30) [architect], although the UIT text does not use the phrase; it describes the mechanism, a chain of authorizations from a well-known root, without the name.

The pair lines up with Sam's older vocabulary. Reputation by behavior is the direct repute of [section 6.1](#reputes-not-ratings). Reputation by reference is indirect or intermediated reputing, in which a "Reputery = Indirect Source (Implied Reputer) or Intermediate Source" stands between the behavior and the relying party (ReputationIIW2017 deck > slide Indirect and intermediated reputing @b47f21597) [proposal]. The difference is whom the relying party trusts. An exchange in the Windley Q&A put it plainly, speaker unattributed in the captions: asked whether reputation by reference is not also an accumulation of behaviors, the answer was "It is!, but it's at a distance.", and later "In reputation you explicitly trust the reputer." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Windley, Digital Identity Needs a Legal Foundation, 27:09 and 28:09). By reference, the relying party trusts the reputer's observation; by behavior, it trusts only its own.

### 7.1 ACDC chains as the carrier {#acdc-carrier}

The 2025 SEDI deck names ACDC chaining as the carrier for reputation by reference. Among the features it says require issuer linkability: "Verifiable reputation via ACDC chained evidence dossiers" and "Multiple roots-of-trust for reputation" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Unique features of KERI/ACDC that require Issuer linkability @b47f21597) [proposal]. A chained ACDC is reputation by reference made verifiable: each edge is an issuer's attributable statement, and a relying party walks the chain back to roots whose reputations it already holds ([chapter 05, section 5](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#edges)). "Multiple roots" is the important qualifier. A diploma may chain to several accreditors and to a business registry at once, and the relying party weighs each root by its own reputation, which is UIT's well-known association of [section 4](#well-known-association) repeated at every root.

What is not established should be said as plainly. No specification and no implementation defines a reputation computation over ACDC chains, a schema for reputes, or a way to carry reputation by behavior as credentials. Windley's suggestion that receipts could become credentials feeding a market of scoring algorithms is a talk (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Windley, Digital Identity Needs a Legal Foundation). And Sam's 2022 deck titled Automated Reasoning and ACDCs, subtitled Secure Decentralized Reputation Algorithms, turns out on reading to contain the 2016-2018 reputation material and his elastic-constraint reasoning slides, with no slide showing an ACDC used as reputation input (AR_ACDC_Rep deck, read in full, `raw/20` section 2.7). It is evidence that Sam linked ACDCs to reputation in 2022, not evidence of a design, and it should not be cited for one.

### 7.2 Transitivity across a delegation {#delegation-transitivity}

The one place the reputation theory reaches the protocol is the `DID` configuration trait, and the link was made in writing only in August 2026. Sam's discussion #1598 says the trait exists "to formally signal that the controller of the delegated AID is identical to the controller of the delegating AID" (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598, body of 2026-08-09, R-016, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1598) [proposal], so that reputation moves in both directions: "any reputation earned by the delegator is also imbued in the delegatee", and "Any consequence of misbehavior, incurred by the delegatee is now reflected back on the delegator" (same). It is needed "because not all delegated AIDs have the same legal controlling entity as their delegator AID." (same). And the protocol takes no position on what follows: "What a second party does with this declaration of controller equivalence between two AIDs (delegator and delegate) is up to the second party." (same).

[Chapter 03, section 8](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#delegation-trait-rulings) carries these rulings with the trait's specification history, and [chapter 07, section 8.4](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#did-trait) the shibboleth that reads `DID` as transitive endorsement. What this chapter adds is the reason. `DID` is a statement about the second binding of [section 4](#well-known-association), AID to entity, made by the one party able to make it with authority, the controller, and countersigned by the delegator's anchoring seal. It transfers reputation because reputation attaches to entities, and the trait asserts that two AIDs are one entity. It does not transfer authority, which is why it does not propagate to further delegations and why the specification states no validator consequence ([chapter 03, section 8](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#did-effect-unspecified)). Read this way, the gap chapter 03 records is not an omission; reputation is the relying party's to compute, so the protocol carries the assertion and stops.

The same discussion says why an entity would split itself across delegated AIDs in the first place. "One way to avoid common mode sources of systemic failure is to use multivalent key management infrastructure." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598) [proposal], and delegated AIDs are how one controller runs infrastructure with different failure modes under one reputation. UIT section 5 develops the same multivalent architecture for an organization's root AID (UIT > 5 Multi-valent Key Management Infrastructure for an Entity, p.23 @b47f21597) [proposal].

## 8. Cheap pseudonymity and identity assurance {#cheap-pseudonymity}

The argument that ties the chapter together is in discussion #1598, whose title names it: Cheap Pseudonymity, Identity Assurance as Reputation, Multivalent DKMI, and the DID config trait [proposal]. An AID is self-certifying, which is its strength, and anyone can make one, which is its weakness. Sam's words: AIDs "are just cheap cryptographic pseudonyms (cryptonyms) for the controlling entity that holds the private keys the control the AID." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598, body of 2026-08-09, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1598; "the control" is the source's typing for "that control"). So "an AID is not very useful for real world interactions between controllers unless a given AID is no longer cheap." (same). A controller that misbehaves under one AID can abandon it and mint another. He said the same at the conference: identity assurance "solves one problem. It's called the cheap pseudonymity problem", because "nobody can trust an AID that anybody can create." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 36:15 and 36:37) [architect].

The remedy is cost. "And for reputation to be useful and meaningful it must be costly." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598) [proposal]. A cryptonym with costly reputation attached is no longer cheap, because abandoning it abandons the reputation, and that is what makes it a basis for trust in its controller.

This is the cleanest statement in the corpus of why KERI's control guarantee is necessary and not sufficient. Self-certification removes every authority from the question of control ([chapter 01, section 3](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#root-of-trust)). It cannot remove the cost of the question of trust, and a design that treats a freshly incepted AID as trustworthy because its log verifies has confused the two. This chapter's synthesis: the reflex that nobody vouches for the identifier is correct for control and wrong for trust.

### 8.1 Identity assurance as costly reputation {#assurance-as-reputation}

"One type of costly reputation is identity assurance." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598) [proposal]. The controller discloses its legal identity to an identity prover, and "The prover issues a perpetually verifiable assertion that binds the AID to that real identity. That AID now is not cheap." (same). Behavior attributable to the AID is now attributable to a person or organization who bears consequences. Sam ranks it first among the forms of reputation because "identity assurance is the form of reputation most widely used in our society" (same).

Persistence is what makes the cost worth paying. The SEDI deck lists "Strongest Identity assurance amortized over the lifespan of unbounded term (persistence) AIDs" among KERI's distinguishing features (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Unique features of KERI/ACDC that require Issuer linkability @b47f21597) [proposal]. Proofing is expensive, in-person at the highest levels; an identifier that survives key rotation needs it once, where a key-bound identifier would need it again at every rotation or device change. For SEDI, Sam's proposed architecture makes the identity-assurance step issue a management credential used only to request bulk-issued, per-disclosee credentials ([chapter 06, section 3](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#sedi-architecture)), which keeps the assured binding out of everyday presentations. The statute's proofing requirements and the point-in-time character of assurance are chapter 06's to state.

### 8.2 Assurance as accumulated weak bindings {#weak-bindings}

The SEDI deck's diagram of identity assurance shows several assurers, each asserting a weak binding between a natural or legal person and an AID's controller, and its caption line is "Relatively weak binding reinforced by multiple bindings = reputation" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Identity Assurance and Reputation @b47f21597) [proposal]. It is UIT's well-known reputational association ([section 4](#well-known-association)) applied to people instead of organizations. No single proofing act is strong, and independence across assurers is what makes the binding credible.

That framing has a consequence a reviewer should hold onto. The binding of an AID to a person is never cryptographic, however strong the AID. It is a sum of assertions by assurers, each with its own reputation, so its strength is bounded by theirs and by their independence. An assurance credential is authentic in the [section 5](#two-kinds-of-trust) sense, attributable to its assurer, and its veracity is the assurer's reputation. The same deck's SPAC material adds that an out-of-band authentication step at relationship setup "also provides protection from cheap pseudonymity" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide OOB Setup) [proposal], and that a relationship "sufficiently authenticated and is therefore reputable (not cheap) can be used to issue the equivalent of a letter of introduction" to a new one (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Relationship Formation Sub-Protocol) [proposal]: reputation by reference at the scale of one relationship. [Chapter 12, section 5](12-confidentiality-and-secure-messaging.md#contexts) owns that protocol.

### 8.3 Reputable pseudonymity {#rap}

Cost does not have to mean a legal name. The SEDI deck names a target state between the cheap pseudonym and the fully identified person: "RAP = Trust based on contextual behavior that is securely cryptonymously attributable but without linkage to an actual person or legal entity." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Reputable Authenticatable Pseudonymity (RAP) @b47f21597) [proposal]. A pseudonym earns its cost by behavior in a context rather than by proofing. The same slide states the limit at once: "there is no practical pseudonymity without strong counterincentives for 2nd parties to share contextual behavior data." (same). Breach corpora and ambient correlation tooling mean a pseudonym survives only if those who see its behavior are deterred from linking it, which puts the burden on the contractual layer of [chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#layered-privacy) rather than on cryptography.

In the 2026 talks RAP grows into RAP-C3, which Sam offers as his one-line summary of KERI (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 03:20) [architect]. The letter that expands to "authenticatable" in the deck is read as "autonomic" in the talk summaries (`raw/20` section 2.5); the corpus should not normalize one to the other.

## 9. The PAC trilemma {#pac-trilemma}

UIT's last theoretical section states a trilemma for identifier system security overlays: privacy, authenticity and confidentiality, at most two at the highest degree. "One must make a trade. This is a trilemma for identifier system security overlays." (UIT > 1.8 Identifier System Security Overlay Properties, p.12 @b47f21597) [proposal]. The reason given is that no single cryptographic operation provides all three, so properties are layered, and layering exposes weaknesses at the seams. Non-repudiable signatures give the strongest authenticity and are correlation points; Diffie-Hellman gives confidentiality without non-repudiation (same).

KERI chooses. v1.40 says the best choice is complete authenticity and confidentiality, "because this best protects all participants from impersonation fraud." (UIT > 1.8, p.12) [proposal]. The 2020 text, v1.32, said only that KERI provides the highest authenticity, called it "a trilemma for identifier systems", and implied that both privacy and confidentiality were therefore incomplete (UIT v1.32 > 1.8 Identifier System Security Overlay Properties, p.11 @416b7ac). The choice of confidentiality as the second property is new in 2025, and v1.40 names the diagram Smith's Triangle. The 2025 SEDI deck opens with it as a theorem: "An interaction between two parties may be two of the three, private, authentic, and confidential at the highest degree" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide PAC Theorem — Trilemma @b47f21597) [proposal]. A theorem in name only: the corpus holds no proof of it and no outside assessment, and it is a design heuristic stated by its author, not a surveyed result.

The trilemma is the theoretical reason behind three things the bible already says. KERI-core "does not promise unlinkability, and cannot" ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#layered-privacy)): its bottom layer is non-repudiable signatures, which correlate. The privacy KERI does offer is layered above, by graduated disclosure, contractual protection and decorrelation ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#decorrelation)). And the stated goal is "not unlinkability, but rather unexploitability through the strongest security." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide KERI's Paradigm Shift: Security First) [proposal], which is the reason the reputation of [section 6](#reputation-defined) is compatible with privacy at all: reputation needs correlation within a context, and the harm to guard against is exploitation across contexts, not attribution within one. [Chapter 12, section 1](12-confidentiality-and-secure-messaging.md#trade-space) owns the trade space and the SPAC protocol that works inside it; this section states only the theorem and KERI's choice.

## 10. Load-bearing assumptions and conceded weaknesses {#conceded-weaknesses}

### 10.1 Established {#established}

Within the limits of [Status](#status), the following are stable across a decade of Sam's writing and consistent with the specifications where those speak at all. KERI secures control of identifiers; an identity is an identifier plus attributes, and the attributes are claims ([section 1](#identifier-first)). Authenticity and veracity are different judgments, and the KERI specification applies that distinction to certificate authorities in normative text ([section 5](#two-kinds-of-trust)). Reputation is a relying party's contextual evaluation of attributable behavior, not a credential and not a rating ([section 6](#reputation-defined)). A human-meaningful name is secure only as a secondary identifier legitimized in an AID's trust domain ([section 3](#trust-domain)), and did:webs implements exactly that.

### 10.2 Contested, unbuilt or unverified {#contested}

- No reputation mechanism is specified or implemented. The doctrine says what reputation is and where it belongs; nothing in the specifications or keripy computes, stores or exchanges it. A design that relies on a reputation layer is relying on something that exists only as theory and as each relying party's own logic.
- Identity assurance is not cryptographic. It is a sum of assertions whose strength is bounded by the assurers' reputations and independence ([section 8.2](#weak-bindings)). A guarantee stated about an assured AID inherits the assurers' assumptions in addition to KERI's, and a statement that omits them overstates it, in the sense of [chapter 02, section 6](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#assumptions).
- Root-AID establishment rests on common knowledge, which is not a protocol property. Before an AID becomes well known, an attacker who publishes a different AID first, or more widely, can win the reputational race. UIT says the association is secure "once" it is common knowledge ([section 4](#well-known-association)); it does not say how a relying party knows that point has been reached, and nothing in the specifications does either.
- The `aid|lid` resolution of Zooko's triangle is Sam's argument with no outside assessment in the corpus ([section 3](#trust-domain)). LIDs issued by standards bodies are usually issued on an administrative trust basis, and the couplet secures their binding, not their vetting ([section 3.2](#lids-in-practice)).
- The PAC trilemma is called a theorem and is stated without proof ([section 9](#pac-trilemma)). KERI's choice within it is new in UIT v1.40, and the confidentiality half is carried by protocols outside the KERI specification.
- Reputable pseudonymity is conditional on deterrence the protocol cannot supply ([section 8.3](#rap)). Its own definition concedes the condition.
- The `DID` trait carries an entity-equivalence assertion with no specified validator consequence, by design ([section 7.2](#delegation-transitivity)). No governance framework read for this corpus says what it does with the assertion, so the transitivity of reputation across a delegation is, today, an intent with no consumer.
- Sources this chapter could not read. The end of Sam's Tradespace talk is missing from the captions, which stop at 37:02 as he begins a GLEIF analogy for identity assurance; his full argument for how SEDI's assurance answers cheap pseudonymity is therefore read only from discussion #1598 and the decks. The 2015 Open Reputation papers, the source the reputation decks cite on almost every slide, were read for their title pages only (`raw/20` section 7.2). UIT v1.40 deletes, without explanation, a v1.32 sentence about a delegator replacing a delegate's identifier prefix (`raw/20` section 1.8); this chapter does not rely on either text.

### 10.3 Where KERI corrects the outsider's prior {#outsider-priors}

Three priors meet this material often enough to name. Asking who vouches for an AID is the right question about the entity behind an AID and the wrong one about its control; KERI answers the second with a log and leaves the first to reputation, deliberately ([section 8](#cheap-pseudonymity)). Treating a credential as a reputation collapses an issuer's assertion into a relying party's evaluation ([section 6.2](#not-credentials)). And equating privacy with unlinkability measures KERI against a property its author argues no system can have together with strong authenticity, and against a harm, linkage, that the KERI side of the argument replaces with exploitation ([section 9](#pac-trilemma)). A reviewer may contest that argument. A reviewer may not measure KERI against a goal it explicitly declines.
