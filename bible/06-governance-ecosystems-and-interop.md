# Governance, Ecosystems & Interop

**Thesis.** KERI draws a hard line between two kinds of trust decision, and everything in this chapter follows from where that line is drawn. On one side is *cryptographic verification* — signatures, SAIDs, hash-chain consistency, anchor-position validity — which is universal, mechanical, observer-independent, and answerable by anyone, anywhere, at any time, with no authority in the loop. On the other side is *ecosystem-defined behavior* — what a credential means, which edge operator makes a chain valid, whether an issuer is fit to issue, what a recipient may do with disclosed data — which is inescapably semantic, contextual, and human- or policy-governed. An Ecosystem Governance Framework (EGF) lives entirely on the second side; it never adjudicates the first. This is the structural fact that lets KERI claim decentralization honestly: because Layer-1 verification depends on no governance body, no EGF, no registry, and no browser-vendor root program, the only thing an ecosystem's governance can do is publish rules that opt-in participants cryptographically bind themselves to — it cannot become a new root of trust or a new choke point. The corollaries occupy the rest of the chapter: who (if anyone) arbitrates ecosystem behavior; how SEDI applies the "endorse-not-issue" inversion to civil identity; why correlation-resistance and a duty of data loyalty are *layered* properties that KERI-core does not and cannot promise (they live in the ACDC/disclosure/governance strata); how semantics cross trust domains without a global namespace authority; what versioning and migration actually cost; how four independent implementations stay byte-compatible; and how KERI scores against SD-JWT/OIDC4VC on a decentralization scorecard — with the honest caveats about where KERI's advantage is architecture and where it is merely youth.

Adversarial reviewers should carry one framing throughout: a critique that faults KERI for "not preventing correlation" or "still needing governance" is usually attacking the wrong layer. KERI-core promises key-state continuity and duplicity-evidence, not unlinkability and not semantic agreement. The interesting questions are whether the *layering* is clean, whether the governance seam is defensible, and whether the migration story survives contact with four codebases.

## 1. What an EGF is, and the two-layer verification split

The load-bearing distinction is stated most cleanly in the Dossier specification's verification doctrine: verification is a **two-layer process**. Layer 1 is cryptographic validation — "confirming signatures, SAIDs, and KEL consistency" — which is "universal, defined by this spec." Layer 2 is semantic validation, which is "necessarily application-specific and requires context-dependent business logic" (Dossier spec §"Layered verification doctrine"). The spec's own illustration is exact: a generic verifier "can verify that an edge labeled 'lunarPropertyDeed' is cryptographically linked, but it cannot know what that means or how to process it." This separation "is what makes the dossier a universal building block for evidence aggregation." An EGF is the artifact that supplies Layer-2 rules for a given ecosystem: what schemas are blessed, what an issuer must do to be trusted, what validity means for a particular chain of authority, what obligations attach to disclosed data.

That the Layer-2 rules are genuinely *outside* the cryptographic core is visible throughout the ACDC spec, which repeatedly defers behavior to the EGF rather than defining it. The m-ary edge operators (`AND`, `OR`, `NAND`, `NOR`, `AVG`, `WAVG`) define provenance-chain validity, but "validity logic is EGF-dependent" (ACDC spec §Edge Section, L1112-1116). Schema availability constraints "may [be] impose[d]" by the EGF (§Schema Availability, L242). Extensibility is "permission-less... no shared governance... completely decentralized and zero-trust," and a registry of ACDC types collapses to "merely Schema discovery or schema blessing for a given context or ecosystem" (§Extensibility, L2934-2936). The recurring pattern is that the cryptographic machinery is fixed and universal while *meaning* and *fitness* are pushed out to a per-ecosystem governance layer that the core neither knows nor needs.

**The authenticity/veracity axis is the theoretical spine of this split.** Hardman's framing makes it precise: authenticity is "the truthfulness of an imputed origin"; veracity is "the truthfulness of claimed facts in content"; the two "must be analyzed primarily as orthogonal" (`authenticity-vs-veracity.md §Formal definitions`). The key doctrine is mechanizability: "Judgments about authenticity can — if managed very carefully — be reduced to an objective mathematical computation, whereas judgments about veracity inherently require subjective assessments of reputation" (`authenticity-vs-veracity.md §Independence`). Layer 1 verifies authenticity; Layer 2 (governance, reputation, human judgment) adjudicates veracity and fitness. The applied corpus states the same boundary at the level of a single credential: an ACDC validation "can prove that an issuer remains committed to a citation — but only the interaction context... plus governance, plus possible validation external to the world of ACDCs" establishes what proposition is actually true (`citation/index.md`:39, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`). This is a clean statement of what cryptographic verification does and does not prove, and it is why an EGF is never optional in a real deployment even though it is invisible to the crypto.

**The governance hook is cryptographically bound, not merely referenced.** In the applied schemas the EGF is reachable by SAID: GCD (Generalized Cooperative Delegation) credentials carry a `gfw` field described as "A SAID that identifies a governance framework that adds richer semantics to the role field and any custom constraints present in this credential," and when it is defined "a verifier MUST NOT impute a delegator's approval of delegated authority to a delegate without understanding and enforcing the rules" (`gcd/gcd.schema.json` `a.gfw.description`, :79, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`, GCD 3.1.0). *Corrected 2026-10-03:* this sentence previously quoted the field as "is a SAID naming a supplemental rules block," which is not in the schema at either fork's pin; the meaning survives in the schema's next sentence, that the SAID "identifies a JSON rules block that supplements the generic rules in the rules block." Crucially, "The act of issuing or receiving a GCD credential constitutes binding acceptance of the rules" (`gcd/index.md` §Governance Framework, :116 at bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`; :54 at public-schema `95a07e544`). ~~a formulation that recurs verbatim across org-vet, citation, and SEDI~~ *Superseded 2026-10-03:* the sentence is no longer uniform across the corpus. In bakobo it survives verbatim in `gcd/index.md` and in the archived `org-vet-1.0.0/` and `citation-1.0.0/` pages; current `org-vet` 2.0.0 replaces it with four named Ricardian clauses (section 2), and SEDI says it in its own words — "Issuing or accepting a SEDI credential is binding acceptance of its referenced revision" (`sedi-id/index.md`:159). In public-schema `org-vet/` is deleted, and the newer telco pages adopt the idea in fresh wording: "The act of issuing or accepting this credential constitutes binding acceptance of those rules" (`brand-owner/index.md`:79). The doctrine is unchanged; what changed is that it is now restated per credential family rather than copied, which is the more honest shape for it. The Ricardian-contract rule section (`r`) is the general mechanism: rules are "both human and machine-readable and referenceable by a cryptographic digest," supporting the bow-tie model of Ricardian Contracts (ACDC spec §Rule Section, L1239). So an EGF's terms are not a URL a verifier optionally consults; they are a SAID-committed contract whose acceptance is entailed by participation. This is how governance binds without becoming a phone-home dependency or an external authority.

## 2. Who arbitrates ecosystem-defined behavior

The sharpest way to see KERI's governance position is by contrast with the arbiter it rejects. In the X.509/CT world, "the ultimate arbiter of CA trustworthiness is a handful of browser vendors" (`wtbo.md §1`); the root programs of Google, Mozilla, and Apple have "immediate, global effect... no formal appeals process. No government, no standards body, and no external adjudicator sits above them" (`wtbo.md` Note [b]). The load-bearing distinction is between enforcement and observation: "A browser vendor that distrusts a CA is making an enforcement decision — exercising judgment with immediate global consequences... A KERI watcher that detects duplicity is making an observation — surfacing cryptographic evidence that the relying party confirms independently" (`wtbo.md §4`). A watcher "cannot unilaterally 'distrust' an identifier the way a browser root program can distrust a CA. The worst a dishonest watcher can do is stay silent about duplicity it has seen" (`wtbo.md §4`). The architectural claim is that KERI has *no* Layer-1 arbiter at all — verification "bottoms out in mathematics," not in administrators (`wtbo.md §3`).

So who arbitrates Layer-2? The answer is deliberately plural and opt-in. The Dossier model "operates on a decentralized root of trust. A verifier does not rely on a single authority but makes explicit trust decisions about a plurality of evidence issuers" (Dossier spec §"Verifier Trust"). In a compositional dossier "trust is derived from the leaf nodes (the authorities), not merely from the dossier issuer" — the LEI issuer for legal identity, the carrier/regulator for number authority, the trademark steward for brand (Dossier spec §"Use Cases", VVP). There is no single body that certifies fitness across the ecosystem; each verifier composes trust from the specific authorities relevant to the specific claim, and the EGF for that vertical publishes the rules by which those authorities are judged. The flagship example is GLEIF's **Ecosystem Governance Framework for the vLEI**, developed with ToIP (keri.one §7, resource directory), where the arbitration structure is itself a KERI credential chain — GLEIF blesses Qualified vLEI Issuers, who vet legal entities, who authorize roles — and "A verifier receiving an employee's credential can traverse the chain back to GLEIF without consulting a trust registry. The entire graph is self-certifying" (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Real-World Validation`). The ladder's schema pins were re-verified at public-schema `95a07e544`, where the `vLEI/` tree is unchanged since the fork point. keri.one's resources page, re-read 2026-10-03, still lists the vLEI EGF, and its adoption block now also carries "Utah SEDI Identity Proofing" and verifiable-voice items such as "OVC Goes Live with Microsoft" — link titles, not doctrine, but corroboration that the site's own adoption story has widened from vLEI to SEDI and verifiable voice (`raw/13` §11).

This is the direct corrective to the outsider prior that "decentralized identity needs a trust registry." KERI's position is that trust registries "are a band aid; they just move the centralization back to a different level of the architecture... immature and fragmented... primarily address governance for issuers, not issuees," and that ACDC chaining "eliminates the need for trust registries" (`x509-prob.md`). The rise of trust registries in the W3C-VC world is read as "proof that this conceptual gap needs plugging" — the gap being that a VC captures only the immediate issuer→holder edge and lets the broader chain of authority "become implicit" (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 1`). The EGF plus edge-chaining fills that gap without a registry, because the fitness rules travel as SAID-committed governance and the authority chain travels as verifiable edges.

One nuance an adversarial reviewer must hold: the assurance level a verifier demands is *its own* threshold, not an ecosystem-imposed hierarchy. The org-vet schema models Level of Assurance as "a positive number, where larger numbers map to higher levels of assurance," so that "verifiers... decide what level of assurance will satisfy them, and accept any credential having an LoA >= their threshhold" (`org-vet/index.md` §Levels of assurance, :14, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`, `org-vet` 2.0.0; the misspelling is the source's, restored 2026-10-03). LoA-3 ("gold") is defined in KERI-native primitives — a ceremony proving "no MITM between any two members of the signing committee," at least one external observer, and "enough witnesses to reliably detect and recover from duplicity" — mapping to the GLEIF Legal-Entity vLEI (`org-vet/index.md`:20). This is a reframing of CA "high assurance" in duplicity-detection terms rather than administrative vetting, and it keeps arbitration on the verifier's side of the transaction rather than a central authority's.

Two additions from the 2026-10-03 re-anchor sharpen that reading, and one complicates the citation. `org-vet` 2.0.0 adds Ricardian clauses that say outright what the LoA is not: `loaIsTheIssuersProcedure` holds that the LoA "states which procedures the issuer carried out — not a guarantee about the organization," and `assuranceIsPointInTime` "requires a verifier to check the registry rather than treat an unexpired credential as current" (`org-vet/index.md` §Governance framework, :30, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`). Both push judgment further onto the verifier, which is the direction this section argues. The complication is that "org-vet" no longer names one credential. public-schema deleted `org-vet/` at `95a07e544` and renamed its sibling `ovc-org-vet` to `org-identity`, which keeps the LoA table nearly word for word but reframes the credential as one that "connects different identifiers for the same organization" and governs it with citation-style rules rather than the Ricardian clauses (`org-identity/index.md` §Purpose, :5; §Rules and governance, :76-80). Every LoA citation in this chapter is to bakobo's `org-vet`; a reader following it into public-schema will find a different credential.

**The KERI specification added a second instance of recipient-side arbitration this cycle, and its boundary is stated more carefully than most.** The out-of-band-authentication section now carries a worked pattern: the same AID published at two or more `/.well-known/oobi/{aid}` locations "hosted on independently controlled namespaces" — the example given is a domain the controller administers and a third-party platform where the controller has an account — with each URL "delivered to the recipient through a channel whose compromise is independent of the others." `[N]` (KERI spec §OOBI with MFA, L2838 at `fbdd4a615`) Acceptance is thresholded on agreement: the recipient resolves each URL, compares the returned target URLs, and treats the introduction as acceptable "only when a configured threshold of namespaces returns a consistent OOBI for the same AID," so that an adversary "must therefore compromise multiple independent namespaces to influence the bootstrap decision rather than only one." The threshold is the **recipient's** configuration, not the controller's declaration — the exact inverse of the witness threshold of accountable duplicity, which the controller declares in its own inception event. Arbitration over whether to bootstrap at all sits with the party bearing the risk.

The limit on that pattern is stated by the specification itself and must travel with any citation of it. "This pattern does not change the trust model of an OOBI"; the AID binding produced by resolution "is still cryptographically verified through KERI in the usual way," and "the multi-factor arrangement governs only whether the recipient is willing to bootstrap from the OOBIs at all." `[N]` (§OOBI with MFA, L2838) So agreement across independently controlled namespaces is evidence about *willingness*, never about authenticity. A reading that treats N-of-M well-known agreement as strengthening the AID binding is wrong on the spec's own terms, and is exactly the confusion this chapter's Layer-1/Layer-2 split exists to prevent: the crypto is unchanged, and what the arrangement governs is a policy decision the recipient was always free to make. The configuration side of this pattern, and the finding that the implementations checked this cycle do not implement it, are in section 6.

## 3. SEDI: state-endorsed, not state-issued

SEDI (State-Endorsed Digital Identity) is the cleanest application of KERI's root-of-trust doctrine to civil identity, and it is worth dwelling on because it makes the governance boundary concrete against the most centralizing prior of all — the state. Its founding premise "inverts the usual state-ID relationship: identity is 'innate to the individual's existence and independent of the state... fundamental and inalienable' (§63A-20-101(1)). The state endorses an identity; it does not create one" (`sedi-id/index.md` §Purpose, :13-15, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`, quoting Utah Code). The mechanics enforce the inversion: the issuer field names the State, "but the subject is bound by the holder's own personal digital identifier (a KERI AID) — withdraw the endorsement and the identity persists, because the holder, not the state, holds the key" (`sedi-id/index.md`). This is exactly the KERI worldview — key-holder is controller, root of trust is the identifier's own key-event history (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 1) — applied to a domain where the reflexive assumption is that the government owns the identity.

**What the SEDI family is now, as of 2026-10-03.** The paragraph above quotes `sedi-id`, which is still on the page at bakobo `cfcbdcc7a` but is "Superseded for new issuance" and "remains resolvable for existing credentials" (`sedi-id/index.md`:3). The state-issued credentials are now Sam Smith's own — IAR, Core, Residence and Age — pinned to his keripy test module at `80f77b73`, while the presentation, guardian and authorization schemas "are Bakobo profiles for the November SEDI Summit; they are not Sam's schemas or statements of Utah policy" (`README.md` §Status, :65). Bakobo's stated reason for the switch: "calling it Sam-compatible would mislead issuers" (`this.i` `@epnvr5oq`, :2217-2218). The inversion survives the switch — "The state issues Core to the citizen." (`sedi-core/index.md`:3), to the citizen's own AID — so the founding doctrine of this section is unchanged. What changed is detail this chapter had built on, and the next three paragraphs mark each point.

**The state's authority chain is a credential chain, not KERI delegation — and that is the governance seam working as section 1 describes.** keripy's SEDI fixtures at `80f77b73b` head the state's hierarchy "Create State's three level delegation chain AIDs" and "Root AID -> Org Unit (division/department/program) -> Issuing Agent" (`tests/sedi/test_sedi.py`:3165-3166), but every one of those AIDs is created with a plain `incept`, and a search of the module for `delcept`, `dip` and `delpre` finds nothing (`raw/11` §4). The chain is ACDCs: the root issues an Organizational Unit credential, the unit issues an Issuing Agent credential whose `orgUnit` edge is `I2I`, and the agent issues each citizen's Core with a `utahAgent` edge, also `I2I` (:3519, :3598-3605, :3997-4004). This is a fixture — the spec author's intended shape, not library behaviour — but it is the same move GCD 2.0.1 made when it decoupled "delegated AUTHORITY" from the "KERI COOPERATIVE-DELEGATION KEL binding", which it now treats as "a stronger, optional fact" (`this.i` `@n8wq3d`, :258-260). The consequence for this chapter: a reviewer who reads "delegation chain" in SEDI as KEL delegation will look for `dip` events and find none, and should instead be walking edges. Authority here is evidenced by chained, revocable credentials whose fitness rules a governance layer supplies, exactly the Layer-2 pattern of section 1.

The privacy teeth are in the data structure. This is the schema layer doing what the governance layer specifies, and it is a template for how an EGF's rules become mechanically enforceable rather than merely aspirational.

**Duty of data loyalty** enters through SEDI's governance framework, `sedi-id/rules.json`, ~~referenced by SAID from every SEDI credential's `r` block (SAID `EA5O9z0TB932sm8kJIVdAIpwLpEWRWC5--VNS5r69frn`)~~. *Corrected 2026-10-03:* that SAID is now the archived revision at `sedi-id-2.0.0/rules.json`, which "remains resolvable under SAID" for credentials that carry it (`sedi-id/index.md` §Governance, :157-158). The current artifact is a single `l` string carrying the former named clauses as text (`d` `EG8X1nQFCrQ4Rg8BjfE0-BIuKSo1TVbwqymQ3Wuam6VW`), because "Sam's r object permits only d and l" (`this.i` `@2vqmhxai`, :2235), and it is referenced from new SEDI *examples* — not from every SEDI credential. The data-loyalty duty therefore binds only where an issuer chooses to reference Bakobo's artifact; it is a profile, not a property of SEDI-as-specified. The clauses, each grounded in Utah Code, codify a fiduciary posture toward disclosed data: `chainLinkConfidentiality` (a verifier MUST NOT "assimilate, aggregate, correlate, sell, or otherwise combine disclosed attributes... for any purpose beyond the disclosed purpose," and "this binds each downstream recipient reached through the edge section of a presentation"); `dataMinimization`; `noSurveillance` (no using a presentation "to enable monitoring, surveillance, profiling, tracking, or persistent correlation"); `respectRevocation`; `safeHarbor` (accepting the presentation elects the statutory safe harbor, and breach forfeits it and is evidence); and `noOverAssertion`, with `purposeLimitation`, `dutyOfLoyalty`, `acceptGuardianPresentation` and `consentAndNotice` also present (`sedi-id/rules.json`:3, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`). Data loyalty is thus a *contractual and governance* property — a duty a recipient owes because acceptance bound it to the rules — not a cryptographic guarantee. This is the essential point for the next section: the correlation-resistance a SEDI holder enjoys is assembled from a disclosure mechanism (selective disclosure of `sedi-age`), a governance duty (chain-link confidentiality, no-surveillance), and a deployment choice ~~(un-registered one-time presentations)~~, none of which is provided by KERI-core. *Two of those three legs moved on 2026-10-03, and section 4 carries the detail.* The deployment choice reversed: Bakobo's presentation schemas now *require* `rd`, the holder's own presentation registry, a decision that "REVERSES the "rd disallowed, unregistered, not logged" clause" and answers the correlation worry by keeping the registry "the holder's own, so "logged" means logged in the holder's KEL and nowhere a third party watches" (`this.i` `@lm37js6k`, :1987, :1993-1994). And the disclosure leg does not hold in the reference implementation: keripy at `80f77b73b` will not grant a registry-backed Age credential with any element withheld, so selective disclosure of a single age threshold is not available through keripy IPEX today `[K]` (section 4).

**One governance tension is open, and it is a governance artifact disagreeing with a credential.** The `noOverAssertion` clause says "the state endorses only name, birth date, image, and Utah residence address (63A-20-301(2)(f))" (`sedi-id/rules.json`:3), while the active Core carries `legalPresenceStatus` as an endorsed attribute on the strength of "an email to Daniel Hardman on 2026-09-23" from Sam Smith (`sedi-core/index.md`:5). That email is relayed correspondence with the weight of a first-hand report, usable for status and intent and not as a written source. So the rules file a SEDI verifier binds itself to and the schema the state issues under disagree about what the state endorses, and nothing citable settles which is right. It is a small instance of a general hazard this chapter's split predicts: when Layer-2 rules and Layer-2 schemas are maintained as separate artifacts, nothing mechanical keeps them consistent.

## 4. Correlation-resistance and data loyalty as layered properties

This is the section where KERI most sharply corrects a common misreading, so it must be stated flatly: **KERI-core does not promise unlinkability, and cannot.** The core is a decentralized key-state continuity and duplicity-evidence mechanism. Unlinkability, correlation-minimization, and the duty of data loyalty are properties of *higher* layers — the ACDC disclosure machinery, the governance/contractual layer, and deployment discipline — composed on top of a substrate that is itself indifferent to them. An adversarial reviewer who expects the KERI whitepaper's key-management guarantees to include privately-verifiable, correlation-free presentation has confused the layers.

The ACDC spec is explicit that even at its own layer the primary goal is not privacy: "the primary design goal is not data privacy protection per se but the more general goal of protection from the unpermissioned exploitation of data. Privacy mechanisms are means, not ends" (ACDC spec §Disclosure Mechanisms, L1659). And it is equally explicit about the *ceiling* of cryptographic unlinkability. Cryptographically-provable correlation resistance is termed **unlinkability**, but "this is a weak form... as it does not prevent statistical correlation from contextual information." "Contextual linkability may defeat cryptographic unlinkability," and "there is no cryptographic mechanism that precludes statistical correlation among a set of colluding Verifiers" (§Bulk-issued, L2785). The blunt conclusion is that advanced cryptographic unlinkability can be "an exercise in diminishing returns" without contractually-protected disclosure. This is a spec that refuses to oversell its own privacy layer.

The layering is best seen by enumerating what each stratum contributes:

- **The disclosure layer (ACDC):** the graduated-disclosure ladder — compact, metadata, partial, nested-partial, selective, full, and bulk-issued-instance disclosure — plus the private/metadata ACDC variants whose blinding UUID (`u`) forecloses rainbow reconstruction of contents from a SAID (§UUID Fields, L89-91). This layer provides *technical* correlation minimization: selective disclosure of a single blinded aggregate element "with no issuee" (`choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md §Caveats`), bulk-issued private ACDCs generating a fresh SAID per usage context (§Bulk-issued, L2767), and herd privacy via sparse Merkle trees so that "a given seal in the KEL... no longer provides a point of correlation to any other transaction event" (§Bulk-issued, L2914-2924). This yields "3rd party unlinkability" (§Bulk-issued, L2854).
- **The governance/contractual layer (EGF, rules):** Contractually Protected Disclosure — chain-link confidentiality and contingent disclosure — where "the disclosed data has 'strings attached'" and terms-of-use MUST propagate to each downstream Disclosee, who "in turn, is the Discloser to the next" (§Contractually Protected Disclosure, L1783-1792). This is the source of "2nd party unlinkability via contractual disincentives to link" (§Bulk-issued, L2854), and it is precisely the SEDI data-loyalty duty of §3. It is a *legal/governance* control, not a cryptographic one; the spec is honest that where "Chain-link Confidentiality does not sufficiently deter provable correlation due to unpermissioned malicious collusion," you must fall back to stronger technical measures (Independent-AID or Independent-Registry bulk-issued ACDCs) "at storage/compute cost" (§Bulk-issued, L2887-2911).
- **The deployment layer:** per-facet AIDs so that "Cecilia as chief executive at Acme" and her other facets are "uncorrelatable identifiers for one person" (`sda.md §6`); frequent rotation of a citation-signing AID independent of the long-lived issuer AID; and pools of AIDs for herd privacy (Dossier spec §8 Privacy). Origin's applied doctrine makes this a hard rule: "Each facet of an identity really should have its own AID... a separate AID each time a person is granted a new Origin role... security and trust never cross-contaminate" (`managing-aliases.md` §1, L91, origin-platform `cb758b1b5`; re-verified 2026-10-03), correcting the surveillance-economy anti-pattern where "most of us are terribly over-identified" (§The need for boundaries, L53, same pin). This is Provenant client documentation — one deployment's stated practice, not KERI doctrine — and the chapter cites it only for that.

**A finding from the reference implementation qualifies the disclosure layer, and it is the sharpest correction in this refresh.** The layering above assumes that the disclosure layer's selective disclosure is actually available when the governance layer is in force. For SEDI's age credential at keripy `80f77b73b` it is not. Sam's Age schema requires a top-level `rd`, so every conforming Age is registry-backed; keripy represents a withheld aggregate element as its SAID string; and for a registry-backed ACDC, keripy's presentation-registry extraction returns `None` whenever any aggregate element after the AGID is withheld — the code comment reads "Every inspected aggregate entry must be a mapping." — and `None` fails the node (`src/keri/acdc/ipexing.py`:1458; `raw/11` §9 item 8) `[K]`. The reason is stated in the docstring and is a sound one: "A verifier cannot distinguish an omitted requirement from an undisclosed one using only its digest" (:1414-1415) `[K]`, so a withheld element might be hiding a presentation-registry declaration the verifier is obliged to check. The consequence is that a holder proving over-21 through keripy IPEX must also disclose every other threshold block, which brackets the age to a band. No test at that commit presents an Age, so nothing exercises the collision; and the module that enforces the rule, `acdc/ipexing.py`, has no caller in `src/keri` at that commit — its `loadHandlers` is re-exported by `acdc/__init__.py` and called nowhere, while keripy's own commands load the V1 handler from `vc/protocoling.py` — so the `[K]` here is code that exists and is tested in the reference implementation, not behaviour of any shipped keripy command *(boundary added 2026-10-03 by verification; see `bible/05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md` §5.1)*; and Bakobo's schema gallery still validates an example, `sedi-age/examples/prove-over-21.json`, that "discloses only its issuee and over-21 blocks" (`sedi-age/index.md`:5, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`). The lesson for this section is structural rather than about age: **two layers can each be correct and still not compose.** A presentation-registry rule that protects detectability at the protocol layer silently withdrew selective disclosure at the disclosure layer, and nobody owned the seam. Whether it is resolved in the schema, in keripy, or by making Age registry-less is open and is Sam's to decide; chapter 10 owns the presentation-registry argument itself.

The **three-party exploitation model** frames why all three layers exist: 1st party is the data subject/Discloser, 2nd party the intended Disclosee, 3rd party any non-intended Observer, and "any use of 1st party data by a 3rd party is likewise, by definition, exploitive" (§Data Privacy, L1712-1726). Third-party protection is encryption (out of scope, deferred to SPAC/TSP); second-party protection is contractual (data loyalty); and correlation-minimization across contexts is technical. The **no-forced-phone-home** property belongs to the disclosure layer and is a genuine architectural correction of OCSP/CRL: the Observer/Registrar split means Observer-to-Registrar sync happens on state changes, not at the Point of Validation, so there is "no forced phone home validation" and no forced Validator-to-Issuer correlation of ACDC usage (§TEL Registrars and TEL Observers, L1693). Contrast OCSP, which "requires the client to query a central responder for every validation, creating a privacy leak (the CA knows every site you visit)" (`wtbo.md §2`). Hardman's `sda.md` paper generalizes the rejection: "A registry consulted at verification time is a phone-home in disguise" (`sda.md §7`; *corrected 2026-10-03* — this sentence previously attributed the paper to Origin, which it is not). The SEDI portrait is committed by digest with bytes holder-carried because a download locator "is phone-home by another name §301(3)/§701, and creates an availability chokepoint" (`sedi-id/index.md` §Image, :89-91, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`). *Corrected 2026-10-03:* this sentence previously quoted the page as saying "a resolvable URL in a credential is 'phone-home' surveillance and an availability single-point-of-failure," which is not on the page at the pin; the meaning is the same. The active Core keeps the mechanism, describing its image block as "Facial Image Proof Value as SAID of typed media block" (`sedi-core/sedi-core.schema.json`:293) — a commitment, not a locator.

The synthesis: correlation-resistance is a *stack*, and the honest KERI claim is that the stack composes cleanly — the disclosure layer minimizes technical correlators, the governance layer imposes loyalty duties on the parties the technical layer cannot reach, and deployment discipline (facet-granular AIDs, ~~un-logged presentations~~ presentations logged only where no third party watches — *revised 2026-10-03* after SEDI's presentation schemas moved to holder-owned registries, section 3) closes the remaining gaps. The keripy finding above is the standing caution on "composes cleanly": it is a claim each pair of layers has to earn at each pin, not one the architecture grants. What KERI-core supplies is the substrate that makes each of those layers *possible* and *verifiable* — SAIDs that blind, AIDs that are cheap and disposable, anchored seals that reveal no PII, TELs that need not phone home — not unlinkability itself. A reviewer should test the seams (does the disclosure layer actually blind labels as claimed? does the governance duty actually bind downstream? does the deployment actually rotate?), not fault the base layer for a property it never claimed.

## 5. Cross-ecosystem semantics: meaning across trust domains

An ACDC is "a fragment of a globally distributed property graph" whose nodes are "securely attributed fragments... a global verifiable knowledge graph that crosses trust domains" (ACDC spec §ACDCs as secure graph fragments, L1060-1068), and "No shared or trusted relationship between the Controllers and Verifiers is REQUIRED" (§AID Fields, L95). The question is how *meaning* — not just authenticity — crosses a trust-domain boundary when there is no shared authority to define it.

KERI's first answer is **type-is-schema**: "no top-level field types exist in an ACDC... the Schema, `s`, field itself is the type field," and "type information is metadata, not data" (§Type-is-schema, L182). Because schemas are content-addressed (the `$id` is a bare SAID, not a URI, and must be static/SAIDified, §Schema ID Field Label, L186-210), the namespace *is* the content-address. This is the direct correction of the centralized-namespace prior: "no need for centralized permissioned name-space Registries to resolve name-space collisions" — the universal content-address plus content-addressable schema is the namespace, and a type registry reduces to "Schema discovery or schema blessing for a given context or ecosystem" (§Extensibility, L2936). Schemas "do not require standardization — just publication and consensus on use within an ecosystem," in pointed contrast to SD-JWT's reliance on IANA registration (`sdjwt-acdc.md §2.7`); and because a schema is referenced "by its cryptographic content, not its location," there is "no link that can rot" (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 6`) — the answer to JSON-LD `@context` link-rot and term-redefinition, where "the meaning of a signed credential can be altered without invalidating the signature" (`keri-primer.md §4.1`, citing W3C vc-data-integrity issue #272).

But content-addressing a schema fixes *identity* of meaning, not *agreement* on meaning across ecosystems. Two ecosystems that mint different schema SAIDs for "residence" have two different types, and no amount of cryptography reconciles them. The applied corpus is honest about this and shows the actual technique: **semantic interop, not field-name interop.** SEDI's historical `sedi-id` design says to "align the component *semantics* with 18013-5 so a bridge crosswalk is mechanical, but keep the statute's vocabulary" — `residence`, not ISO mDL's `resident` — so the interop is achieved by making the *meaning* of decomposed components match the mDL model while the field labels stay statute-native (`sedi-id/index.md` §Name and residence, :79-82, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`). *Corrected 2026-10-03:* the earlier quotation, "component semantics with ISO 18013-5 mDL but keep[s] the statute's vocabulary ('residence' not 'resident')," was a compression presented as verbatim. The example has also aged: Sam's active Core uses its own labels (`givenName`, `familyName`, `birthDate`), which is part of what Bakobo meant when it said its `sedi-id` "differs in field placement" from Sam's (`this.i` `@epnvr5oq`, :2217). The principle — semantic interop is a governance act, field names are not — is untouched by which labels won. Cross-ecosystem semantic agreement is a governance/standards act (align to mDL, align to ISO 6709 for geo, ISO 3166 for jurisdiction), not a protocol act. The edge `s`-constraint is the machinery that lets an ecosystem *pin* which foreign schema a chained node must satisfy: the vLEI ladder const-pins each hop's schema SAID (QVI→LE→AUTH→role), so a chain that crosses issuer trust-domains still carries a machine-checkable declaration of what each far node must be (`vLEI/acdc/legal-entity-vLEI-credential.json`:99, `ecr-authorization-vlei-credential.json`:114, public-schema `95a07e544`, re-verified 2026-10-03; ACDC spec §Edge Section, L1176).

**The same fork now shows the opposite choice, and both are coherent.** Between `8db57eb` and `95a07e544` Provenant removed far-schema pinning from its telco credentials: at the newer pin the `tnalloc` edge's `s` is described only as "SAID of TN allocation schema" with `"type": "string"` (`vvp-dossier/vvp-dossier.schema.json`:180), and no edge in `org-identity`, `brand-owner`, `tn-alloc`, `a2p-campaign`, `vvp-dossier` or `dossier-base` at that pin carries a SAID-valued `const` (`raw/11` §2). One page states the intent: the `issuer` edge is "Intentionally generic — does not constrain the schema of the identity credential" (`tn-alloc/index.md` §Edge structure, :51). This does not weaken the graph, because keripy's verifier treats an edge's `s` as a schema the far node must *satisfy* and validates the far node against it (`src/keri/vdr/verifying.py`:196-197, 208-228 at `80f77b73b`) `[K]`; what it changes is who decides which far schemas are acceptable. The vLEI ladder puts that decision in the schema, so every issuer is bound by it; the telco family moves it to verifier policy. That is the cross-domain question of this section answered two ways inside one organization's corpus, and the choice between them is exactly the kind an EGF should make explicitly rather than inherit from whichever schema template was copied. Neither repository states its reason, so the reading here is interpretive.

When meaning must cross into a *foreign ecosystem* entirely — W3C VC, ISO mDL, X.509, SD-JWT, AnonCreds, signed PDFs — KERI's stance is **bridge, don't adopt.** A designated bridging party verifies the foreign credential per its native rules, then issues a Foreign Artifact ACDC (FAA) or bridge-wrapper attesting "I... successfully verified the attached foreign credential on date X according to policy Y" (Dossier spec §3; `faa/index.md`). This "transforms the problem of verifying a foreign format into the problem of trusting the attestation of the bridging party" (Dossier spec §3), and the trust is explicit and issuer-scoped, never systemic: an FAA's `art_posture` records whether the issuer merely recorded a digest, witnessed the artifact, or verified its integrity, and `rev_latency` records whether and how fast it mirrors the foreign credential's revocation, where a non-zero value "allows verifiers to treat the FAA as a proxy for the foreign credential, assuming they trust the FAA issuer" (`faa/index.md`:30-31, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`). Trust in a bridge is therefore explicit and issuer-scoped, never systemic. *Corrected 2026-10-03:* that last sentence was previously presented as a quotation from `faa/index.md`; it is a summary, found in neither fork, and is now written as one. Two caveats are called out: trust depends on the bridging party's reputation/policies, and "the revocation lifecycles of the original foreign credential and of the bridge wrapper are decoupled unless a specific governance framework explicitly links them" (Dossier spec §3). This is the interop corollary of the lossless/lossy doctrine: keep the lossless ACDC authoritative and treat foreign VCs ~~"as derivative artifacts wrapped/bridged, not as native roots of trust" (`acdc-vc-diff.md`)~~ **Cut 2026-10-03 (verification): not found in `acdc-vc-diff.md` at papers `68a5e2f76`; the wording is `raw/04`'s own summary. The "keep the lossless ACDC authoritative" half rests on that paper's lossless-original/lossy-derivative argument; the "not native roots of trust" half is now unsupported.**; "AIDs can be transformed to DIDs, but the opposite transformation is typically impossible" (`sdjwt-acdc.md §2.2`), so the bridge direction matters.

The uncontested part: content-addressed static schemas eliminate the namespace-collision and link-rot problems and make type-identity cryptographic. The load-bearing assumption a reviewer should flag: *semantic* agreement across ecosystems remains a human/governance achievement, exactly as the authenticity/veracity split predicts — the protocol guarantees you are looking at schema `E...`, not that your ecosystem and mine mean the same thing by it.

## 6. Versioning and migration cost

Versioning in the KERI stack is layered like everything else, and each layer has its own compatibility rules.

**The wire/encoding layer (CESR)** versions the *code tables themselves* through a protocol genus/version code, `-_GGGVVV`, which "MUST NOT provide a count... but MUST modify the protocol genus and Version of all the following Count Codes" (CESR spec §Protocol genus/version table, L609). Genus lets one CESR serve multiple protocols each with their own tables; version lets a protocol evolve its tables (`CAA` = 2.00, `-_AAABAA` = KERI/ACDC v1.00, `-_AAACAA` = v2.00; CESR spec L615, L865-866). The backward-compatibility semantics are stated as a normative asymmetry: "Any addition of a new code to the code table is backward-breaking in at least one direction... New implementations with the new codes can accept streams from old implementations, but old ones will break if they receive the new ones" (§Protocol genus/version codes, L618). A **minor** bump = "a code is added to a table" (breaks only new-sender→old-receiver); a **major** bump = "a code's meaning changes" (breaks both directions) — with far more minor room than major (4096 minor per major) deliberately (L620-622). This is the same mechanism as **crypto agility**: a new algorithm "enters by claiming an unused CESR code-table slot; non-upgraded parsers still frame the stream correctly (read code → look up length → read bytes)" (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 8; CESR spec §SAID, L1180). Every CESR fragment quoted in this paragraph was re-read at `main` `bad6edd84` this cycle and is `[N]`, unmoved and verbatim; the knowledge-base gloss on agility is a distillation of those same sources rather than a source in its own right, and carries the scope stated in section 0. The asymmetry claim is the load-bearing one, and the six paragraphs that follow rest on it.

**A correction, and it is a cut rather than a re-anchoring.** Until this refresh the paragraph above ended by saying that post-quantum FN-DSA (Falcon) primitives were being introduced "in successive updates" as exactly such additive minor entries, cited to the CESR specification's Annex A and to its post-quantum section. **That claim is withdrawn.** The quoted string exists at neither CESR branch head. It was mined from commit `d35125e`, which is reachable from no ref in the CESR repository — a dangling object from an abandoned pull-request branch, surviving only in one local clone and destined to vanish on any garbage collection. A pin that *resolves* is not a pin that is *citable*: `git rev-parse` succeeds on any object still in a local store, and only a reachability check answers whether a reader can get there. What the withdrawn sentence used to support was the concreteness of the agility argument — it was this chapter's only worked instance of a named algorithm claiming named slots. The abstract mechanism it illustrated is untouched and was re-verified word for word this cycle. What follows replaces the illustration with one that can be cited, and the replacement turns out to carry more weight than what it replaces.

**What the standardizing line says about post-quantum is older than the corpus had recorded, not newer.** At `bad6edd84` the code-table entry policy still reads that "there is the expectation that the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) soon will approve standardized post-quantum resistant cryptographic operations," and names Falcon exactly once, as a candidate: "Falcon appears to be among the leading candidates with open-source code already available." `[N]` (CESR spec §Code table entry policy, L783) NIST published FIPS 203, 204 and 205 in August 2024, so the branch the community is standardizing is anticipating an approval that has already happened. And it carries **zero** post-quantum codes of any kind: an exhaustive search at the commit for FN-DSA, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, ML-KEM, FIPS, `1AAQ` and `1AAR` returns nothing beyond that single occurrence of "Falcon"; the fixed four-character table ends at `1AAN`, the one-character table ends at `a`, and the two-character table ends at `0S`. Any claim that CESR-as-standardized has post-quantum primitives is wrong, and was wrong in this chapter until now.

**The real post-quantum work is on the forward branch, and read as governance rather than as wire mechanics it is the more interesting half.** CESR `v1.1` at `65fd7518b` defines roughly thirty-three post-quantum primitives — FN-DSA under FIPS 206, ML-DSA under FIPS 204, SLH-DSA under FIPS 205 — across the four-character `1`, `2` and `3` fixed tables, all `[N1.1]` and none of it normative today. Chapter 04 owns the wire mechanics of those rows. What belongs here is that they arrive under two new normative rules that are themselves versioning-governance doctrine, because they change how a code point gets allocated at all.

The first rule makes the compact code space a governed resource rather than a first-come-first-served one. "The one- and two-character code tables are a scarce resource" `[N1.1]` (CESR spec §Code table entry policy, L785 at `65fd7518b`) — fifty-two codes in the one-character table, sixty-four in the two-character, against 262,144 per four-character table — and admission now turns on relative overhead rather than on importance: entries there "MUST be reserved for Primitives whose raw size is small enough that a longer code would materially increase the size of the encoded Primitive." The consequence is a prohibition, not a preference: "Post-quantum codes MUST therefore be placed in the four-character fixed tables given by the `1`, `2`, and `3` selectors, and MUST NOT consume entries in the one- or two-character tables." `[N1.1]` (§Code table entry policy, L785) The policy already on `main` is a *quality* filter — at least 128 bits of cryptographic strength, best-of-class operations only. The `v1.1` policy adds a *scarcity* filter on top of it, and that is a different kind of rule: it governs who may spend a shared namespace, not who is good enough to enter it. This is the first time the CESR code space is administered rather than merely populated, and it is a versioning-governance change dressed as an encoding detail.

The second rule removes discretion from table selection entirely. "Which of the three four-character fixed tables a given Primitive occupies is not a matter of choice. It is determined by the raw size of the Primitive modulo 3" `[N1.1]` (§Code table entry policy, L787 at `65fd7518b`), because the encoding must align on a 24-bit boundary: a raw size that is a multiple of three needs no lead bytes and takes the `1` selector, congruent to two needs one lead byte and takes `2`, congruent to one needs two lead bytes and takes `3`. The specification draws out the consequence itself — the primitives belonging to one algorithm are commonly spread across more than one table, just as an Ed25519 verification key uses the one-character code `D` while an Ed25519 signature uses the two-character code `0B` — so a reader who infers an algorithm family from a selector is reading the wrong thing. The governance point is that this converts what could have been a committee decision into arithmetic. There is nothing to negotiate about placement, so placement cannot become a venue for anyone's preference. Taken together the two rules run the chapter's thesis in the opposite direction from section 1: where meaning is pushed *out* to a governance layer, allocation is pushed *back* into mechanism, and the residue of genuine policy is confined to a single admission question about relative overhead.

**The message layer** carries a version string as its first field. v2 format `PPPPMmmGggKKKKBBBB.` encodes protocol, protocol version, CESR genus version, serialization kind, and size (CESR spec §Version String, L1131). The critical backward-compat rule: "Compliant Version 2.XX implementations MUST support the old Version 1.XX Version String format to properly verify field maps created with 1.XX format events" `[N]` (CESR spec §Legacy Version 1.XX, L1158 at `bad6edd84`; KERI spec L273), and the `.` terminator exists specifically to let future versions change the version-string size while preserving regex extractability (L1152). So the stack is designed for old-format ingestion by new code, not the reverse — a deliberate one-way compatibility gradient.

**The schema layer** distinguishes two "versions" that MUST NOT be confused: the `$id` SAID is "the normative determiner," "cryptographically verifiable," and used in validation; the semver `version` field is "informative" and NOT used in validation, though any content change forces a new SAID and SHOULD also bump `version` (ACDC spec §Schema Versioning, L230-234). The applied corpus operationalizes this: "$id is a SAID... Edit content → the SAID changes → registry.json and any credential referencing the old SAID must be updated. Never invent or hand-patch a SAID" (`KNOWLEDGE_TRANSFER.md` §Hard facts, :88-90, bakobo `cfcbdcc7a`), and `registry.json` is "the crawl-free index of released schemas (SAID → path)" (:91-92). A schema evolution is therefore a new content-addressed object, and forward-compatibility across schema versions is handled at the edge layer, where an edge's `s`-constraint ~~"enables forward-compatibility constraints without adding each minor version to `oneOf`" and "enables self-deprecating old schemas" (ACDC spec §Edge Section, L1176-1182)~~. **Cut 2026-10-03 (verification): neither phrase found in the ACDC spec at `f0bd097de` or `2362e48a1`; both were compressions presented as verbatim. The edge-layer forward-compatibility claim is unsupported until re-cited.**

**The Dossier specification supplied a clean worked instance of that cost this cycle, and it is the sharpest illustration the corpus has.** At `b9227753c` **both** of the specification's normative schema SAIDs changed: the base dossier schema from `ECpZgR2Ybj2QCfFZrnNQUqhbLnZuMl5cjL2MtgDaxMbY` to `EKuLzS_oN_mIao5og1NOujtF2QMXloiMCZP7xuAR5tY7`, and the endorsement schema from `EAfn0gRMUnp6d1hyE5qJCN86kBFBp80JwMdm0BqiC1B0` to `ECRjgun8t3cay_YBiVxEY-qXKCz37oUrvE5GvmySwxCh`. `[N]` (`schemas/dossier-base.json` and `schemas/endorsement.json`, `$id`, L3 at `b9227753c`) The commit that caused it was titled "Correct signing language in the schemas and recompute their SAIDs" — the schemas' prose descriptions were rewritten to speak of anchoring rather than signing, which is as editorial a change as a specification ever makes. **Under content-addressed schemas it is not editorial; it is a wire-format change.** The base schema's own IS-A rule is the reason: "A schema S IS-A dossier iff S's top-level allOf array contains a $ref to this schema's SAID, or recursively to another schema that IS-A dossier" (`schemas/dossier-base.json`, `description`, L5), and a `$ref` naming the old SAID no longer resolves to anything. Every derived schema in the wild must be re-pointed and re-SAIDified, and every credential referencing one of those is affected in turn.

This is the price of the property section 5 praises. Content-addressing gives type-identity that no registry can equivocate about and no link can rot out from under, and the same mechanism means there is no such thing as a correction too small to break a reference. A semver-style "informative" version field exists precisely so that *humans* can tell a prose fix from a semantic one, but it ~~"is NOT used in validation"~~ **Cut 2026-10-03 (verification): not verbatim in the ACDC spec at `f0bd097de`, whose L230 reads "The `version` field value is not used in the JSON Schema validation against the ACDC"; the claim stands on that sentence.** (ACDC spec §Schema Versioning, L230-234), so nothing mechanical can act on that distinction. The honest statement of the trade is that KERI moves the cost of schema evolution from *ambiguity* to *coordination*: you never wonder which version of a schema a credential meant, and you always have to re-point every reference when the bytes move. A reviewer weighing the migration claims below should hold this example next to them, because it is a case where the architecture's own virtue generated the breaking change.

**The applied corpus shows what the coordination cost looks like when nobody pays it.** Provenant revised its own dossier base this cycle: at public-schema `95a07e544` it moved from `dossier-base/dossier-base.json` (`$id` `EFf_1uI94VBDwHH7yEkyWUq3jtWy1HXCvG5YYY1DNWfW`) to `dossier-base/dossier-base.schema.json` (`$id` `EHxI61CGs7FRuStz8P6QWJyMK4u2bGH1LQqIXsA4bT7q`, :3), renamed `assemble_dt` to `assembly_dt` and `class` to `cls`, and adopted the same IS-A rule quoted above (:5), plus a division of labour it states as "all evidence MUST be carried in e, never in a" (:5). bakobo/schema at `cfcbdcc7a` still publishes the old base, `EFf_1u…` with `assemble_dt` (`dossier-base/dossier-base.json`:3, :65). Neither public base matches the Dossier specification's own base SAID quoted in the previous paragraph, and whether either is meant to track the specification was not checked (`raw/11` §9 item 9). So three published "dossier base" schemas now exist, each internally consistent and each a different type under content-addressing, and a derived schema that IS-A one of them IS-NOT-A the other two. There is a smaller seam inside public-schema too: its one derived dossier, `vvp-dossier` 2.0.0, inherits the new base by `$ref` while still requiring the ACDC v1 `ri` where the base offers v2 `rd` (`vvp-dossier/vvp-dossier.schema.json`:7-11, :19, :37-38). None of this is a defect in the architecture; it is the architecture reporting, precisely and without ambiguity, that three parties have not coordinated. That is the honest form of the trade stated above — content-addressing does not prevent divergence, it makes divergence impossible to miss.

**The bootstrap layer produced a smaller migration this cycle, and it is instructive for a different reason: the trust model did not move at all, only the plumbing.** The Well-Known OOBI was repathed. Where the specification previously described an OOBI returned from a `GET` to an RFC5785 well-known URL at `/.well-known/keri/oobi/{aid}`, it now reads "An OOBI MAY be returned as the result of an HTTP `GET` request to a well-known URI [RFC8615] using the registered `oobi` suffix," with the example path `/.well-known/oobi/{aid}`. `[N]` (KERI spec §Well-Known OOBI, L2693 and L2698 at `fbdd4a615`) Three things changed at once: the `keri/` path segment is gone, the normative reference moved from RFC5785 to RFC8615 — the bibliography's new entry 42 notes that RFC8615 "Obsoletes RFC5785," while RFC5785 itself was kept as entry 34 rather than removed — and the permissive `may` became a normative `MAY`. An implementer who built against the old path is now non-conformant. Four rules are also new rather than restated: the AID in the path "MAY identify any KERI role"; "the request is unauthenticated and the response is untrusted at the transport layer," so "any returned material MUST be verified in-band before use, as with any other OOBI"; hosts provisioning the suffix "SHOULD make responses reachable over both `http` and `https`"; and the media type "is determined by its CESR-compatible serialization." `[N]` (§Well-Known OOBI, L2713) The governance-relevant part is the second of those, and it is a restatement, not a discovery: it is the same untrusted-bootstrap stance the specification already took at L2629 and L2633, now written where an implementer of the well-known endpoint will actually read it. So this is a conformance change for implementers and a non-change for the trust model — which is the correct shape for a change at this layer, and worth noting because it is easy to mistake a repath for a policy shift.

Alongside it a **Bootstrap Configuration** section was added, defining three named buckets for pre-configured OOBIs. `iurls` are introduction OOBIs whose target AID "identifies a KERI role such as a controller, witness, watcher, juror, judge, or registrar," with a catch-all rule that "An OOBI of unspecified purpose is an `iurls` entry." `durls` are data OOBIs resolving "to SAID-addressed data rather than to a KERI AID, for example an ACDC schema referenced by a `sad` URI," whose integrity "is verified by re-deriving the SAID of the returned bytes and comparing it to the SAID embedded in the URI." `wurls` are "a list of singular well-known OOBI URLs designated by the operator for use as factors in an out-of-band multi-factor authentication arrangement" — the OOBA configuration of section 2 — with two constraints that matter: "Each entry is one URL, not a MOOBI," so the multiplicity lives across entries and never inside one, and a consumer may keep `wurls` in a store separate from the others "so that the additional trust assumptions of an OOBA arrangement do not leak into the general endpoint cache." `[N]` (KERI spec §Bootstrap Configuration, L2851-2855 at `fbdd4a615`) The specification's own hedge should travel with every citation of this section: these buckets "are a deployment convention and SHOULD NOT be treated as distinct wire forms," since "every entry is an OOBI in one of the forms defined above" (L2851), and "the on-disk schema, file format, and merge semantics of a configuration file are out of scope" (L2857). There is no configuration format to conform to; only bucket semantics.

**And here the section makes an empirical claim that this refresh could not confirm.** Its opening sentence is descriptive rather than prescriptive — "Implementations have converged on three named buckets that classify pre-configured OOBIs by their intended use" `[N]` (§Bootstrap Configuration, L2851) — so the specification presents itself as ratifying deployed practice. The two implementations checked this cycle do not support that. KERIA at `7e685edaa` reads `iurls` and `durls`, both settable from the environment and merged into each agent's configuration at startup, and contains **no `wurls` anywhere in `src/keria`**. `[K]` signify-ts at `ffba8406e` has no bootstrap-bucket implementation at all: its single occurrence of `iurls` is a type declaration in `src/types/keria-api-schema.ts`, which is generated from KERIA's OpenAPI specification and is a reflection of the server's configuration shape rather than any client behavior. `[K]` Neither reads `wurls`; neither serves a `/.well-known/` route of any kind. Enumerating every `add_route` call in KERIA at that commit — forty-one routes across `agenting.py`, `aiding.py` and `credentialing.py` — turns up no well-known endpoint; KERIA's OOBI surface is `/oobis`, `/oobis/{alias}` and `/identifiers/{name}/oobis`, and its `WellKnown` class is a contact annotation recording well-known URLs observed for a contact, not an endpoint served. `[K]` So the repath is a live conformance question for keripy and for witness hosts, and moot for KERIA; and "implementations have converged" is, on the evidence available here, a claim about a convergence that at least two of the four implementations have not joined. This is not a defect in the specification — ratifying a convention in advance of universal adoption is ordinary standards practice — but the corpus should record it as a finding rather than repeat the sentence as though it were verified. Whether keripy implements the buckets was not checked this cycle and is an open question, not a negative result.

**Migration cost is where KERI stakes its most aggressive comparative claim**, and it is worth stating carefully because it is both strong and honestly hedged. The argument is that the fair comparison between KERI and X.509 is not deployed-today capability but *migration cost*: PKI's post-quantum transition is "mandatory, global, and expensive... a global re-rooting," whereas KERI's PQ migration is "one rotation per identifier, made unilaterally... not a global re-rooting," so "early KERI adopters are not accumulating architectural debt" (`wtbo.md §6`). The grounding is that pre-rotation already hides the next key behind a hash, so a controller can "rotate to stronger quantum-safe functions over time" and remain "computationally infeasible indefinitely" (KERI spec §Pre-rotation, L1680), and that integrating a NIST-finalist PQ algorithm into keripy "took only a couple of hours" (`kspqs.md`, personal communication, Sam Smith, Oct 7 2025). The quantum posture is "hybrid ECC+PQ multisig plus the hash-hidden next key" (`00-lens.md` claim 8). This is a real architectural advantage — unilateral per-identifier migration versus coordinated global ceremony.

**Two things bound that argument, and they were not stated sharply enough before this refresh.** The first is a sourcing bound: the "couple of hours" figure is a first-hand report of a conversation, not a written or reviewable artifact. A report of that class is usable for status and intent — that the work was attempted, that it is not abandoned — and not as evidence about effort, design or scope, and it certainly cannot be reconciled against anything. The second bound is that whatever was integrated did not land: keripy at `b8f60166b` contains **no post-quantum codes at all**, and a case-insensitive search of `src/keri/core` at that commit for FN-DSA, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, Dilithium, SPHINCS, Kyber and ML-KEM returns nothing. `[K]` So the single quantitative data point behind the migration-cost claim describes an exercise no reader can inspect, on code that is not in the reference implementation. The architectural argument — one unilateral rotation per identifier against a coordinated global re-rooting — stands on the pre-rotation mechanism and does not need that figure; the figure should stop being carried as though it corroborated the implementation half. The honest counterweight (below) is that the *implementation* cost of getting a new code-table entry exactly right is nontrivial and error-prone, and that it remains a forward-looking cost rather than one anybody has yet paid.

**Before the counterweight, its scope, because the scope is now most of the story.** The account below comes from a private, unpublished contributor knowledge base pinned at `9489ac9bd`, **whose own code baseline is keripy `60ab9e08` — 493 commits behind `b8f60166b`** — and which declares itself a description of `2.0.0-dev6` against keripy's current `2.1.0-dev1`. Every landmine and invariant cited here therefore describes a superseded dev line. They stand as **discipline**: a reviewer touching this code should assume each hazard is still there until they have looked. They do not stand as statements about keripy today, and none of them was re-tested at the current pin. The knowledge base's own banner claim that all twenty landmines still hold was verified at `60ab9e08` and has at least one known counterexample since, so each item below is stated individually rather than under that claim's cover. One further correction of framing: the knowledge base writes these entries as advice about work in flight — "The Falcon PR must touch every shadow table" — and that framing is out of date in the strongest possible way. **Nothing is blocked, because nothing has been written.** The knowledge base is candid about why: its own open decisions ask which of two conflicting in-repo Falcon design documents is canonical — fixed-size signatures with compact codes and no digest-derived AIDs, against variable-size signatures with digest AIDs — and its plan defers the post-quantum work entirely behind "resolve the two conflicting Falcon spec docs first" (`PLAN.md` §7 and §9, at `9489ac9bd`). A chapter that named a winner there would be inventing an answer no source has. What survives intact is the discipline; what does not is the urgency.

With that scope attached, the knowledge base is the best available account of the implementation cost, and it is sobering. Adding a single new primitive code is "the highest-risk surface in the flagship" because a code lives in *multiple* shadow tables: registering only in `MatterCodex` + `Matter.Sizes` is not enough; digest codes must also update `Saider.Digests` and `Serder.Digests`, and the relevant `*Dex` sets (`PreDex`, `DigDex`, `SmallVrzDex`/`LargeVrzDex`), plus in 2.0 the `BodyUniversalCodex` (`BUDex`) and `Counter.BUCodes` — "Registering only in MatterCodex + Matter.Sizes is not enough and fails late/silently" (keripy-knowledge landmine L5; invariant §A9). The sizing arithmetic is unforgiving: fixed codes need `fs % 4 == 0`, variable codes `(ls + rs) % 3 == 0`, lead size `ls = (3 - (rize % 3)) % 3` where "the double-modulo is intentional (0→0, 1→2, 2→1). A 'simplification' here breaks triplet alignment" (§A1-A5), and "Get the sizing math wrong and a parser that predates your code desyncs the entire stream — silently, because CESR is self-framing" (§A intro). Pre-rotation verification can even go "permissive on a digest-code mismatch," silently excluding a mismatched `ondex` from threshold satisfaction rather than failing closed, and "New digest codes (Falcon/PQ) widen this surface" (landmine L6). Two further migration hazards live in storage: `Komer` records "have no schema versioning → adding a field breaks old stores" and "Any new field on a persisted record needs a migration" (landmine L18), and pre-1.2.0 DB key formats crash format-assuming iterators so "escrow contents [are treated] as discardable across migrations" (landmine L2; invariant I4). The lesson for a reviewer: KERI's *protocol-level* migration story (unilateral rotation, additive codes) is genuinely cheaper than PKI's, but the *implementation-level* migration surface is a minefield the reference codebase navigates with explicit shadow-table discipline, and a naive change "passes keripy's own tests but diverges from the spec/wire format" — which "is a defect, because it silently breaks interop" (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 5).

**One observation about the tooling around that discipline, because this chapter is where ecosystem maturity is assessed and it is a real data point.** The knowledge base ships a freshness ritual and tells its readers to run it "at the start of any KB-using session or before a PR." That guard is **dead**. Its `freshness.sh` defaults to a repository path that no longer exists, and the session registry that selects between the 2.0 and 1.x knowledge bases points at two more paths that no longer exist — all three were invalidated when the repositories moved, and the script now errors out rather than reporting anything. Nothing told anyone. That is the mechanism by which a knowledge base whose entire value proposition is currency could fall 493 commits behind in silence, and it is the same failure mode section 7 identifies on the interop surface: a rule or a check that lives in one place, with nothing watching whether it still runs, drifts without announcing itself. The general lesson is worth stating plainly, because it applies well past this one script — **when a document asserts that a mechanism is active, the mechanism has to travel by the same route the document does, or the document becomes a confident lie the moment the environment moves.** A quiet guard and a working one look identical from outside, and only one of them is good news.

## 7. Backward compatibility across keripy, keria, and signify-ts

The interop surface is not one implementation but at least four that must agree byte-for-byte: keripy (the Python reference), KERIA (the cloud agent), signify-ts (the TypeScript edge signer), and SignifyPy. The governing doctrine is that "keripy's 'intent layer' is exogenous: the CESR / KERI / ACDC specifications" (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 5) — the spec is the external contract, and "CESR code-table entries and sizing, field names and order, SAID derivation, version strings, count codes are external contracts." This inverts the usual "reference implementation is the truth" assumption: passing keripy's tests is *not* sufficient; the wire format is.

The KERIA/Signify split is itself an interop architecture with a two-AID delegation spine: the Client AID (edge, delegator) and the Agent AID (cloud, delegated `dip` with `di` = Client AID), where "unencrypted private key material never leaves the client" and the agent holds only encrypted blobs it cannot decrypt (`keria/docs/protocol.md §Key Generate Methods`; `signify-ts/README.md`). Externally the agent is "just another KERI node relaying edge-signed CESR" — the Signify split is "an internal implementation detail invisible to verifiers" (`keria/README.md §Message Router`). This matters for compatibility because it means the *external* wire contract (CESR-over-HTTP on the KERI Protocol interface, port 3902) is the same normal KERI contract every implementation speaks, while the *internal* admin contract (SKRAP, the Signify/KERIA Request Authentication Protocol) is a private surface between edge and cloud.

Three compatibility surfaces are load-bearing and each has documented parity hazards:

- **HTTP message-signature construction** is "the highest-risk interop surface" — the signed byte string `ser` must be assembled identically in TS and Python or auth breaks silently. Default signed fields must match (`@method`, `@path`, `signify-resource`, `signify-timestamp`), but the two default lists are in *different order* between `authing.ts` (`@method` first) and `authing.py` (`Signify-Resource` first); order agreement is enforced by the emitted `Signature-Input` header, not the constant, "Still, this asymmetry is a landmine" (`10-keria-signify.md §7a`). Timestamp format even differs cosmetically (signify-ts writes `000+00:00` where keripy writes ISO `Z`), and both must parse each other's format.
- **Key-derivation determinism**: the passcode-to-key path must be byte-identical across edge implementations. The `bran` construction (`Salt_128 '0A' + 'A' + passcode[0:21]`), the Argon2 stretching, and the salty path must match, or "the edge and any other edge impl derive different keys from the same passcode" (`10-keria-signify.md §7c`). keripy's `SaltyCreator` must produce identical output to signify-ts `SaltyCreator` for the same salt/tier/path. The salty-path landmine (missing delimiter → seed collisions → recovery break) is an active cross-impl bug, confirmed as twin #928 (keripy-knowledge landmine L10).
- **Event/threshold/serialization parity**: the CHANGELOG parity fixes prove these are live constraints — `rotate` defaulting to next (not current) threshold (#208), correct `adds`/`cuts` (#359), string/hex `s` parsing, `ondex` computed from the *prior* establishment event not the proposed one (#378), and a `rotated=true` flag on signing, "matching KERIpy `BaseHab.sign` behaviour" (`signify-ts/CHANGELOG.md` 0.4.0). Rotation-ilk switching (`drt` when delegated) and default rotation thresholds (`max(1, ceil(count/2))` in hex) must match keripy exactly (`10-keria-signify.md §7d`). A fresh instance landed this cycle and is the best kind of evidence that the hazard is live. signify-ts's `messagize` built a source-seal sequence number with `new Seqner({ sn: parseInt(seal[1].s) })` (`src/keri/core/eventing.ts`:432 at `ffba8406e`), which reads KERI's hex `s` as decimal and is wrong from sequence number `0xa` upward; at `5a174b545` it reads `new Seqner({ snh: seal[1].s }).qb64b` (:430), the hex constructor `[K]`. The invariant that both implementations treat `s` as hex was broken in shipped client code until 2026-09-16, and nothing in the type system caught it — which is what "maintained by vigilance" means in practice.

**The four implementations are not on one keripy line, and a reader of this chapter has to carry that.** KERIA's dependency tightened this cycle from a range to an exact pin, `"keri==1.2.14",` (`pyproject.toml`:30 at `928ce26a4`; it read `"keri>=1.2.13,<1.3",` at `7e685edaa`) `[K]`. keripy `main` is on the `2.1.0-dev` line, where this cycle's work landed — IPEX V2, `E1E` enforcement, presentation-registry checks — and **none of it is in a `keri==1.2.14` dependency** (`raw/10` §11a). So a claim that "keripy does X" at a `main` commit is not evidence that a KERIA deployment does X; where KERIA matches keripy `main`, it did so by reimplementing the behaviour in its own code, and that is independent evidence rather than inheritance. For the parity question this cuts both ways. It means the interop surface is not four implementations of one specification at one moment but a 1.2.x server, a 2.1-dev reference, and two clients that must speak to whichever they meet. And it means the section 4 finding about Age credentials is a statement about keripy `main` and says nothing yet about what a KERIA-hosted holder can present.

The formal compatibility contract for the admin API is machine-enforced: signify-ts "generates its request/response types directly from KERIA's OpenAPI (`npm run generate:types`)," so "KERIA's specing.py/spec.yaml is the source of truth for the admin API contract; the TS client is a downstream consumer" (`10-keria-signify.md §7e`). But the deepest layer — the CESR matter/counter/indexer code tables — is *not* generated; it is independently reimplemented in each language, "the deepest interop layer (the qb64 derivation-code tables must be identical across all four impls)" (`10-keria-signify.md` Gaps). This is the same shadow-table hazard of §6 seen from the interop side: a code added to keripy's tables but not to signify-ts's tables (or added with different sizing) is an invisible break.

**This cycle produced the best test that hazard has ever been given, and the result is the strongest interop evidence in this chapter.** The `v1.1` post-quantum table of §6 is roughly thirty-three new rows across three code tables — exactly the class of change the paragraph above says is most likely to drift. The finding is that **no shipped implementation carries it.** keripy at `b8f60166b` has no post-quantum codes; a search of `src/keri/core` for every scheme name returns nothing. `[K]` Upstream signify-ts at `ffba8406e` has none either; a search of `src` for the same terms returns no match. `[K]` CESR `main` at `bad6edd84` defines none to carry. `[N]` The only implementation of those rows is an **unmerged branch on a personal fork**, and it is marked here as what it is: not `[K]`, because `[K]` means an implementation anyone runs behaves this way, and no one runs a branch that exists on one fork.

The right reading of that is not the one it first invites. An earlier framing of this finding — that the reference implementation and the TypeScript client disagree about the post-quantum code table — does not survive checking. **They agree, in that neither has it.** What exists is a fork branch ahead of both, and a specification branch ahead of the standardizing line. That is a far less dramatic finding about interop and a far more honest one, and it is the outcome the layering predicts: nothing shipped, so nothing drifted. The hand-maintained code tables are the seam most likely to drift, and the seam has not drifted, because the change that would have stressed it has not been taken up by anyone obliged to keep parity. A reviewer should hold this as evidence of **discipline preserved by inaction**, which is weaker than discipline preserved by process and should not be dressed up as the latter. The real test arrives when the `v1.1` codes reach `main` and four implementations must add thirty-three rows each, with sizing arithmetic that fails late and silently when it is wrong.

**The fork branch does supply one genuinely positive piece of evidence, and it is the best the corpus has for its own agility thesis.** An implementation written independently of keripy confirms that primitive sizing is a pure function of the code, never of the value: its parser selects a table by primitive class, looks up the hard code, and returns the table's declared full size as the element's length, and group framing is the same shape — the count is in quadlets, so the frame is known before any content is read. The payload is never decoded before its length is decided. The consequence is the one the agility argument has always asserted and never demonstrated: **the thirty-three post-quantum rows required no parser change at all.** The commit that added them touches the sizing table and a test file, and does not touch the parser; a 21-kilobyte SLH-DSA signature frames correctly the moment the table knows its size. That is the "read code → look up length → read bytes" claim of §6 holding under an implementation that did not inherit keripy's assumptions, which is exactly where a designed-in property either holds or breaks. Mark the evidence honestly: it is `[K]`-adjacent at best, since the code is on an unmerged fork, and it is evidence about the *encoding design*, not about the ecosystem's readiness to adopt it.

The honest reviewer's takeaway: backward compatibility across the stack is maintained by *convention and vigilance* (spec-as-contract, generated types where possible, CHANGELOG parity notes) rather than by a single shared codebase, and the two independent message-signature implementations plus the hand-maintained code tables are the surfaces most likely to drift. Two findings from this cycle should be read alongside that. The code-table seam was not stressed, because the change that would have stressed it exists only on a specification branch and a fork, so parity here is an absence of divergence rather than a demonstration of process. And on the bootstrap surface, where the KERI specification records that "implementations have converged" on three configuration buckets, neither KERIA nor signify-ts reads `wurls` and neither serves a `/.well-known/` route at all (§6) — so at least one place where the specification describes convergence, the implementations have not converged. The vigilance model works when someone is watching a given seam. Nothing in the architecture says who is watching which.

## 8. Decentralization scorecard: KERI versus SD-JWT / OIDC4VC

The following scorecard contrasts KERI/ACDC with the SD-JWT / OIDC4VC family (SD-JWT VCs presented under OpenID4VP/OID4VCI, which build on JWT/OAuth/OIDC). A methodological caveat first, because an adversarial reviewer must weigh the source: the SD-JWT column is grounded in Hardman's comparison papers, which are KERI-partisan advocacy. The *technical* claims in it (JWT tokens verified only against current key state; `kid` points at a key not an identifier; standalone envelopes pushing toward issuer registries) are checkable against the SD-JWT and JWT specifications and are accurate as stated; the *evaluative* framing ("re-centralizes," "drift") is argumentative. Treat the ratings as a structured argument, not a neutral benchmark.

| Principle | KERI / ACDC | SD-JWT / OIDC4VC | Source grounding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root of trust | Self-certifying identifier; "bottoms out in mathematics" | Administrative — issuer key trusted via registry/CA; bottoms out in administrators | `wtbo.md §3`; `x509-prob.md` |
| Holder key rotation | Survives; identifier constant across rotation (transferable AID) | Solved by issue-to-identifier indirection where DID method allows | `keri-primer.md §2.1`; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Issuer key rotation | Credential validity binds to issuer *key state at anchor position*, survives rotation | `kid` binds to a key; "key rotation invalidates historical signatures" | ACDC spec §Binding to Key State, L1673-1675; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Historical / point-in-time verification | Yes — anchored signatures defeat retrograde attacks; validate against key state "as of" the anchor | No — "tokens are only designed to be evaluated against current key state" | invariant H1; `was.md §Mitigation 3`; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Trust-chain expression | Native typed, operator-bearing edges → verifiable DAG of authority | Captures only issuer→holder; broader chain "becomes implicit" | ACDC spec §Edge Section; `acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 1` |
| Issuer-registry dependence | Eliminated by chaining; registry reduces to "schema blessing" | "SD-JWTs will need registries of trusted issuers... centralized, governed" | ACDC spec §Extensibility L2936; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.4` |
| Revocation privacy (phone-home) | Observer/Registrar split → "no forced phone home validation" | Inherits OCSP/status-list phone-home tendencies | ACDC spec §TEL Registrars L1693; `wtbo.md §2` |
| Correlation control | Layered: graduated/selective disclosure + CLC + per-facet AIDs (see §4) | Selective disclosure at token level; weaker chain/registry-driven correlation | ACDC spec §Bulk-issued; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.5` |
| Crypto agility / PQ migration | Additive code-table slot + unilateral per-identifier rotation; no PQ primitive standardized yet (§6) | Tied to JWT/COSE alg negotiation; migration coordinated | CESR spec §SAID L1180 `[N]`; §Code table entry policy L785 `[N1.1]`; `wtbo.md §6` |
| Jurisdiction independence | Chains to a global root, crosses borders (vLEI, VVP) | CA/ecosystem trust is jurisdiction-bound (SHAKEN not accepted EU/Asia) | `acdc-vc-diff.md`; `keri-primer.md §1.1` |
| Canonicalization robustness | Insertion-order field maps; SAID over exact bytes; no JCS/JSON-LD | JCS "brittle"; JSON-LD term-redefinition alters signed meaning | CESR spec §Order-Preserving L1250; `keri-primer.md §4.1` |
| Offline / open-loop verification | Yes — "verify authority without consulting its issuer" | Token verification typically needs issuer metadata/keys reachable | `sda.md §7`; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Ultimate arbiter | None at Layer 1; plural, opt-in EGFs at Layer 2 | Registry governance + underlying CA/browser-vendor roots | `wtbo.md §1`, §4; `x509-prob.md` |

The unifying "drift" thesis behind the scorecard is that "SD-JWT, W3C-VC, and most DID methods imported PKI/OAuth assumptions (issue-to-key thinking, current-key-state-only verification, standalone envelopes, trust registries, `@context` link-rot, three-party holder bias) and thereby re-created centralization one level up while losing time, trust-chain, and key-history fidelity" (`06-papers-di-drift.md §9`). The complementary framing is lossless-versus-lossy: ACDC is the archival RAW format and W3C-VC / SD-JWT / ISO mDL are the lossy JPEG/MP3 distribution formats, so the recommended architecture keeps the lossless ACDC authoritative and *generates* the lossy format when a consumer needs it (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Lossless vs. Lossy`).

## 9. Load-bearing assumptions and contested points

An adversarial reviewer should hold the following as the flagged uncertainties of this chapter, separated from the well-established.

**Well-established.** The two-layer split (cryptographic Layer 1 universal, semantic Layer 2 governance) is a coherent and repeatedly-stated architecture, not a rationalization (Dossier spec §"Layered verification"; ACDC spec §Extensibility). KERI-core genuinely does not promise unlinkability, and the ACDC spec is unusually honest that even its own privacy layer is defeatable by contextual/statistical correlation (§Bulk-issued, L2785). Content-addressed static schemas genuinely eliminate namespace-collision and link-rot (§Extensibility, L2936). The wire-versioning and crypto-agility mechanisms are concrete and specified, and the asymmetry they rest on was re-verified verbatim and unmoved at CESR `main` `bad6edd84` this cycle (§Protocol genus/version codes, L618). The no-phone-home revocation property is real and mechanically enforced by the Observer/Registrar split.

**Superseded this cycle, and recorded rather than removed.** This chapter previously stated that post-quantum FN-DSA primitives were being introduced into CESR "in successive updates" as additive minor entries. That claim is **withdrawn**: its quote is at neither CESR branch head and was mined from an unreachable commit on an abandoned branch (§6). It had been the chapter's only worked example of the crypto-agility mechanism operating on a named algorithm. What replaced it is stronger than what it replaced — a real, citable `[N1.1]` code table, arriving under two new allocation rules that are themselves governance doctrine — but the replacement is on a forward branch and the standardizing line still has no post-quantum primitive at all. **Superseded on 2026-10-03, also recorded in place.** Section 3 had described SEDI as a four-attribute root credential whose governance framework every SEDI credential references by SAID, with correlation-resistance completed by un-registered presentations. **Contested or assumption-laden.** (1) The "no arbiter" claim is a Layer-1 claim only; at Layer 2 an EGF like GLEIF's *is* an authority, and a reviewer may fairly argue that the ecosystem's dependence on GLEIF concentrates trust even if the cryptography does not — KERI's rebuttal is that this authority is opt-in, per-vertical, and cryptographically bound rather than globally imposed, but the concentration is real. (2) The migration-cost advantage is architecturally sound at the protocol level but partly offset by a treacherous implementation-level code-table surface (keripy-knowledge landmines L5, L6, L18; invariant §A) — and both halves of the evidence for it are weaker than they look. The "couple of hours" figure is a single first-hand report of a conversation with the protocol's author (`kspqs.md`), usable for status and intent and not for effort or design, describing work that is not in keripy at `b8f60166b`. The landmines are knowledge-base observations at pin `9489ac9bd`, whose code baseline is 493 commits behind current keripy and a `2.0.0-dev6` line against `2.1.0-dev1`; they are discipline, not a description of the code as it stands, and the framing that a Falcon pull request is imminent is simply false — the work has not started, and the knowledge base's own plan blocks it on resolving two conflicting in-repo design documents (§6). (2a) A related and newly sharp point: **KERI's post-quantum story is entirely `[N1.1]` plus one unmerged fork branch.** The standardizing CESR line has zero post-quantum codes; no shipped implementation has any; the forward branch's codes cannot be cited as normative and may not merge in their current form, since the branches are divergent in both directions. The architectural agility claim survives all of that untouched — it is a claim about what adding a code costs, not about whether one has been added — but any statement that KERI *has* post-quantum support is wrong at every pin this chapter names. (2b) The Dossier schema-SAID recompute (§6) is the counterexample a reviewer should press on the migration argument: an editorial prose fix forced a wire-format break, because content-addressing admits no such thing as a change too small to move a reference. (3) Cross-ecosystem *semantic* agreement is explicitly a human/governance achievement, not a protocol guarantee; the protocol fixes type-identity, not meaning-agreement, and any claim of "semantic interop" is really "we both aligned to mDL." (4) Backward compatibility across the four implementations rests on convention and vigilance (spec-as-contract, generated admin types, CHANGELOG parity) rather than shared code, with the independent message-signature implementations and hand-maintained CESR tables as the drift-prone seams (`10-keria-signify.md §7`). The one hard test available this cycle came out well but proves less than it appears to: keripy and upstream signify-ts agree about the post-quantum code table in that **neither has it**, and the standardizing CESR line defines nothing for them to carry, so the seam held by inaction rather than by process (§7). (4a) A second, smaller instance of the same pattern: the KERI specification's Bootstrap Configuration section says "Implementations have converged on three named buckets," and the two implementations checked at their current pins do not support that reading — KERIA reads `iurls` and `durls` but has no `wurls` at all, signify-ts has no bootstrap-bucket implementation, and neither serves a `/.well-known/` route in either the old or the new path shape (§6). A specification sentence in the descriptive mood is an empirical claim, and this one is unconfirmed at the pins this chapter can reach. (4b) The four implementations are not on one keripy line: KERIA pins `keri==1.2.14` at `928ce26a4` while keripy `main` is `2.1.0-dev`, so keripy-`main` evidence does not describe KERIA deployments, and signify-ts was still breaking the hex sequence-number invariant until a fix at `5a174b545` (§7). (4c) Layers that are each correct can fail to compose. At keripy `80f77b73b` a registry-backed Age credential cannot be granted with any aggregate element withheld `[K]` (in `acdc/ipexing.py`, which no keripy command calls, and exercised by no test), so the presentation-registry rule withdraws the selective disclosure SEDI's privacy story leans on (§4); the schema Bakobo validates and the protocol keripy enforces disagree about the age credential's central use case, and which gives way is open. (4d) SEDI's governance artifact and its active credential disagree: `noOverAssertion` lists four endorsed attributes, Core endorses `legalPresenceStatus` on the strength of an uncitable email (§3). Separately, the authority chain that issues SEDI credentials is a credential chain built with plain `incept`, not KERI delegation, which a reviewer looking for `dip` events will misread. (4e) The applied schema corpus has forked: bakobo/schema `cfcbdcc7a` and public-schema `95a07e544` now publish different organization-vetting credentials (`org-vet` 2.0.0 against `org-identity`) and different dossier bases, neither matching the Dossier specification's base SAID, and inside public-schema the vLEI and telco families take opposite positions on far-schema pinning (§§2, 5, 6). Any citation of "the schema" for these must name the fork. (5) The scorecard's SD-JWT/OIDC4VC column is sourced from KERI-partisan comparison papers; its technical claims check out, its evaluative framing is advocacy. (6) The honest external gaps apply to the whole ecosystem story: KERI's formal literature "remains thin" (the strongest KAWA analysis is a non-peer-reviewed 2025 Master's thesis), and "legal and regulatory recognition... [is] still developing... constraints of youth, not of architecture" — X.509 has eIDAS legal standing KERI lacks, "a gap of legal recognition, not of technical capability" (`wtbo.md §5-6`, Note [c]). A governance-and-ecosystem critique that lands is one that targets these seams; one that faults KERI-core for lacking unlinkability, or faults the protocol for not achieving semantic agreement, is aiming at the wrong layer.
