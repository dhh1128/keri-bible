# 11 — Infrastructure and Operations {#infrastructure}

## Thesis {#thesis}

KERI's infrastructure is separated control over shared data. The controller designates and runs, or rents, a witness pool that promulgates its key event log; each validator chooses, and may keep secret, a watcher pool that confirms what it is shown; and one layer up, an issuer's registrar serves transaction event logs to observers that verifiers control. The specification fixes that split and very little else: who controls which pool, KAWA's threshold arithmetic, the controller's duty to propagate events widely enough that a validator cannot be eclipsed, the pattern in which witnesses act as a second authentication factor, and discovery by untrusted OOBIs. Everything an operator actually decides — how many witnesses, where they run, who runs them, whether a witness doubles as a mailbox, how many watchers a validator consults and whose they are — is outside the specification, and is either an argument made in a talk or a paper or a choice one vendor made. The chapter's job is to keep those apart. Its central argument is one the specification does not state but its arithmetic implies: KAWA's fault bounds count independent faults, so a pool whose witnesses share an operator, a cloud, a region or a codebase has fewer witnesses than its size suggests, and the defence against eclipse lives in the breadth and independence of watchers rather than in the geography of witnesses. The operational state of the field, read from the KERI Foundation's repositories in October 2026, is that witnesses are productized, watchers detect duplicity among one identifier's witnesses but do not yet share what they see, registrars and observers have just appeared, and several things said about them in talks are not yet true of the code.

## 1. Separated control over shared data {#separated-control}

### What the specification fixes {#role-split}

The specification gives the controller and the validator one pool each, and gives neither any say over the other's. Of witnesses: "The controller chooses how the witnesses are hosted." (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L43 @fbdd4a615). Of watchers: "the pool is under the ultimate control of the AID's event validator. To clarify, it is not under the control of the AID's controller." (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L45 @fbdd4a615). It names what each pool is for. The witnesses are "an AID's witness pool constitutes a highly available and secure promulgation network for that AID" (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L43 @fbdd4a615), and a validator's watchers are a pool that "constitutes a highly available and secure confirmation network" (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L47 @fbdd4a615). [spec]

Promulgation and confirmation are the two words worth keeping. A witness pool exists so that the controller's events are available and so that conflicting versions are hard to get receipted; a watcher pool exists so that a validator can check what it has been shown against what others have seen. Chapter 02 holds the security content of both roles and the reasons they must not blur ([chapter 02, section 8](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#witnesses) and [chapter 02, section 9](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#observer-layer)). This chapter is about running them.

### Where the split came from {#split-lineage}

The split is older than the specification's text. Sam Smith's 2020 Verifiable Trust Bases deck states the principle in two lines: "Shared data = good, shared control = bad." and "KERI = separated control over shared data." (Trust Bases deck, p. 38 @b47f21597). The deck justifies the split as a trade rather than a security claim: "Separated control between controllers and validators may provide better decentralization, more flexibility, better scalability, lower cost, higher performance, and more privacy at comparable security." (Trust Bases deck, p. 38 @b47f21597). [proposal] The phrase "at comparable security" matters for a reviewer. The deck does not claim that separation is what makes KERI secure; end-verifiability does that. Its claim is that separation buys everything else without costing security.

In 2026 Sam described the same split as a lesson about governance rather than about engineering. Of the registrar and observer layer he said "We shouldn't have shared governance." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 16:54), and he put the operational corollary as "KERI doesn't exist without infrastructure." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 35:37). Both are captions, cited for intent.

### Witnesses are not watchers, in operation {#roles-in-operation}

The commonest operational error in the field is the one chapter 02 records from Sam's July 2026 ruling: deployments that let a relying party rely on a witness's view, which is wrong because a witness attests only to the controller's commitment and never checks a delegator's seal ([chapter 02, section 9](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#witnesses-as-watchers)). The operational form of his rule is "An event should not be Seeable by a second party unless a watcher can see it, not if a witness can see it." (keripy GitHub > #1559, comment of 2026-07-31, R-008, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/issues/1559#issuecomment-5138781953) [proposal]. For an operator this has a concrete consequence that the rest of this chapter returns to: a product that ships witnesses and no watchers has shipped the promulgation half of KERI's infrastructure and none of the confirmation half.

## 2. Registrars and observers: the split one layer up {#registrar-observer}

The registrar and observer pair applies the witness and watcher split to ACDC state. An issuer's registrar holds its transaction event logs (TELs); a verifier's observer fetches and verifies them, and the verifier asks its observer rather than the issuer. Chapter 05 carries the specification's mechanism and Sam's framing ([chapter 05, section 9](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#registries)), and chapter 10 carries the extension to issuee-controlled registrars ([chapter 10, section 6](10-presentation-registries.md#governance-split)). What is new for operations is that both services now exist as code-adjacent documentation.

### What the KERI Foundation published in October 2026 {#kf-registrar-observer}

The KERI Foundation pushed `kf-observer` on 2026-10-08 and `kf-registrar` on 2026-10-09. Both READMEs describe themselves in the doctrine's own terms. The observer "is the ACDC counterpart of a watcher. Watchers serve KELs; this process serves TELs." (kf-observer README > kf-observer, L8 @d6368aef9), and the registrar is "the ACDC counterpart of a witness. Witnesses serve KELs; this process serves TELs." (kf-registrar README > kf-registrar, L7 @bf14eac4d). The observer README states the anti-correlation rule as a requirement: "Wallets and verifiers must not talk to the registrar — they talk to an observer (anti-correlation). For SEDI, observers are not optional." (kf-observer README > kf-observer, L9 @d6368aef9). The observer ingests bulk TEL from a registrar, checks each event against the issuer's KEL, and "stores a verified copy" (kf-observer README > kf-observer, L5 @d6368aef9); when the bulk feed lacks the issuer's KEL it falls back to fetching it from a witness. [practice]

That design keeps rule 2 of the doctrine: the observer serves events and the verifier verifies them, so the verifier depends on the observer's data, not its verdict. The observer README says it carries no business logic for whether an ACDC is issued, and leaves that check to the verifier (kf-observer README > Trust boundary @d6368aef9) [practice]. Sam's own gloss on the dependency is that observers "depend on watchers, but they aren't watchers" (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 52:36): a TEL is only as good as the KEL that anchors it, so an observer consumes watcher-grade key state rather than replacing it.

### Two departures from the witness and watcher pattern {#registrar-departures}

Neither README was checked against code, and both observations below are this chapter's reading of them.

The first is that the relationship is permissioned from the issuer's side. The registrar's external port is "authenticated with KRAM and restricted to configured observer AIDs" (kf-registrar README > Dual HTTP, L24 @bf14eac4d), and the observer README tells its operator that "This node’s AID must be allow-listed on each registrar" (kf-observer README > Running, L103 @d6368aef9). [practice] Any watcher may query any witness; an observer may query only the registrars that have listed it. That is a reasonable control against scraping, but it means a verifier's choice of observer is bounded by every relevant issuer's allow-list, which sits uneasily with the framing that the observer belongs to the verifier. How a verifier obtains an observer that all the issuers it cares about will serve is not addressed anywhere read.

The second is that a TEL has no availability layer of its own. The registrar README says plainly "There is no registrar pool, no TEL receipts" (kf-registrar README > Dual HTTP, L27 @bf14eac4d) [practice]. A TEL's integrity still rests on its KEL anchors, which is what makes the design safe; its availability rests on one registrar. KAWA has no analogue here yet, and a reviewer evaluating TEL availability should treat a single registrar as a single point of failure rather than assume the witness model carries over.

## 3. The five Ws: a map, not an architecture {#five-ws}

The "five Ws" — wallet, witness, watcher, web, wizard — are the KERI Foundation's organizing frame for its work program. They appear in no specification. The earliest dated appearance read is the Foundation's GitHub profile, which aims to "Finish KERI's code base and build" One-click KERI "through the 5 W's: Wallet, Witness, Watcher, Web, and Wizard." (KF profile > Why?, L7 @68a0d8883) [practice]. The Foundation's services page attributes the decomposition to Sam and glosses each W in a line, including "Witness – promulgation of key state" and "Watchers – validation of authoritative key state" (keri.foundation services page, fetched 2026-10-15) [practice]. Sam wrote in March 2026 that "the KERI Foundation got funding to support open infrastructure (5Ws) as a public utility" (CESR spec GitHub > #165, comment of 2026-03-19, https://github.com/trustoverip/kswg-cesr-specification/pull/165#issuecomment-4090031232) [proposal].

The frame maps onto protocol roles only in part. Wallet is the controller's key-management side, and witness and watcher are specification roles. Web, in Thomas Mayfield's gloss, is OOBI discovery plus transports: "This is the discovery and transport layer of KERI." (KERIConf26 caption, Thomas Mayfield, KERI Infrastructure and the 5 W's, 8:37). Wizard has no protocol content at all. The services page calls it "Wizard – Guiderails & bumpers" (keri.foundation services page, fetched 2026-10-15), and Mayfield's example of why it is needed is that "the majority of users are not going to understand how to set their witness threshold." (KERIConf26 caption, Thomas Mayfield, KERI Infrastructure and the 5 W's, 9:50).

One gloss is wrong in KERI's own terms, and a reviewer may meet it. The services page's "validation of authoritative key state" is broader than the specification's watcher, which keeps a first-seen copy of a KEL and detects duplicity; a watcher reports, and only the relying party decides what to rely on ([chapter 07, section 8.2](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#watcher-enforcement)). The services page is a funding document rather than a design, but if a design document inherits its vocabulary, the role collapse comes with it.

The frame's useful content is its claim of independence, which is a standard to hold vendors to: "Each of these five roles are independently operated, independently verified and uniquely can be independently replaced." (KERIConf26 caption, Thomas Mayfield, KERI Infrastructure and the 5 W's, 10:56). Present the five Ws as the community's map of the deployment problem, not as architecture.

## 4. Witness pools: arithmetic, sizing, diversity {#witness-pools}

### What is normative {#kawa-arithmetic}

KAWA fixes the arithmetic and nothing else. The controller designates N witnesses and a threshold M, and the guarantee is that "at most one sufficient agreement occurs or none at all" provided "at most F* = N-M of the witnesses are potentially unavailable and at most F < M is duplicitous" (KERI spec > Immunity and Availability, L1866 @fbdd4a615) [spec]. Chapter 02 states the property and its bounds ([chapter 02, section 7](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#immunity-constraint)) and the TOAD discipline ([chapter 02, section 8](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#witnesses)). The specification says nothing about where witnesses run, who runs them, or how many is enough for a given risk. The one place it uses the phrase common-mode is about keys, not witnesses: reserve rotation keys held by a custodian "may be used to prevent types of common-mode failures without burdening the provisional participants" (KERI spec > Reserve Rotation, L1503 @fbdd4a615). So the diversity doctrine that follows has no normative text behind it.

### The common-mode argument {#common-mode}

Sam's argument is in his own voice only at a conference, and so is usable here for intent: "Users benefit from witness pools that have no common mode failure sources." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 40:11) and "if your witnesses all come from the same vendor, the vendor is a common mode failure source." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 40:17). Phil Feairheller stated the vendor's side of it: "we tell our customers, you can't just get your witnesses from us." (KERIConf26 caption, Thomas Mayfield, KERI Infrastructure and the 5 W's, 31:23, in the Q&A).

No source states the derivation, so this chapter does, and marks it as synthesis. KAWA's bounds count faulty witnesses, and counting them as separate faults assumes they fail separately. A pool whose N witnesses share one operator, one cloud provider, one region or one codebase can be taken down, coerced or compromised together, and against that cause its effective size is one. The diversity doctrine therefore follows from KAWA's assumption rather than from any rule KAWA states. Chapter 02 already names the failure mode, correlated witness failure on the same cloud provider ([chapter 02, section 9](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#observer-contested)). The analysis corpus says the same thing operationally, in one model-authored analyst's notes: "if witnesses are all hosted by the same cloud provider or operated by the same organization, correlated failures become likely." (dg-c01-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L199 @149fa6c35).

The argument has a limit that a reviewer should press. Diversity defends against faults in witnesses. It does nothing about the controller, who chooses the witnesses: the analysis corpus lists "Witness sets are controller-chosen" and "No global witness reputation or enforcement" as residuals of witness misbehavior (background.md > 4.4.3 Witness Misbehavior, L340-341 @149fa6c35). A malicious controller can run every witness itself, and KAWA was never meant to stop it; the defence there is the validator's watchers ([section 7](#watchers)).

An older antecedent sits in Sam's papers. A 2019 deck on permissioned ledgers argues that a known validator set is a censorship target: "In a permissioned ledger, because the validator nodes are known, the government could mount a censorship attack against the validator nodes within its jurisdiction" (Censorship Resistance deck, p. 4 @b47f21597) [proposal]. It predates KERI's witness design and is about ledgers, so it is lineage, not doctrine; but its point carries over, since a witness pool is published in the controller's KEL and is therefore a known target.

### How many witnesses: there is no normative number {#pool-size}

Several numbers circulate, none normative, and the best-attested one is secondhand.

- One or four. Evan Asawaka relayed Sam's recommendation during his Q&A: "It was either one or four cuz he says when you have three or four as your signing threshold for witnesses you can't be eclipsed" (KERIConf26 caption, Evan Asawaka, Mobile Wallet, 21:55). The caption's "three or four" is almost certainly "three of four". This is a caption of a secondhand report of a spoken recommendation, and should not be attributed to Sam in public without the video. Its use of "eclipsed" is the vendor sense discussed in [section 6](#eclipse).
- One-of-one or three-of-four as product policy. The Foundation's boot service hardcodes the same pair: its product rules say the "witness profile is `1-of-1` or `3-of-4`" (kf-boot README > Product Rules, L164 @14c8f616e) [practice].
- Five at GLEIF. Karla McKenna said in the same Q&A that a five-witness pool is "the same amount of witnesses that GLEIF maintains in the infrastructure" (KERIConf26 caption, Evan Asawaka, Mobile Wallet, 21:17) [practice], and proposed that the vLEI governance frameworks carry witness-count guidance. No framework read for this chapter does.
- Whatever the user picks. healthKERI's interface lets the user choose N per region and, in Phil Feairheller's words, "our user interface will default to the most logical threshold" (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 24:33), naming KAWA as the rule [practice].

The pairing of one and four is coherent with the specification's inequality. At 3-of-4, F* = N − M = 1, so one witness may be down and agreement is still reachable, and F < M leaves room for a duplicitous witness that a 1-of-1 pool, where F < M forces F = 0, cannot tolerate at all. A chapter can state that arithmetic as [spec] and the recommendation as reported practice. The analysis corpus warns in the other direction that "Real deployments may use minimal witness sets (e.g., 1-of-2 or 2-of-3) for cost or convenience" (dg-c06-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L124 @149fa6c35); that is one model-authored analyst's expectation, with no evidence cited.

### Placement as deployed {#placement}

The Foundation's own guide treats a single host as a demonstration. Its title reads "Single Host (not very practical)", and it says the single-host layout does not "provide geographic or operator separation; production should use" four or more hosts (kf-boot setup guide > Set up kf-boot on Ubuntu 24.04, L1 and L11 @14c8f616e) [practice]. Ryan Hansen said the same of co-located witnesses: "We would not say do that in production. It doesn't really give you much help." (KERIConf26 caption, Ryan Hansen, Witnesses and Watchers, 9:02), and described the ideal as spreading across providers as well as regions: "you could choose from multiple different providers to spread your witness surface out as far as you can" (KERIConf26 caption, Ryan Hansen, Witnesses and Watchers, 10:10).

healthKERI runs four regions, "We have New York, Frankfurt, Singapore, and Sydney." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 26:36), and lets the user place witnesses per region [practice]. Region diversity is not operator diversity: four regions of one vendor are four regions of one common-mode source, which is exactly Sam's point. The only demonstrated multi-vendor witness pool in the sources read is Evan Asawaka's desktop wallet rotating witnesses from two provider plugins onto one AID (raw/18 section 3.4, from the talk; not quoted, because the caption's description of the demo is not specific enough). Procurement is pushing in the same direction: of Utah, Phil Feairheller said "They need multiple vendors and the way we synchronize with each other is the KERI protocol." (KERIConf26 caption, Thomas Mayfield, KERI Infrastructure and the 5 W's, 30:57).

### Pools on other substrates {#ledger-backed}

The 2020 Trust Bases deck presented witness pools as pluggable onto very different substrates, with internal and external witness networks under one root AID and ledger oracles on both the promulgation and confirmation sides (Trust Bases deck, pp. 36-37 @b47f21597; slide labels only, because the extracted layout is scrambled) [proposal]. This is the origin of the specification's backer, a ledger-registrar alternative to a witness, which chapter 03 covers ([chapter 03, section 9](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#backers)). The deck's root-of-trust claim is what makes any substrate admissible: "End user alone may verify. Zero trust in intervening infrastructure." (Trust Bases deck, p. 13 @b47f21597). No source read describes a deployed ledger-backed witness network.

## 5. Witnesses as an authentication factor {#second-factor}

This is the clearest case in the topic of a vendor feature that is a direct instance of normative text.

### The normative pattern {#second-factor-spec}

The specification defines it. A witness pool acts as a threshold structure when "each witness only accepts local events that are protected by a unique authentication factor in addition to the controller's signatures on the event" (KERI spec > Validator Roles and Event Locality, L1764 @fbdd4a615), and an attacker must then "compromise not merely the controller's set of private key(s) but also the unique second factor on each of a threshold number of witnesses" (same, L1764) [spec]. A local event in this sense is one "sourced locally on a device under the supervision of the validator or was received via some protected channel using some form of MFA" (KERI spec > Validator Roles and Event Locality, L1762 @fbdd4a615). Chapter 03 carries keripy's docstring version of the same pattern ([chapter 03, section 9](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#local-vs-remote)).

### The instances {#second-factor-instances}

keripy ships the client side. The `kli witness authenticate` command is described as "Perform authentication against an witness to get a OTP code" (keripy cli/commands/witness/authenticate.py, argparse description, L23 @927c0cb87), and the receipting doer takes a "map of witness AIDs to (time,auth) tuples for providing TOTP auth for witnessing" (keripy app/agenting.py, Receiptor.receipt, L62 @927c0cb87) [code]. Phil Feairheller says he added it: "actually I added, to KERI core, at the very beginning of my work at healthKeri, the ability for a two-factor auth for witnesses." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 25:20).

The Foundation's witness requires it. Its README says witnesses are "secured with TOTP-based two-factor authentication before receipting events" (witness-hk README > witopnet — Witness Operational Network, L8 @39e1c3fc7), and its developer guide says "Before the witness will receipt events, the controller must register its AID with two-factor authentication" (witness-hk developer guide > Step 7: Authenticate with TOTP, L172 @39e1c3fc7) [practice].

### The reason given, and the trade nobody named {#second-factor-trade}

The rationale offered for TOTP was lock-in, not security. Phil Feairheller rejected a proprietary authenticated API because "we thought that was more vendor lock-in than we really were comfortable with, so we went with this one-time password solution." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 25:50). He also acknowledged the usability cost: per-witness codes across four witnesses are "really onerous" (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 27:16), so healthKERI offers one code for all of an AID's witnesses [practice].

A shared code is a weaker second factor than the specification describes, and this is the chapter's analysis rather than any source's. The specification's wording is a "unique authentication factor" per witness and "the unique second factor on each of a threshold number of witnesses". One code that satisfies every witness collapses a thresholded second factor into a single one: an attacker who steals it has the second factor for the whole pool. Whether a batch code is an acceptable instance of the specification's pattern is open, and an adversarial review of a deployment should ask which one it runs.

## 6. Eclipse attacks: two meanings {#eclipse}

Two different things are called eclipse attacks in the sources, and a review should not let them merge.

### The specification's eclipse: isolating a validator {#eclipse-validator}

The specification's eclipse is an attack on a validator. A non-establishment dead attack must get its alternate event to the validator before the original arrives: "This looks like what is commonly known as an [[24](#EclipsAttack)] on a validator." (KERI spec > Non-establishment Dead-attack, L1638 @fbdd4a615). The obligation falls on the controller: "a controller MUST propagate the event sufficiently widely enough that the attacker cannot eclipse all components, such as Watchers, that the validator may consult." (KERI spec > Non-establishment Dead-attack, L1638 @fbdd4a615) [spec]. Witnesses raise the cost further, because the attacker must also compromise "a threshold-satisficing number of witnesses protecting that event" (same, L1638).

The defence is breadth and time. For establishment events the specification says a dead attack must prevent the validator from reaching any original copy of the history, and "This may be very difficult given a sufficiently widespread watcher network." (KERI spec > Establishment Dead-attack, L1652 @fbdd4a615). The general principle is the defender's asymmetry: "a successful dead attack requires the isolation of a validator from ambient sources of the KERL" (KERI spec > Security Properties, L1884 @fbdd4a615), which chapter 02 quotes in full ([chapter 02, section 9](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#ambient-detection)). [spec]

### The vendor's eclipse: reaching the witnesses {#eclipse-witness}

The vendor's eclipse is an attack on the reachability of a witness pool. After placing four witnesses in four regions, Phil Feairheller said "So, someone would immediately now have to attack four different places around the world to eclipse" (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 30:55), and the relayed one-or-four recommendation in [section 4](#pool-size) is in the same register.

That is a reasonable informal use, but it is not the specification's sense. An attacker who isolates a validator from enough witnesses could feed it a forged history only if the controller's keys were also compromised; and the specification's obligation is propagation to whatever the validator may consult, above all its watchers. Geographic spread of witnesses does not by itself satisfy that obligation, because witnesses are the controller's infrastructure and the validator may not consult them at all.

### What is missing {#eclipse-gaps}

The specification gives no quantitative propagation target, no watcher count, and no guidance on how a validator should choose watchers to make an eclipse expensive. The breadth and independence of a validator's watchers is where the specification's defence actually lives, and everything about it is [practice] ([section 7](#watchers)). The same holds for any discovery network: a network that can withhold a validator's copy of the original KEL is an eclipse tool, which is the cost [section 10](#keridemlia) assigns to KERIdemlia.

## 7. Watchers as deployed {#watchers}

### The normative frame, and a tension inside it {#watcher-frame}

Watchers are validator-chosen and deliberately kept off the KEL: "Watchers are not explicitly managed by key events." so that a validator's watcher infrastructure "MAY be kept confidential and, therefore, unknown to potential attackers." (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L45 @fbdd4a615). They also have a reason to share: "Watchers have a strong incentive to share all the KELs they watch." (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L49 @fbdd4a615), and first-seen "strongly incentivizes both parties to support a widespread low-latency global network of watchers and watcher pools that share their first-seen KELs." (same, L49) [spec]. Sam repeated the incentive at the conference: "There's a huge incentive for watchers to cooperate." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 40:42).

The specification asks for two things that pull against each other in operation. A validator's own watcher set should be confidential, and watchers should share widely. A shared network that a validator subscribes to satisfies the second and weakens the first, because the network's operator learns which identifiers each subscriber watches. No source read resolves this, and it is listed as an open question in [section 14](#open-questions).

### What vendors built {#vendor-watchers}

healthKERI first rented per-customer watchers and then moved to a pooled network on Sam's advice, which Phil Feairheller reported as "He said it's no more secure giving them VMs inside of our infrastructure than if I just created a global watcher network." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 33:24). Each subscriber is now assigned watchers as "We do four watchers. So, pick them at random, one in each region." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 36:38) [practice].

The Foundation's boot service makes one watcher mandatory: "one hosted watcher is required before onboarding completes" (kf-boot README > Product Rules, L169 @14c8f616e) [practice]. That is one watcher, against healthKERI's four, and it is hosted by the same operator that allocates the witness pool. Veridian announced watcher services as roadmap: "we are deploying highly available watcher infrastructure services in the second half of this year." (KERIConf26 caption, Thomas Mayfield, KERI Infrastructure and the 5 W's, 8:12).

A vendor-run global watcher network is a concrete instance of the scope concentration that chapter 02 warns about: what makes a watcher "super" is the scope of KELs it watches, and one operator's randomly assigned watchers cover every subscriber's counterparties ([chapter 02, section 9](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#super-scope)). Nothing about that is unsafe while the relying party verifies what it is given; it is a privacy and concentration cost, not an integrity one.

### What the open watcher code does {#watopnet}

The Foundation's watcher, `watopnet`, is a validator-owned watcher that compares an observed AID's witnesses. Its README says it "monitors Autonomic Identifiers (AIDs) and verifies key-event consistency across witnesses" (watcher-hk README > watopnet — Watcher Operational Network, L3 @9be717f8a) [practice]. Its core loop, `Sentinal`, "Detects even, behind, ahead, and duplicitous conditions" by fetching each witness's key state (watcher-hk watopnet/app/watching.py, Sentinal docstring, L811-812 @9be717f8a), and when it finds duplicity it logs that "witnesses have a duplicitous event" and returns (watcher-hk watopnet/app/watching.py, Sentinal.watch, L968 @9be717f8a) [code]. It depends on the witness package: "Witnesses must exist first." (watcher-hk README > Relationship to witopnet, L14 @9be717f8a).

A negative result, scoped to its pin: a search of `watcher-hk` `src/` at `9be717f8a` for `gossip`, `peer` and `share`, re-run for this chapter, found no watcher-to-watcher exchange and no evidence publication; the only hits are an import of keripy's `peer` module and HTTP socket handling [code]. So the open watcher detects duplicity among one identifier's witnesses, but does not take part in the specification's network of watchers that share first-seen KELs, and does not act as a juror. It sees only what the identifier's own witnesses serve, so it cannot detect a fork that a compromised controller showed to a different audience through a channel those witnesses never saw. These are maturity facts at a pin, not defects, and they are the concrete content of the doctrine's concession that observability infrastructure is immature ([chapter 07, section 14](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#residuals)).

### Local watchers {#local-watchers}

The specification already permits a watcher inside the controller's own wallet, watching its own witnesses: a validator "MAY use its own watcher pool to watch its own witness pool of the AID that it itself controls" to "detect external attacks on its witnesses" (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L45 @fbdd4a615) [spec]. The closing panel at the 2026 conference proposed it as the ordinary user's answer to duplicity on their own key: "you should have a local watcher that is informing your wallet of any changes to your key state that you didn't source" (KERIConf26 caption, panel, What KERI allows us to do that we couldn't before, 24:18). The boot service excludes it from its own scope, listing "local watcher or sidecar behavior" among the things kf-boot does not own (kf-boot README > Scope, L50 @14c8f616e) [practice]. Chapter 02 records the related recommendation that a validator's default watcher be an in-process local resolver ([chapter 02, section 9](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#ambient-detection)).

### Failure modes and incentives {#watcher-failure-modes}

The analysis corpus names how observer protection degrades: "Sparse deployment", "Lazy or siloed observers", "Non-overlapping validator trust domains" and "Economic or governance disincentives" (background.md > 6.7 Failure Modes and Degradation, L822-825 @149fa6c35). It classifies the extreme outcome, duplicity that persists undetected, this way: "This is an acknowledged design tradeoff, not a protocol failure." (background.md > 6.7 Failure Modes and Degradation, L830 @149fa6c35). And it gives the phrase that reconciles the protocol's view with the operator's: "Observer mechanisms are core but optional" (background.md > 6.9 Analytical Implications, L853 @149fa6c35). Optional to the protocol, core to the security outcome: "Absence of observers is a deployment choice, not an inevitability" (same, L856).

The analysts' notes add concrete failure modes, among them "Inconsistent polling frequencies (watchers check weekly instead of continuously)" and "Alert fatigue (evidence of duplicity exists but is not acted upon)" (dg-c02-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L132-134 @149fa6c35), and the economic one: "Running watchers has costs but diffuse benefits; free-rider problems likely" (dg-c05-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L175 @149fa6c35). These are one model-authored analyst's expectations and cite no evidence. The free-rider point is worth setting against the specification's incentive argument: the specification argues that first-seen gives watchers a reason to share, and the analyst argues that the benefit of a watcher is diffuse enough that each party will prefer someone else's. The open watcher's lack of sharing at its pin is at least consistent with the analyst.

## 8. Validator policy and operational posture {#validator-policy}

### The rule, and what the analysis says beneath it {#acceptance-rule}

The specification fixes one thing about what a validator does with evidence: "An honest validator MUST trust when there is no evidence of duplicity and MUST NOT trust when there is any evidence of duplicity" until the duplicity is reconciled (KERI spec > Indirect exchange via witnesses and watchers, L53 @fbdd4a615) [spec]. Everything else is local policy. The analysis corpus says "There is no protocol-mandated acceptance rule." (background.md > 7.3 Acceptance Rules, L923 @149fa6c35), and lists among a validator's options on duplicity that "It may select one branch" (background.md > 7.6 Fork Handling at Validators, L997 @149fa6c35).

Both analysis statements are stronger than the specification allows unless read narrowly. Read as saying that no rule chooses among valid histories or fixes which inputs a policy weighs, they are consistent with it. Read as licensing a validator to rely on one branch of a duplicitous log, the second conflicts with the MUST NOT, unless selecting a branch counts as reconciliation under the first-seen discretion the specification grants ("It is at the validator’s discretion", KERI spec > Advantages, L1842 @fbdd4a615). No source settles which reading is intended (raw/07 section 13.6). An operator should implement the specification's rule as the default, which the analysts' own recommendation agrees with: "Reject identifiers on detection of duplicity (fail-closed)" (dg-c03-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L238 @149fa6c35). Chapter 02 holds the theory of local validity ([chapter 02, section 10](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#local-validity)).

### The analysts' notes, sorted against doctrine {#ciso-notes}

The six dg-c0X analyses each end with operational notes that describe themselves as "explicitly non-normative and does not assess protocol correctness" (dg-c01-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L187 @149fa6c35). They are one analyst's view, model-generated, and none of their empirical-sounding claims cites evidence. Where they pull against doctrine, doctrine wins.

These are consistent with doctrine and usable as operating guidance:

- Assume compromise: "Treat controller compromise as inevitable and design for graceful degradation." (dg-c01-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L195 @149fa6c35). This is the survivability objective of [chapter 02, section 1](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#survivability) stated as an operating rule.
- Defaults in tooling: "Provide strong default thresholds in tooling." and validators "refusing to accept identifiers with weak thresholds" (dg-c01-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L211 @149fa6c35). The second is local policy that the specification permits and does not require.
- Watchers as a primary control: "Deploy watcher infrastructure as a first-class operational concern." (dg-c01-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L238 @149fa6c35).
- Custody of next keys: "Attackers who obtain current keys frequently have broader access (credential stores, backups, build pipelines), making pre-rotation key security questionable." (dg-c04-claude.md > 7.1 Deployment Reality of Recovery Rotation Scenarios, L150 @149fa6c35). This does not weaken the pre-rotation firewall, which assumes the next keys are kept apart from the current ones ([chapter 02, section 5](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#pre-rotation-firewall)); it warns that operations can void the assumption. It belongs here as a custody requirement: next-key material must not live in any store, backup or pipeline that holds current signing keys.

These conflict with doctrine and are recorded so that a reviewer recognizes them, not as recommendations:

- Recovery escrow: "Controllers registering recovery intentions with trusted third parties before compromise" (dg-c04-claude.md > 7.4 Operational Mitigations, L176 @149fa6c35). Pre-rotation already is the sealed recovery commitment, and it needs no third party; this is the administrative-trust reflex of [chapter 07, section 2.1](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#trusted-authority).
- Authoritative KELs: "Publish authoritative KELs for high-stakes identifiers (e.g., root authorities)" (dg-c03-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L235 @149fa6c35). Publishing a KEL is fine. The adjective is the error: a KEL's authority is its signatures, and a publisher can make one available but not authoritative (doctrine rule 10).
- Governance as mandatory: "Governance is not optional" (dg-c03-claude.md > 7. Pragmatic CISO's Operational Plausibility Notes, L259 @149fa6c35). Reconcilable as a deployment requirement for high-stakes use; wrong as a protocol requirement, since the protocol enables the observer layer without mandating it.

Two more are policy options with limits rather than errors, and this chapter's reading of them is not adjudicated by any source. A validator could accept only recent recovery rotations, "Validators only accepting recent recovery rotations with fresh evidence" (dg-c04-claude.md > 7.4 Operational Mitigations, L177 @149fa6c35), as local policy, at the cost of rejecting a legitimate late recovery; no time window appears in the recovery rules ([chapter 03, section 10](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#recovery-rotation)). And the analyst's claim that "Higher M values make recovery rotation harder to execute maliciously (but also harder to execute legitimately)" (dg-c04-claude.md > 7.4 Operational Mitigations, L175 @149fa6c35) looks mistaken: authority to rotate comes from the pre-committed keys, and a correct witness receipts a properly authorized event whatever the threshold, so raising M changes receipting fault tolerance, not who can rotate.

## 9. Mailboxes {#mailboxes}

### Not a specification concept {#mailbox-status}

The KERI specification has no mailbox; neither its `main` nor its `v1.1` branch contains the word (raw/18 section 9.1). Everything in this section is [code], [practice] or argument.

A mailbox is store-and-forward for a controller that is not always online. Kent Bull's definition: "Mailbox is a store and forward mechanism." (KERIConf26 caption, Kent Bull, KERIA Tutorial, 7:27). In KERIA the agent plays that part, "An agent functions as a mailbox." (KERIConf26 caption, Kent Bull, KERIA Tutorial, 8:09), and he was careful about what that does not make it: "What I'm saying is that the agent is not a witness. The agent is not a watcher." (KERIConf26 caption, Kent Bull, KERIA Tutorial, 19:17). Chapter 03 covers the agent's custody boundary, where the same role appears as blind store and relay ([chapter 03, section 12](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#keria-signify)).

### The conflation, and its history {#mailbox-conflation}

Two practitioners describe bundling mailboxes into witnesses as an accident. Kent Bull: "because of an artifact of accident of history, witnesses in the beginning of GLEIF's implementation of the the KERI spec, witnesses and mailboxes were combined." (KERIConf26 caption, Kent Bull, KERIA Tutorial, 7:15), and "HealthKERI actually did witnesses the right way in the sense that it's only a witness." (KERIConf26 caption, Kent Bull, KERIA Tutorial, 7:08). Phil Feairheller, answering an audience member who assumed offline messages go to witnesses, called that "a common misconception" (KERIConf26 caption, Evan Asawaka, Mobile Wallet, 19:53, in the Q&A) and said "we did make mailboxes a part of the witness deployment simply because we were moving very fast and had nowhere else to hang them" (same, 19:57), then "So, we have since moved away from that." (same, 20:05). He said healthKERI now offers standalone mailboxes extracted from keripy's mailbox code [practice].

keripy shows both the separation and the conflation [code]. The role exists on its own: `Roles` includes `mailbox` beside `witness` and `watcher` (keripy kering.py, Rolage, L378-380 @927c0cb87). But `setupWitness` builds a mailbox into every witness, `mbx = mbx if mbx is not None else Mailboxer(name=alias, base=hby.base, temp=hby.temp)` (keripy app/indirecting.py, setupWitness, L62 @927c0cb87), and the controller side's `MailboxDirector` defaults to `witnesses=True` (keripy app/indirecting.py, MailboxDirector.__init__, L513 @927c0cb87), so that its `addPollers` will "add mailbox pollers for every witness for this prefix identifier" (keripy app/indirecting.py, MailboxDirector.addPollers, L621 @927c0cb87) in addition to any endpoint authorized in the `mailbox` role. In the reference implementation a witness is a mailbox by default, and a client polls its witnesses as mailboxes by default.

### The open witness still bundles one {#witness-hk-mailbox}

The talks do not mention a tension in the Foundation's own code. Its witness, which is healthKERI's donated code, still carries a mailbox per witness. The README lists the witness server's job as "KERI event processing, receipting, OOBI resolution, and mailbox services" (witness-hk README > witopnet — Witness Operational Network, L6 @39e1c3fc7); the developer guide says "Each provisioned witness gets its own non-transferable KERI identifier (Hab), its own keystore, and its own mailbox." (witness-hk developer guide > Architecture, L245-246 @39e1c3fc7) [practice]; and the code constructs a `Mailboxer` for each witness and serves `qry` messages with route `mbx` as a server-sent event stream (witness-hk witopnet/core/witnessing.py, Witness.__init__, L497, and witopnet/app/indirecting.py, HttpEnd.on_post, L280-284 @39e1c3fc7) [code]. So "did witnesses the right way" and "moved away from that" are true of healthKERI's product offering but not of the open witness code at its pin. Either the mailbox is vestigial, or the separation the speakers describe has not reached the code the Foundation distributes. That is a question for a maintainer, not a defect to report.

### Why the separation matters {#mailbox-separation}

No source argues the case for separation as doctrine, so this is the chapter's reconstruction, and it is interpretive. A witness is designated in the controller's KEL, so it is public and tied to the AID. A mailbox holds the controller's inbound traffic. Bundling them means the infrastructure a controller publishes for the availability of its key state also sees who sends it messages and when, which is the correlatable metadata that the ESSR and SPAC line of work exists to protect. Bundling also couples two different availability requirements: a witness must be up for key events to be receipted, and a mailbox must be up for messages to be received, and an operator who sizes one for the other's load or uptime has sized it wrongly.

## 10. Discovery {#discovery}

Chapter 01 carries the principle that discovery is separated from trust ([chapter 01, section 4](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#discovery-vs-trust)), chapter 06 carries the bootstrap configuration rules ([chapter 06, section 6](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#bootstrap-buckets)), and chapter 07 records why the observer layer is not a discovery service ([chapter 07, section 8.5](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#observer-not-cache)). This section adds the operational and historical layer.

### OOBIs with URLs will do {#oobis-will-do}

The specification's position is that KERI needs no discovery network of its own: "KERI does not, therefore, need its own dedicated discovery network; OOBIs with URLs will do." (KERI spec > Out-Of-Band-Introduction (OOBI), L2635 @fbdd4a615). Discovery is percolated, which makes intermediaries untrusted: "Percolation means that each discoverer, in turn, may share what it discovers with any subsequent discoverers." and "Percolating intermediaries do not need to be trusted." (KERI spec > SPED (Speedy Percolated Endpoint Discovery), L2843 @fbdd4a615). Just-in-time, need-to-know discovery "avoids the need to have a dedicated global infrastructure for the discovery of verifiable data and the associated proofs." (KERI spec > JIT/NTK Discovery, L2847 @fbdd4a615) [spec].

Two operational consequences in the OOBI section get less attention than they deserve. The self (blind) OOBI exists so that a controller's witness AIDs need appear only in its KEL, not in every configuration file that bootstraps communication with them: "Just SOOBIs will do." (KERI spec > Self (Blind) OOBI (SOOBI), L2827 @fbdd4a615). And the worst case of a hostile OOBI is bounded: "an OOBI may be part of a DDOS attack but not as part of a service endpoint cache poison attack." (KERI spec > OOBI Forwarding, L2831 @fbdd4a615) [spec].

### Bootstrap the controller, then redirect {#oobi-bootstrap}

In March 2026 Sam settled how an OOBI should bootstrap when witness AIDs grow long, as post-quantum ones will. The answer is that the user should be handed the controller, not the infrastructure: "Ultimately what the User cares about iOut of Band is to be given the controller AID of interest not its witness AIDs" (CESR spec GitHub > #165, comment of 2026-03-19, R-011, https://github.com/trustoverip/kswg-cesr-specification/pull/165#issuecomment-4090031232) [proposal]. The bootstrap OOBI names the compact controller AID, and "The service endpoint in that bootstrap OOBI serves up the service endpoints of the witnesses (essentially a redirect)." (same comment), as a multi-OOBI JSON response of any length. He tied it back to the trust rule: "OOBIs themselves are insecure discovery mechanisms, whatever you discover you must still verify" (same comment). For an operator this means the published introduction, the QR code or the link, should name the controller AID, and the witness endpoints should be served behind it, so that a change of witnesses never stales the introduction.

### Percolation, as first argued {#percolation}

The 2021 Percolation deck is where discovery was first argued this way, and it states the result more sharply than the specification: "KERI End-Verifiability means zero-trust in the percolation path." and "Discovery becomes an availability not a security problem." (Percolation deck, p. 3 @b47f21597). On the next slide, "End-to-end verifiability means any discovery source is as good as any other." (Percolation deck, p. 4 @b47f21597) [proposal]. The deck's defence against a discovery eclipse is a claim from percolation theory: "User permissioning means honest nodes self-isolate dishonest-nodes." (Percolation deck, p. 2 @b47f21597). That is a theoretical claim, not a demonstrated property of any deployment.

Two lines of the deck have not reached the specification. Speedy percolated discovery "Replaces or Augments User Permissioned DHT", and "Watcher Network may provide super Nodes for aggregated discovery if desirable" (Percolation deck, p. 4 @b47f21597) [proposal]. So in 2021 Sam treated a DHT as something percolation could augment rather than rule out, and expected watcher networks to double as discovery aggregators. Chapter 07 records his 2023 objection to describing watchers as merely discovery mechanisms. The two are compatible: a watcher may aggregate discovery without that being its security role.

The Foundation's boot service applies percolated discovery to itself. Its README says "clients verify the service by percolated discovery" and "clients do not need preinstalled boot-server inception material" because "signed replies prepend the boot-server KEL" (kf-boot README > Transport And Auth, L95-97 @14c8f616e). Its routes are signed `exn` messages under KRAM, and "This service does not use ESSR." (kf-boot README > Transport And Auth, L81 @14c8f616e) [practice]. Chapter 09 covers KRAM ([chapter 09, section 1](09-kram-and-request-authentication.md#problem)).

### KERIdemlia: the boundary case {#keridemlia}

KERIdemlia is a 2021 work-in-progress paper by Ryan W. West and Sam Smith describing a prototype Kademlia DHT that "stores key state for identifiers, which maps identifiers to witness identifiers and witness identifiers to witness IP addresses" (KERIdemlia paper, p. 1 @b47f21597), and it claims "Keridemlia may serve as an end-verifiable replacement for DNS." (KERIdemlia paper, p. 3 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Its write path admits only signed data: "It only allows signed, verified data to be published to the network, discarding all unverified data." (KERIdemlia paper, p. 1 @b47f21597). The same design appears a year earlier as a sequence of slides in the Trust Bases deck, ending in the step "Resolve Node Prefix to IP Mapping" (Trust Bases deck, p. 52 @b47f21597), so the DHT idea is Sam's from 2020 and KERIdemlia is a student-scale prototype of it.

The paper is candid about what it stores. It stores a key event state rather than the KEL, and requires the client to go to a witness anyway, because "a malicious Keridemlia node could publish false KES data to mislead clients" and "Replaying the KEL also verifies the signatures of each event and allows the caller to detect any dishonesty." (KERIdemlia paper, p. 8 @b47f21597). It bounds its own security claim correctly: returning signatures "limits attacks by malicious Keridemlia servers to only dropping data entries or denying service." (KERIdemlia paper, p. 8 @b47f21597). And its prototype did not do the verification the design rests on: "It does not completely implement the third step of verifying the authenticity of all data before publishing it to the ledger" (KERIdemlia paper, p. 8 @b47f21597).

It went nowhere further. The specification chose OOBIs with URLs, and its OOBI section never says DHT [spec]. A search of keripy's `src/` at `927c0cb87` for `kademlia` and `dht` finds nothing [code]. The repository the paper implies was not found (raw/18 section 11.2). No source rejects the design; it was superseded in practice by OOBIs and percolated discovery, and should be cited that way.

Whether a DHT fits KERI's rule that discovery is untrusted is a question no source answers, so this is the chapter's analysis. It fits in principle, and the paper is right about why: nothing a DHT returns is trusted, the paper itself requires replaying the KEL from a witness after lookup, and that is the specification's rule that what an OOBI leads to "MUST be verified by some other mechanism" (KERI spec > Out-Of-Band-Introduction (OOBI), L2629 @fbdd4a615). Under that rule a DHT is one more untrusted OOBI source. But the paper omits the two attacks that matter most for a DHT. The first is eclipse and Sybil attacks on the DHT itself: an attacker who places node IDs near a target key can answer lookups for it, and the paper's own bound, dropping entries or denying service, is exactly the capability a dead attack needs, because denying a validator the original KEL is its precondition ([section 6](#eclipse)). Discovery becomes an availability problem, as the Percolation deck says, but in KERI the availability of first-seen history to a validator is itself security-relevant. The second is lookup privacy: a global DHT tells the nodes on each lookup path which AIDs a querier is resolving and when, while just-in-time, need-to-know discovery discloses nothing to third parties that the exchange did not already disclose. The boundary KERIdemlia marks is therefore this: a discovery network is admissible only as an untrusted availability layer and never as the only path, and that is a real cost, because availability of first-seen history is part of KERI's security.

## 11. Software and hosting {#software-hosting}

### The premises the software is written against {#zero-trust-computing}

hio is Sam's structured-concurrency runtime, and keripy and the Foundation's services run on it. The last section of the hio deck states the security premises: "The network is always hostile, internally & externally; Locality is not trustworthy." and "Data is signed/encrypted in motion and at rest." (Hio deck, p. 60 @b47f21597) [proposal]. One terminology collision follows. The deck's "Locality is not trustworthy" is about network position: being inside a perimeter earns nothing. The specification's local event is one earned by supervision or authentication ([section 5](#second-factor-spec)). The two are consistent, since specification locality is earned by authentication and not by position, but a reader skimming both will think they contradict.

### Concurrency, and logging key-state fetches {#hio-rulings}

Two engineering rulings from Sam bear on running the reference implementation. On parallel receipting, he corrected the assumption that several doers give parallel latency: "Multiple doers running in a single process are only logically parallel. They are still sequential within the one process." (keripy GitHub > #855, comment of 2025-12-03, R-010, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/issues/855#issuecomment-3607600921), adding "HIO now supports multiprocessing." (same comment) [proposal]. A witness or agent operator who expects concurrency from doers alone will not get it. And on KRAM, he asked that every request for missing key state be visible: "Whenever you create a cue to get keystate, also do a logger.info so that someone watching the logs can do analytics on missing keystate." (keripy GitHub > #1323, review of 2026-03-12, R-070, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/pull/1323#pullrequestreview-3939540930) [proposal]. Missing key state is the signal an operator would see first if an identifier's witnesses were unreachable or a validator were being isolated, so this is the one logging rule in the corpus with a security reading.

### Deployment stack, and the unexplained container objection {#deployment-stack}

The Foundation's documented stack is Python 3.14, libsodium, Falcon, nginx terminating TLS, and Circus under systemd (kf-boot setup guide @14c8f616e; witness-hk Ansible README > Architecture @39e1c3fc7) [practice]. The boot service "runs under one root HIO `Doist`" (kf-boot README > Runtime Shape, L135 @14c8f616e). The boot service is a private control plane with public data planes, in Keanu Pahio's description "the boot server is private. So it's not publicly accessible." and "But the witness server and the watcher server, those two are public." (KERIConf26 caption, Keanu Pahio, KERI Development and Deployment, 6:59 and 7:09), and the witness README says of the boot host "Keep on localhost in production." (witness-hk README > witopnet-sample.sh, L176 @39e1c3fc7) [practice].

Sam's objection to containers is relayed, not argued. Ryan Hansen: "Don't tell Sam though because he does not like Docker for this kind of a deployment for security reasons" and "Sam's pretty adamant that there's some major security concerns about that approach." (KERIConf26 caption, Ryan Hansen, Witnesses and Watchers, 11:54 and 12:17). No source read states what the concerns are. That is a gap: a doctrine-level position, that KERI infrastructure should not be containerized, exists only as hearsay in one talk. Meanwhile the Foundation's witness repository carries a container build and a Helm chart on a `v1.3.6` branch (commit `272ded4d3`, 2026-09-28, files under `charts/witness-hk/` and `.github/workflows/build-push.yml`), not on `main` at the pin [practice]. Whether that branch reaches `main` is the practical test of how binding the objection is.

One oddity in the Foundation's deployment code is recorded without interpretation. The witness repository's Ansible pilot deploys a process tree ending in "run-keripy-witness.sh → keri runWitness(...)" with "editable checkouts of `keripy` and `hio`" (witness-hk Ansible README > Architecture, L14 and L18 @39e1c3fc7) [practice]. That reads as running keripy's built-in witness rather than `witopnet`, the package the same repository ships. Which witness the pilot actually runs was not checked.

## 12. Running a witness: what the reference code does {#witness-code}

Two facts about the reference witness matter to anyone operating one, and both are at [code].

The first is one witness per controller. healthKERI's witnesses, in Phil Feairheller's words, "are non-promiscuous. They will only witness for the people for the one AID that you reserve them for." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Feairheller, Infrastructure for Security, 25:59), each with its own keystore. The open code keeps that shape: a provisioned witness holds a "list of controller AIDs this witness serves (currently one)." (witness-hk witopnet/core/witnessing.py, Witness docstring, L471 @39e1c3fc7), and the guide notes that "it rejects AIDs it does not recognize." (witness-hk developer guide > Step 7: Authenticate with TOTP, L186-187 @39e1c3fc7) [code]. Nothing in the specification requires or recommends this; a witness is a non-transferable AID designated in some controller's KEL, and nothing stops many controllers designating the same one. The arguments for one per controller are a vendor's: isolation, no shared keystore, simple scaling. The docstring's "currently one" marks it as a present choice rather than a rule. A plausible privacy benefit, that a shared witness learns which AIDs share an operator, is not argued in any source read.

The second is the TOAD defect chapter 02 records: keripy's receiptor waits for every witness rather than the threshold, so one witness down can block receipting indefinitely ([chapter 02, section 8](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#witnesses)). An operator sizing a pool for F* = 1 should know that the reference client, at the pin chapter 02 read, does not yet take the benefit.

## 13. The KERI Foundation and the vendor landscape, as of 2026-10-15 {#landscape}

Everything in this section is [practice], dated, and the first thing a refresh should re-read.

### What exists {#landscape-exists}

The Foundation's open witness (`witness-hk`, package `witopnet`), watcher (`watcher-hk`, package `watopnet`) and boot service (`kf-boot`) derive from healthKERI's donated code. Sam said so at the conference: "HealthKERI had developed Locksmith wallet, watchers and witnesses for their proprietary products." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 43:33). The desktop wallet is `locksmith`. `kf-registrar` and `kf-observer` are new in October 2026 ([section 2](#kf-registrar-observer)). The older `keri-foundation/observer` and `keri-foundation/registrar` repositories remain one-line placeholders; raw/14 section 5's statement that neither service was implemented was true of those two and is superseded by the `kf-` pair. The boot service's onboarding allocates a witness pool and a hosted watcher, after which the wallet rotates its account AID onto the pool (kf-boot README > Onboarding Flow @14c8f616e).

Jay Alexander Elliot's argument for keeping the wallet and provider plugins separate is the Foundation's openness claim: absent that boundary, "the system may still look open on paper, but it becomes less open in practice" (KERIConf26 caption, Jay Alexander Elliot, Open KERI Infrastructure, 1:27).

### What is promised {#landscape-promised}

Hosted test networks: "we'll be providing some witness and watcher networks on our own servers so that people who want to test it out can test it out" (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 44:04). Whether they are live was not checked. One-click KERI: the Foundation's profile, in a 2024 statement, says "we hope to finish One-click-KERI in a year" (KF profile > When?, L23 @68a0d8883), and Sam described the same goal in April 2026 as "One click, it'll install KERI infrastructure for you." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 41:22). Per-ecosystem deployment recommendations: Phil Feairheller expects them from the Foundation, "I suspect that KERI Foundation will be a central location for that stuff." (KERIConf26 caption, Evan Asawaka, Mobile Wallet, 22:32, in the Q&A). Nothing of the kind was found in the repositories or pages read.

Both package READMEs give `pip install witopnet` and `pip install watopnet` under a "From PyPI" heading (witness-hk README > From PyPI, L34 @39e1c3fc7; watcher-hk README > From PyPI, L43 @9be717f8a). ## 14. What is established, what is contested, and open questions {#established-contested}

### Established {#established}

The role split is normative: witnesses are the controller's promulgation network and watchers the validator's confirmation network, and neither party controls the other's pool. KAWA's inequality is normative, and nothing about pool size, placement or operator is. The controller's duty to propagate events widely enough that a validator cannot be eclipsed is normative, and the defence it names is watcher breadth. Witnesses as a thresholded second factor is a normative pattern with a shipped instance in keripy and a required instance in the Foundation's witness. Discovery is untrusted and needs no network of its own. The specification has no mailbox.

### Contested or unresolved {#contested}

Whether KAWA's guarantees survive correlated witnesses is not contested so much as unstated: the bounds assume independent faults, and the diversity doctrine that follows is synthesis, carried in this corpus only in captions and a model-authored analysis. The one-or-four witness recommendation is secondhand. The analysis corpus's statements that there is no protocol-mandated acceptance rule and that a validator may select a branch go further than the specification's MUST NOT trust unless read narrowly. Sam's objection to containers is hearsay with no stated reason. The vendor sense of eclipse is not the specification's.

### Open questions {#open-questions}

1. Is the per-witness mailbox in `witness-hk` vestigial, or is the open witness still meant to serve as a mailbox ([section 9](#witness-hk-mailbox))?
2. What are Sam's security concerns about containerized witness and watcher deployment ([section 11](#deployment-stack))?
3. Does any watcher implementation share first-seen KELs or duplicity evidence with other watchers, as the specification's incentive argument assumes ([section 7](#watopnet))?
4. How does a validator reconcile the specification's confidential watcher set with subscribing to a vendor's shared watcher network ([section 7](#watcher-frame))?
5. Is one TOTP code across all of an AID's witnesses an acceptable instance of the specification's unique per-witness factor ([section 5](#second-factor-trade))?
6. Does one witness per controller AID have a written privacy rationale, or is it purely operational ([section 12](#witness-code))?
7. Why does the registrar allow-list observers, and how does a verifier obtain an observer that every relevant issuer's registrar will serve ([section 2](#registrar-departures))?
8. Which witness does the `witness-hk` Ansible pilot deploy, keripy's `runWitness` or `witopnet` ([section 11](#deployment-stack))?
9. Will the vLEI governance frameworks or the Foundation publish witness-count guidance ([section 4](#pool-size))?
10. Was KERIdemlia ever evaluated against DHT eclipse and Sybil attacks or lookup privacy, or set aside when percolated discovery arrived ([section 10](#keridemlia))?
11. Can `background.md`'s "select one branch" be squared with the specification's MUST NOT trust rule ([section 8](#acceptance-rule))?
