# 12 — Confidentiality and Secure Messaging {#confidentiality}

## Thesis {#thesis}

KERI's core makes no promise about who can read a message or who can see that two parties are talking. [Chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#layered-privacy) says so and defers protection against third parties to SPAC and TSP. This chapter is where that deferral lands. The answer it finds is a priority order and a construction. The order is authenticity first, confidentiality second, and the best privacy those two allow third, because Sam Smith argues the first two can be won outright with ordinary cryptography while privacy can only be defended at a cost. The construction is ESSR, encrypt-sender-sign-receiver, applied between autonomic identifiers instead of long-term public keys: the sender's identifier goes inside the ciphertext, the receiver's identifier goes under the signature, and each party's keys are looked up from its key event log, so the binding survives rotation. Everything above that construction, which is the part ordinary readers mean by privacy, is built from three things: relationships partitioned into contexts so that the context rather than the identifier is what an observer can correlate, contexts nested inside other contexts and routed through intermediaries so that third parties see only generic traffic, and, for data already shared with a second party, legal accountability rather than concealment.

The reviewer's reflexes this chapter corrects are three. Privacy is not the same property as confidentiality, and KERI's authors use the narrower surveillance meaning of privacy, which is about who talked to whom, not what was said. Cryptographic unlinkability is not the goal: the authors argue it is defeated by contextual re-identification and that it costs accountability. And the honest boundary is stated by the specification that carries this work rather than by its critics: in ToIP's Trust Spanning Protocol, authenticity and confidentiality are cryptographic, while metadata privacy depends on what intermediaries do, and timing, size and frequency survive every mechanism the protocol offers.

## Status {#status}

This is the least normative chapter in the bible. Almost everything in it is [proposal]: whitepapers and slide decks in Sam Smith's Papers repository, written between 2021 and August 2026, most of them stating his position in his own voice and several saying so ("In my opinion", SPAC paper > PAC Trilemma, L11 @b47f21597). The PAC trilemma is posited, not proved. Apart from the TSP sentences marked [spec] in them, nothing in sections [1](#trade-space), [2](#vocabulary), [3](#effective-privacy), [5](#contexts) or [7](#sustainable-privacy) is in any specification.

Three things have reached further, and they reached different places.

- [spec] in CESR: the ESSR wrapper and payload count codes and the X25519 sealed-box and HPKE-Base cipher codes are in the CESR specification's master table on both `main` and `v1.1` ([section 9](#wire)).
- [spec], experimental, outside the KERI suite: ToIP's Trust Spanning Protocol specification adopts ESSR by name, together with SPAC's nesting, routing and relationship forming, in recognizable form ([section 10](#tsp-spec)). Its status line reads "Experimental Implementor's Draft Rev 3 with Errata (September 30, 2026)" (TSP spec, L4 @5599b4556). The [spec] marker on TSP text in this chapter means present on the `main` branch ToIP is standardizing, and that branch calls itself experimental. TSP is also not a KERI-suite specification: a KERI AID is one kind of identifier it accepts among several, and it is written by a different working group.
- [code] in keripy, narrowly: one ESSR send path and SPAC's payload-type tag ([section 11](#implementation)). There is no receive handler, no routing, no relationship forming and no padding. keripy has ESSR, not TSP.

Talks from the KERI Conference of April 2026 are cited for intent and status only, as edited captions. Sam co-authors both the SPAC papers and the TSP specification, so agreement between them is not independent corroboration; where they differ, the difference is recorded and neither is preferred silently.

## 1. The trade space: authenticity and confidentiality before privacy {#trade-space}

### The PAC trilemma {#pac-trilemma}

The organizing claim is a trilemma, stated as Sam's opinion and not proved [proposal]: "One can have any two of the three (privacy, authenticity, confidentiality) at the highest level but not all three." (SPAC paper > PAC Trilemma, L13 @b47f21597). Its 2021 form adds the mechanism that makes it bite between authenticity and privacy: strong authenticity is non-repudiation, and a statement anyone can prove you made is a statement that links you. "Transitive value transfer will violate complete privacy." (PAC deck, slide 9 @b47f21597). The same deck gives the only formal definitions of the two kinds of privacy in the corpus: strong privacy is "un-correlated interactions over unbounded time and space", weak privacy is "un-correlated interactions over bounded time and space" (PAC deck, slide 12 @b47f21597). The weak definition is the seed of the time-value argument of [section 7](#sustainable-privacy).

The wording has moved, and a reviewer quoting it should say which version. The 2021 deck has both "to the same degree" (PAC deck, slide 2 @b47f21597) and "May exhibit any two at the highest level but not all three at the highest level" (PAC deck, slide 81 @b47f21597). The 2023 paper uses "at the highest level". The 2025 deck reverts to the older phrase and applies it to an interaction: "An interaction between two parties may be two of the three, private, authentic, and confidential to the same degree" (Seven Privacies deck, slide 8 @b47f21597). The two are not the same claim. The phrase about the highest level bounds the corner of the trade space; the phrase about the same degree bounds every symmetric point inside it. No source reconciles them ([open question 1](#open-questions)).

### Priority is not layering {#priority-not-layering}

The order of priority is ToIP's, adopted by Sam: "one should start with high authenticity, then high confidentiality, and then as high as possible privacy" (SPAC paper > PAC Trilemma, L17 @b47f21597). He separates that order from the order in which mechanisms are layered in a message: "the ordering of the ToIP design goals w.r.t. authenticity, confidentiality, and privacy may be different from the ordering of layers" (SPAC paper > PAC Trilemma, L31 @b47f21597), and he calls the conflation "a source of much confusion in prior discussions" (L33). A reviewer who infers from the priority of authenticity that the signature must be the innermost layer has made exactly that conflation; ESSR in [section 4](#essr) signs on the outside.

### Cold war and hot war {#cold-hot-war}

The argument for putting privacy last is an asymmetry between two kinds of contest [proposal]. "Authenticity and confidentiality are like a cold war." Strong signatures and strong encryption cost little and can be made arbitrarily strong, so "there need be no casualties in this cold war" (SPAC paper > Cold War versus Hot War, L116 @b47f21597). Privacy is different because every identifier used for routing is an attack surface for correlation and the adversary's resources are unbounded: "casualties are unavoidable" (L118), so "In the hot war of privacy, we have to Pareto optimize protection mechanisms." (L128). The paper's subtitle states the resulting objective: "The Best Privacy Possible Given the Strongest Authenticity and Confidentiality" (SPAC paper, L3 @b47f21597).

That is a design objective, not a theorem, and its assumption is load-bearing: that the cryptography of the cold war really is cheap and sufficient. The paper's own qualification is that the only weakness left is "in the strength of the key management of the associated key pairs" (L116), which is KERI's home ground ([chapter 03, section 5](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#pre-rotation)). A reviewer who thinks privacy deserves a higher priority is disputing the objective function, and should say so, rather than faulting a design for meeting the objective it states.

## 2. Vocabulary: privacy, confidentiality, metadata privacy {#vocabulary}

### Who versus what {#who-versus-what}

Sam's working definitions have been stable since 2021 [proposal]. "Privacy is primarily about control over the disclosure of who participated in the conversation (non-content meta-data = identifiers)." "Confidentiality is primarily about control over the disclosure of what (content data) was said in the conversation and to whom it was said (partitioning)." (SPAC deck, slide 10 @b47f21597). The paper extends who to include "conversation (interaction) identifiers" (SPAC paper > PAC Trilemma, L48 @b47f21597), which is why interaction identifiers become a privacy problem in [section 6](#interaction-identifiers).

There are two definitions of privacy in circulation, and the paper chooses one for protocol design. The ToIP definition is about a receiver honoring the sender's expectations about shared data; the surveillance definition is about "the correlatability of the publically viewable metadata included in the communication" (SPAC paper > Privacy and Confidentiality, L57 @b47f21597). The choice: "for the purposes of protocol design the definitions of confidentiality and privacy that are most suitable are the surveillance definitions" (L78). The legal footing is the US distinction between content and non-content metadata: "Relatively weak legal protection (subpoena) for identifiers", "Relatively strong legal protection (warrant) for content" (L73, L75). The first definition does not disappear; it becomes the subject of [section 7](#accountability), where it is answered by contract rather than by protocol.

The TSP specification makes the same split and names it, which is the vocabulary a reviewer should use when talking to the wider community [spec]. The protection against correlation of envelope metadata "is termed metadata privacy, differentiating it from the narrower understanding of privacy" (TSP spec > Authenticity, Confidentiality, and Metadata Privacy, L105 @5599b4556), where the narrower understanding is content exposure, which TSP calls confidentiality. Its summary: "TSP messages always assure authenticity, optionally confidentiality, and if utilized, metadata privacy." (L107).

### The three-party exploitation model {#three-parties}

The model that organizes the rest is the one [chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#three-party-model) already cites from the ACDC specification: a first party discloses to a second, and anyone else is a third. The SPAC paper adds two refinements [proposal]. Privacy toward a third party "is protected if the 3rd party has no knowledge of the identifiers used by the 1st and 2nd parties" (SPAC paper > Three-Party Exploitation Model, L142 @b47f21597). And intermediaries have no fixed place in the model: "An intermediary may be considered a 1st, 2nd, or 3rd party depending on how and by whom it is trusted or not." (L140). That second sentence is the hinge of [section 6](#nesting-routing): an intermediary that both ends trust is a second party to the routing metadata it handles and a third party to the content it carries.

The division of labor that follows is the one 06 records in a line, and this chapter fills its first term. Protection from third parties is cryptographic and structural (this chapter); protection from second parties is contractual ([section 7](#accountability), and [chapter 05, section 8](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#contractual-disclosure)); correlation-minimization across contexts is technical ([section 5](#contexts), and the disclosure layer of [chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#disclosure-layer)).

### The drift toward "confidential" {#confidential-drift}

Between 2023 and 2026 Sam's vocabulary moved toward the word confidential, and the move is the newest doctrine in this section [proposal]. A January 2026 fragment says that for data shared over the internet, between private and confidential "the difference is in name only" (Confidential data fragment, L3 @b47f21597), and that the word privacy "carries an emotional bonus compared to" confidentiality (Confidential data fragment, L5 @b47f21597). Its conclusion is the strongest statement in the corpus of why second-party protection cannot be technical: "once shared, there is no technology that by itself protects the first party's interests" (Confidential data fragment > Three-Party Model, L11 @b47f21597). The 2025 deck turns this into a taxonomy: "Information that is not-shared = private (one party data)" and "Information that is shared with restrictions = confidential" (Seven Privacies deck, slide 13 @b47f21597). The deck never enumerates seven privacies; its Combinations slide (slide 15) lists seven states of sharing, surveillance and identification, and reading those as the title's seven is an inference this chapter does not adopt as a defined term ([open question 9](#open-questions)).

The practical effect for a reviewer is that a KERI author who calls shared data confidential is usually making a claim about legal protection of something already disclosed, not about encryption.

## 3. Effective privacy and the threat ranking {#effective-privacy}

### Exploitation, not correlation {#exploitation-not-correlation}

The move that makes the rest tractable is a change of target [proposal]: "Protection against exploitation via correlation provides a more gentle trade space than mere protection against correlation." (SPAC paper > Effective Privacy, L103 @b47f21597). Protection against correlation as such has no stopping point, because "One can always play the equivalent of a trump card" (SPAC paper > Cold War versus Hot War, L122 @b47f21597): some adversary with enough resources can always correlate. The target becomes exploitably correlatable identifiers, and the goal effective privacy. This is the same move the editor makes in 2026 when he says the achievable goal is decorrelation, not uncorrelatability ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#decorrelation)); the SPAC paper is its earliest written form.

The assumption is explicit and a reviewer may attack it: state actors are out of scope "for the time being", because "to attempt state-actor proof mitigations would likely take us down rabbit holes that we can never return from" (SPAC paper > Exploiters, L155 @b47f21597). Every privacy property in this chapter is stated against identity thieves and data aggregators, not against an adversary that can watch every link.

### Identity theft first {#identity-theft-first}

The ranking that follows inverts the usual privacy-first ordering [proposal]. Identity thieves monetize through asset theft and "do not monetize aggregated content data or metadata" (SPAC paper > Identity Theft: Threats and Mitigations, L177 @b47f21597); aggregators monetize metadata through advertising. The deck states the conclusion: "The internet is broken primarily because of identity theft." and "The infrastructure changes needed to fix identity theft will enable easier adoption of the changes needed to better protect against aggregators." (SPAC deck, slide 62 @b47f21597). That is an argument about adoption sequence, not a measurement, and the deck offers none.

The identity-theft half lands in authentication, not privacy. The attack the paper describes is recursive privilege escalation across connected trust domains, where "a given trust domain is only as strong as the weakest connected trust domain" (SPAC paper > Threats, L185 @b47f21597), and the mitigation is that "any step that has strong authentication with individually signed requests for authorization will stop the attack from proceeding" (SPAC paper > Mitigations, L195 @b47f21597). Signed requests are the subject of [chapter 09](09-kram-and-request-authentication.md#problem), and the bearer-token reframing of [chapter 07, section 5.2](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#bearer-tokens) is the same argument from the other side.

Where SPAC itself fits in the ranking is narrower than its name suggests [proposal]. The paper's summary is that its most fruitful use against metadata exploitation "may be in improving VPNs", while "protection from 2nd party aggregators may be best provided by higher layer protocols that provide contractually protected disclosure" (SPAC paper > Summary Threats and Mitigations, L256 @b47f21597). Of WireGuard it says "We should not reinvent that wheel." (SPAC paper > Effective Privacy, L109 @b47f21597). The paper then designs intermediated routing anyway, so the VPN remark endorses the model of a trusted delivery service over public roads; it is not advice to use a VPN and stop.

## 4. ESSR: authenticated encryption between identifiers {#essr}

### The building blocks {#building-blocks}

The SPAC baseline was chosen for adoptability: "only highly adoptable, well-proven, cryptographic operations from broadly supported libraries be used" (SPAC paper > Adoptability via Dumb Crypto, L323 @b47f21597), and "we only consider pre-quantum safe algorithms" (L327) [proposal]. Signing is libsodium Ed25519, chosen because it has strong unforgeability (SUF-CMA) where "the popular ECDSA signature scheme only has the weaker EUF-CMA" (SPAC paper > Best Authenticity, L331 @b47f21597). Encryption is the libsodium sealed box, an IND-CCA2 integrated encryption scheme with "the end-only viewability of asymmetric public key encryption" (SPAC paper > Best Confidentiality, L351 @b47f21597). These security properties are relayed from literature the paper cites and this chapter did not read; they are standard claims, but they are the paper's claims here.

The TSP specification has since reversed the baseline [spec]. HPKE-Base is mandatory: "TSP implementations MUST support HPKE-Base mode as defined in this document." (TSP spec > Hybrid Public Key Encryption (HPKE), L993 @5599b4556). The sealed box is optional and on notice: "implementors SHOULD consider migrating to the HPKE option specified in this document. We MAY remove this option in the future." (TSP spec > Libsodium Sealed Box, L1052 @5599b4556). The SPAC paper chose the sealed box because RFC 9180 implementations were "not yet broadly available" (SPAC paper > Best Confidentiality, L359 @b47f21597); that reason has aged and the specification acted on it. The specification also adds post-quantum algorithms the paper deliberately excluded: ML-DSA-65 signatures, and an ML-KEM-768 and X25519 hybrid KEM, where "Post-quantum support in TSP is not a separate mode" (TSP spec > HPKE PQ and PQ/T Algorithms, L1048 @5599b4556).

### Why encryption alone is not authenticity {#hpke-not-authenticity}

IND-CCA2 encryption says nothing about who encrypted [proposal]: "IND-CCA2, by itself, is insufficient to provide strong authenticity of the ciphertext." (SPAC paper > Best Confidentiality, L361 @b47f21597). The paper relays RFC 9180's own warning that all HPKE modes are "vulnerable to key-compromise impersonation attacks", and RFC 9180's remedy, "using a digital signature generated with the sender's private key over the HPKE container" (L361). The deck reduces it to a slide title, "HPKE is not Authenticity" (SPAC deck, slide 23 @b47f21597). This chapter relays RFC 9180 through the paper and did not read the RFC.

### Why encrypt-then-sign is not enough either {#encrypt-then-sign}

Signing the ciphertext defeats key-compromise impersonation but opens a different forgery [proposal]: "An attacker can strip the signature and resign the ciphertext with the attacker's key but has never seen the plaintext of the cipher." (SPAC paper > Best Combined Authenticity and Confidentiality, L366 @b47f21597). The fix binds both ends: the receiver's key under the signature, and "bind the sender's public key inside the ciphertext" (L366). That construction is ESSR, "Encrypt Sender's key and then Sign Receiver's key" (SPAC paper > Modified ESSR, L370 @b47f21597), from Jee Hea An's 2001 analysis (IACR ePrint 2001/079).

The property the extra binding buys is named in the paper's table of four unforgeability notions, third-party and receiver unforgeability of plaintext and of ciphertext: "Simply using the encrypt then sign approach provides TUF-PTXT, TUF-CTXT, and RUF-CTXT but not RUF-PTXT." (SPAC paper > Modified ESSR, L372 @b47f21597), and ESSR provides all four. In words: under encrypt-then-sign a malicious receiver may find another plaintext and key pair that produce the signed ciphertext, and claim the sender addressed that to it.

The TSP specification restates the result more carefully and independently of the paper's wording [spec]: "No naive composition of public-key encryption and signature achieves authenticated encryption in the public-key setting." The three naive orders "each fail at least one of these notions" (TSP spec > Cryptographic Algorithms, L957 @5599b4556). The paper's stronger statement in its appendix, "The ESSR framework basically tells us that there is really only one secure approach to mixing encryption with signatures." (SPAC paper > Appendix: Using Crypto in a way that is Too-Clever-By-Half, L1165 @b47f21597), is Sam's generalization, not An's result; An's paper analyses the generic compositions. Neither this chapter nor the mining note behind it read An's paper ([open question 2](#open-questions)), so the property table is relayed, twice, through authors who share an interest in ESSR.

### Identifiers in place of keys {#aids-not-keys}

KERI's modification is to bind identifiers rather than keys [proposal]: "We apply ESSR to KERI by modifying the basic ESSR approach to support unbounded-term identifiers by replacing the sender's long-term public key with its AID" (SPAC paper > Baseline KERI ESSR, L388 @b47f21597), and the receiver's likewise, with each party's current keys looked up from its key event log. The motivation is that a long-term key is not unbounded-term: NIST cryptoperiods force its abandonment after a year or two, and with it any reputation bound to it (SPAC paper > Unbounded-Term AIDs instead of Long-Term Public Keys, L266 @b47f21597). With an AID, stale-key use is detectable: "If the provided message is using stale keys for a given AID the associated cryptographic operations will fail against the current key state" (L274). The encryption key need not be managed separately: "with certain caveats, the encrypting public key may be cryptographically derived from the signing public key" (L272). keripy does exactly that ([section 11](#implementation)).

The specification adopts the substitution and states its benefit as a binding property [spec]: "TSP binds VIDs where ESSR binds public keys." A binding to a VID "survives rotation of the keys behind it, where a binding to a public key would not." (TSP spec > Cryptographic Algorithms, L959 @5599b4556). It also states the cost, which the paper does not. For the receiver's identity under the signature, "TSP provides no means to establish which key state was current when a signature was made" (TSP spec > Security and Privacy Considerations > Binding of identifiers, L1480 @5599b4556), so ESSR's argument, which assumes the receiver's key was fixed at signing time, has to be rebuilt. The specification rebuilds it by composition: a malicious receiver would need a plaintext and key pair reproducing a ciphertext that is itself bound to both identifiers, and "No scheme specified here admits a collision meeting these constraints." (L1482). That is an argument in the specification, not a proof cited from the literature, and it is the place a cryptographer reviewing this design should press.

For a KERI reader the cost is sharper than for TSP generally. KERI's answer to the question of which key state was current is anchoring, as [chapter 03, section 11](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#as-of-anchor) describes, and an ESSR message is not anchored in anyone's log. So the as-of-anchor defense against the retrograde attack does not reach an ESSR message: a receiver later in possession of a retired key is limited by the composition argument above, not by KERI's anchoring. The SPAC paper's stronger replay option, anchoring a digest of the message in the sender's KEL ([below](#essr-variants)), would restore it at the cost of a key event per message.

### Variants, and what each gives up {#essr-variants}

The paper treats every departure from full ESSR as a trade that must be stated [proposal]: "Any variation from the full ESSR policy may make a security trade-off induced by removing one of its protections." (SPAC paper > KERI ESSR Summary, L435 @b47f21597).

- Plaintext-sourceless. The sender's AID appears twice in full ESSR, in plaintext so that a firewall can verify the signature before decrypting, and inside the ciphertext. Dropping the plaintext copy trades upstream DDoS filtering and public verifiability for not exposing the sender: "DDOS or firewall protection (security) could be traded for not exposing the sender's AID (privacy)" (SPAC paper > Plaintext Sourceless Variant, L401 @b47f21597). Under a one-relationship-identifier policy, where "a given receiver AID, is only used for one given sender AID" (L407), the mapping can be shared upstream and public verifiability restored.
- Sourceless. Removing the sender from the ciphertext too, even under that policy, "still exposes the receiver to an attack when the approved sender is malicious" (SPAC paper > Sourceless Variant, L411 @b47f21597): the approved sender can sign ciphertext it never saw the plaintext of.
- Destinationless. The paper argues the receiver's AID can sometimes be dropped, because with AIDs "an attacker can't purport any plaintext to have been encrypted to a different key pair other than the one that appears in the receiver's KEL" (SPAC paper > Destinationless Variant, L421 @b47f21597). The argument then needs a malicious receiver to be unable to find a stale key and plaintext that reproduce a signed ciphertext, which the paper frames as a birthday-style problem and concludes only that such a receiver "may not gain any appreciable advantage" (L423). This is the weakest-supported claim in the ESSR material and should be treated as unproven ([open question 7](#open-questions)).

Replay is left open in the base construction [proposal]. The paper offers two mechanisms: a policy "that once the key state has been updated no messages signed with stale keys may be accepted", or anchoring a digest of the message in the sender's KEL (SPAC paper > Replay Attack Protection, L431 @b47f21597). The first does nothing against replay within one key state. The TSP specification places freshness above itself: an application that needs "ordering, delivery, or freshness of its own obtains them from the layer that understands what its messages mean" (TSP spec > Security and Privacy Considerations, L1473 @5599b4556). For KERI that layer is KRAM ([chapter 09](09-kram-and-request-authentication.md#problem)); Sam said as much in answer to a question at the 2026 conference, which is intent, not design, and KRAM's own normative status is empty ([chapter 09, section 5](09-kram-and-request-authentication.md#normative-status)).

### End-only viewability {#end-only-viewability}

The confidentiality principle is the dual of end-verifiability ([chapter 01, section 4](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#end-verifiability)) [proposal]: "If the edges (ends) are secure, then the security of the middle doesn't matter." (SPAC paper > End-Verifiabilty and End-Only-Viewability, L282 @b47f21597). Where ambient verifiability lets anyone verify, end-only viewability means confidential data "is only viewable one-where, one-time by one-other-body" (L297). The two overlays are not independent: the authenticity overlay has to underwrite the confidentiality overlay, because "the encryption key state must be securely attributable to the intended identifier for confidentiality to work" (TSP deck, slide 15 @b47f21597). Encrypting to a key you cannot attribute is encrypting to whoever substituted it.

One property in the paper's list has no counterpart elsewhere in the corpus: first-party non-viewability. Because the sealed box's sender cannot decrypt what it sealed, a party can encrypt to the destination, or to an isolated part of itself, and delete the plaintext. "This is the zero-trust principle of encrypted-at-rest, not merely encrypted-in-motion." (SPAC paper > Appendix: 1st Party Non-Viewability, L1175 @b47f21597). A distributed first party "can enforce on itself a policy of end-part-only viewability by using PKE" (L1177). This is the mechanism behind [chapter 07, section 5.5](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#secure-pipe)'s line that secure pipes protect data in motion but not at rest.

### DDoS efficiency constrains nesting {#ddos-efficiency}

The paper makes the cost of dropping a hostile packet a first-class constraint on how signatures and encryption may nest [proposal]. "Any protocol that requires decryption in order to make a drop decision can only make that drop decision at the edge host" (SPAC paper > DDOS Protection Efficiency, L1135 @b47f21597), because only the edge host holds the decryption key, and "multiplying nesting layers directly impacts DDOS efficiency" (L1133). Hence the outer signature over plaintext in full ESSR: "if no decrypt must be done in order to drop (as in at end-only-viewability) then we can have very efficient DDOS protection" (L1137). The same appendix argues that mix networks degrade drop efficiency and are open to sleeper attacks, and gives the JSON-LD canonicalization amplification attack as an example of a protocol whose drop decision is expensive (SPAC paper > Security-by-obscurity and Amplification Attacks, L1145-L1152 @b47f21597). These are Sam's engineering judgments; the factor-of-ten estimates for firmware, software and host filtering in the same appendix are illustrative, not measured.

The constraint has a direct consequence for privacy. Every additional nested layer that a relay must decrypt before it can decide to drop costs it efficiency, so the nesting of [section 6](#nesting-routing) is not free, and its depth is a security trade, not only a privacy gain.

## 5. Relationships, contexts and partitions {#contexts}

### The formal definitions {#context-definitions}

The formal core of the SPAC privacy model is a list of definitions, identical in the paper and the deck [proposal] (SPAC paper > Relationships > Basics, L471-L485 @b47f21597).

- "A cryptonym is a cryptographically derived pseudonym with at least 128 bits of entropy in the derivation." (L471)
- "A relationship is a pairing of two AIDs, one each from a different controller." (L473) A relationship may be bidirectional or one-way, so "for any two AIDs there are three different relationships" (L442).
- "Two different AIDs are by themselves uncorrelated in an information-theoretic security sense" (L474).
- "A context is a set of events." (L475)
- "A partition is a set of contexts with mutually disjoint relationships." (L478)
- Members of a partition "may be correlatable due to other information associated with any pair of contexts" (L480), though the partitioned relationships by themselves are not.
- Within a context, correlation is wanted: "The secure attributability of an AID, together with its perfect correlatability within a context, enables secure reputational trust" (L482).

The TSP specification carries the identifier half of this almost word for word [spec]: VIDs must be "cryptographically non-correlatable in an information-theoretic security context" (TSP spec > Cryptographic Non-Correlation, L152 @5599b4556).

### The context is the correlator {#context-correlates}

The doctrinal consequence is that a high-entropy identifier is not a supercookie; the context in which it is used is what an observer correlates. The paper states the information-theoretic half (L474, above). Sam put the other half as a slogan at the 2026 conference: "It is the context that is the supercorrelator not the identifiers." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, SPAC & TSP, 14:00), and, as advice, "Think context when you're worried about correlation. Don't think identifiers." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, SPAC & TSP, 15:46). These are captions, cited for framing; the written support is the paper's L474 and L480.

This reframes a common critique. A reviewer who objects that an AID is a stable correlator is right within a context and wrong as a general claim, and the design wants the first: "We want perfect correlation within a context so that we have accountability." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, SPAC & TSP, 16:27). The same reasoning underlies the editor's ruling that participation in an exchange is not correlation ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#participation-not-correlation)) and [chapter 01](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#durable-correlator)'s observation that a signed presentation is a durable correlator: inside its context it is supposed to be. The design question is never whether an identifier correlates, but which contexts share it.

The boundary of the claim is in L480: contexts can be correlated by everything else about them, timing, size, network address, behavior. The partition removes the identifier as a correlator and nothing else. [Section 10](#tsp-limits) gives the specification's list of what survives.

### Out-of-band authentication and cheap pseudonymity {#ooba}

Every secure protocol needs at least one factor outside the channel [proposal]. Any secure protocol "requires at least one out-of-band-authentication (OOBA) factor in order to protect against a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack" (SPAC paper > OOB Setup, L573 @b47f21597). The same factor answers cheap pseudonymity, the ability to mint a fresh identifier to slip past a filter: "Requiring an OOBA factor for any given identifier makes that identifier relatively expensive." (L577). And it need not cost privacy, because the out-of-band step is not visible on the in-band channel (L579).

This agrees with [chapter 07, section 2.6](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#multiple-well-knowns): out-of-band authentication raises the cost of a new identifier and protects the bootstrap from a man in the middle; it does not create a cryptographic binding the AID lacked.

### Two meanings of OOBI {#oobi-overload}

The paper uses the term OOBI in a sense wider than KERI's. An existing relationship can introduce a new one, acting as "a 3rd party reference or reputable letter of introduction" (SPAC paper > Relationship Formation Protocol, L637 @b47f21597), and "We call this an Out-Of-Band-Introduction (OOBI) because the reputable relationship authentication was established Out-Of-Band with respect to the new relationship." (L651). In KERI proper, an OOBI is a discovery URL that carries no trust at all ([chapter 01, section 4](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#discovery-vs-trust)). The SPAC OOBI is a reputable introduction inside an already authenticated relationship. A reviewer who reads the term in the SPAC papers as KERI's untrusted discovery hint will misjudge relationship formation as trusting unverified data, and one who reads KERI's OOBI as the SPAC introduction will over-trust a URL. The TSP specification avoids the collision by saying that an introduction "conveys a VID and nothing more" until verified (TSP spec > Security and Privacy Considerations > Introductions, L1564 @5599b4556).

## 6. Nesting and routing {#nesting-routing}

### Five steps from direct to private {#five-steps}

The paper builds its protocol in five steps, each ending in a one-line verdict on the deck, and the verdicts are the summary [proposal].

1. Direct. ESSR between two AIDs, with IP addresses in the clear. "Strongly Authentic and Confidential but not Private" (SPAC deck, slide 36 @b47f21597).
2. Direct protocol as wrapper. A business relationship is carried inside the ciphertext of a communication relationship, so "No 3rd party observer can view that there is a relationship" (SPAC paper > Direct Protocol as Wrapper, L630 @b47f21597). "Strongly Authentic and Confidential and Somewhat Private" (SPAC deck, slide 37 @b47f21597). The paper names the two kinds of context here: a hop-wise communication context and an end-wise interaction context.
3. Relationship formation. An existing relationship introduces a new one ([section 5](#oobi-overload)), so a new context needs no new out-of-band ceremony.
4. One shared intermediary. Third parties see each end talking to the intermediary and nothing more. The intermediary sees which AIDs relate but never the content, and the paper says those AIDs "are never stored on disk" by it; "They are only ever in memory." (SPAC paper > One Shared Intermediary, L699 @b47f21597). "Strongly Authentic and Confidential and Mostly Private" (SPAC deck, slide 40 @b47f21597). The stated limits are that both ends must trust the one intermediary, and its network provider can correlate timing and size.
5. Non-shared intermediaries, one trusted by each end. Neither end learns the other's intermediary relationship, so "An attacker has to get that by successfully attacking both C and D." (SPAC paper > Discussion, L792 @b47f21597). Routing tables are signed at rest, and "This approach is fully zero-trust" (L796) in the sense that a compromised table cannot redirect traffic undetectably. "Strongly Authentic and Confidential and Very Private" (SPAC deck, slide 42 @b47f21597).

A last step nests a third relationship inside the end-wise routed one, which "makes the existence of this new end-wise relationship (A3, <>, B3) hidden from not just 3rd parties but also the 2nd party trusted intermediaries" (SPAC paper > Cross Relationship Context Interaction Contexts, L865 @b47f21597; subscripts flattened). The paper names the result "a three-level nested communication protocol" (L873), and the deck's verdict drops the qualifier: "Strongly Authentic and Confidential and Private" (SPAC deck, slide 44 @b47f21597). That unqualified verdict is the one claim in the sequence a reviewer should not accept as stated. It holds against identifier correlation by third parties and intermediaries; it does not hold against timing and size analysis by an observer of more than one hop, which the paper's own step-4 limits admit and the specification's limits section confirms ([section 10](#tsp-limits)).

### Routing split between two halves {#dead-drop}

The non-shared design splits the route so that no single party holds it [proposal]: "The non-shared intermediary protocols above use a combination of both source vector routing and table routing." (SPAC paper > Combined Source Vector and Table Routing as Dead Drop, L1045 @b47f21597). The sender carries only part of the route; the rest is filled in from the far intermediary's table, so the far-side intermediary acts as "a dead drop controlled by the far-side destination" (L1049). The paper's security argument leans on the vector part being only in memory, which it says raises the attack from disk access to code-supply-chain compromise. That is an operational property of an intermediary, and the specification records it as one ([section 10](#tsp-limits)).

### Interaction identifiers {#interaction-identifiers}

Interaction identifiers get the same treatment as AIDs [proposal]. Using the SAID of a nonce-salted first message as the interaction identifier makes it "cryptographically universally unique to a given interaction" (SPAC paper > iSAIDs as IIDs, L813 @b47f21597), and the salt prevents recovering the message from its SAID by rainbow table. Putting the identifier inside the ciphertext makes the interaction as private as the relationship that carries it: "if the relationship context is private, then any of its interaction contexts are also private (QED)" (SPAC paper > Privacy by Isolating Interaction Contexts, L827 @b47f21597). The "QED" is the paper's; the inference is sound given the definitions, and its premise, a private relationship context, is the thing [section 10](#tsp-limits) qualifies. Chained identifiers, each appearing in only two messages, are preferred to a repeated one: "the latter form is more secure" (L832).

### Multisig is a sketch {#multisig-sketch}

For a multi-key receiver, "each key in a multi-sig provides a unique encryption destination", so the ciphertext "must appear as a list of ciphertexts" (SPAC paper > Multi-sig Extension, L1057 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Nothing else specifies this, and keripy encrypts to the first key only ([section 11](#implementation)). For a design that relies on multisig AIDs ([chapter 03, section 7](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#multisig)), confidential messaging to a group is an open area, not a solved one.

## 7. Sustainable privacy {#sustainable-privacy}

### The time value of information {#time-value}

Sam's answer to the objection that everything leaks eventually is economic, not cryptographic [proposal]. "In general, privacy dissipates over time." (SPAC paper > Time Value of Information, L1064 @b47f21597). But the value of correlated information to an aggregator decays as well, because what it buys is prediction, so there is "a sweet spot for sustainable privacy protection that is governed by the time constant of the time value of exploitable correlation" (L1066). Partitioned contexts make the clock resettable: "Once a context has become leaky, however, a new isolated context can be created that restarts the clock on time value correlatability." (L1068). The 2026 Sustainable Privacy paper names TSP as the mechanism: "The TSP protocol enables control over the exploitable time window of the correlatable information that can be leaked from a given context." (Sustainable Privacy > 3.3, p. 9 @b47f21597).

The argument is qualitative. No source estimates a time constant for any market, so the paper's conclusion that protected information is "sustainably protectable indefinitely" (L1068) is a conditional whose condition has not been measured ([open question 3](#open-questions)). A reviewer may fairly ask for the number; the authors have not supplied it.

### Covert, not clandestine {#covert-clandestine}

The analogy Sam leans on most in 2026 originates in the SPAC paper [proposal]. A covert operative has a public cover identity and can survive long-term; a clandestine one has none, and any leak exposes the whole operation, so "a given clandestine operation is unsustainable long-term." (SPAC paper > Sustainable Management of Contexts, L1084 @b47f21597). Internet privacy tools "have tended to look like clandestine operations where the whole operation must be concealed" (L1086), which makes them fragile. The prescription is to nest: long-term generic communication and routed contexts are covert, and they "provide nested covers to the really valuable private short-term interaction contexts (clandestine)" (L1088). The deck compresses it to "Use Covert to hide in plain sight." and "Nest clandestine contexts inside covert contexts" (SPAC deck, slide 51 @b47f21597).

The covert model has a herd requirement that is easy to miss. If an intermediary's users use it only for what they want hidden, they "make the mere use of C itself a correlatable signal" (L1086). A covert intermediary is only as good as its legitimate traffic, which is a deployment property no protocol can supply.

### The contextual linkability attack {#contextual-linkability}

The SPAC paper introduced, and the Sustainable Privacy paper develops, a named attack on selective disclosure [proposal]: the use of "a set of non-selectively disclosed attributes obtained from the context of the disclosure that is sufficient to re-identify the discloser" (SPAC paper > Contextual Linkability Re-identification Attack, L1101 @b47f21597). The verifier controls the presentation context and can enrich it with location, camera footage, payment data or network addresses. Sam's framing: "Correctly understood, selective disclosure is a naive form of K-anonymity performed by the discloser (presenter)." (L1104). The 2025 deck pushes it to "Therefore, counter-intuitively, selective disclosure increases the traceability of individuals" (Seven Privacies deck, slide 22 @b47f21597), on the reasoning that each disclosure adds the act of disclosing to an aggregator's record. The deck does not quantify that, and it is Sam's assessment.

The legal half of the claim has been corrected by its author, and a reviewer should cite the later form. The 2023 paper says "the receiver of bare, selectively disclosed data must treat it as personal data or may be in violation of GDPR" (SPAC paper > Contextual Linkability Re-identification Attack, L1104 @b47f21597), which reads as a statement of current law. The 2026 paper makes it normative: selective disclosure "should not provide a safe harbor", and "Unfortunately, current regulatory policy does not respect this distinction." (Sustainable Privacy > 2.3, p. 5 @b47f21597).

The 2026 paper adds two arguments the 2023 one lacks [proposal]. First, unlinkable zero-knowledge disclosure has a security cost, not only a weak privacy benefit: if the issuer's signing key is compromised, "This creates a largely undetectable impersonation attack on the issuer." (Sustainable Privacy > 2.4, p. 7 @b47f21597). That is the detectability argument [chapter 07, section 10.1](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#bundled-credentials-detectability) carries from discussion #1098. The paper adds a cryptoperiod argument the bible did not have: because an issuer's key must be rotated within a year or two, "at most, a bare ZKP VC can be used only for applications with a VC lifespan of no more than two years" (Sustainable Privacy > 2.4, p. 7 @b47f21597), where an ACDC anchored in a KEL survives rotation ([chapter 05, section 1](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#what-an-acdc-is)). Second, not all de-identification is broken: for data aggregated from many first parties, "The only allowable de-identification approach is either a differential privacy or a synthetic data privacy-based mechanism." (Sustainable Privacy > 1.2, p. 3 @b47f21597). For non-aggregated data the answer stays contractual.

### Accountability as the remedy {#accountability}

The constructive half of sustainable privacy is legal, and it is where the ToIP definition of privacy set aside in [section 2](#who-versus-what) returns [proposal]. For data shared with a second party, "the only sustainable privacy protection mechanism" is "some form of contractually protected disclosure" (Sustainable Privacy > 3.1, p. 8 @b47f21597). The 2023 version of that sentence named only chain-link confidentiality; the 2026 version adds the duty of data loyalty, defined after Hartzog and Richards: "The 2nd party data holder must act as a loyal agent of the 1st party." (Sustainable Privacy > 4, p. 10 @b47f21597). Consent is not the mechanism, because "the act of consent can be easily socially engineered against the best interests of the consenter" (same page).

Accountability rather than hiding is the 2026 thesis, and its sharpest form is a charge against unlinkability: "Cryptographic un-linkability defeats legal, contractual, and economic accountability" (Seven Privacies deck, slide 26 @b47f21597). At the 2026 conference Sam answered a question about Signal-style deniability in the same terms, relaying research he attributed to a BYU group: "People want accountability, not deniability." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, SPAC & TSP, 18:23), while naming voting as a case that needs a different protocol. No written source in the Papers repository carries the deniability position; it is intent, from a caption.

This is consistent with [chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#contractual-layer) and sharper than it. Chapter 06 presents the contractual layer as one of three. Sam's 2026 position is that without it the other two cannot hold for shared data, and the editor's ruling on bulk issuance ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#decorrelation)) uses the technical layer to make the contractual layer enforceable. The mechanisms by which ACDC carries the contract are in [chapter 05, section 8](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#contractual-disclosure) and IPEX's agree and admit ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#agree-binds)). [Chapter 10](10-presentation-registries.md#asymmetry)'s presentation registry is the same move applied to an issuee's own credentials: correlation inside a context the holder controls, so that misuse is evident.

The critique a reviewer should bring is the weakness the doctrine already concedes in another form ([chapter 07, section 14](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#residuals)): accountability rests on courts, contracts and regulators that the protocol does not supply, varies by jurisdiction, and is enforceable only against a second party that can be found. Against an anonymous third party the contractual remedy is empty, which is exactly why the cryptographic and routing layers of sections [4](#essr) to [6](#nesting-routing) exist.

## 8. Bulk-issuance privacy in the 2026 decks {#bulk-issuance-privacy}

The two April 2026 IIW decks connect bulk issuance to the privacy model of this chapter, and they are the newest and least developed material in it [proposal]. The mechanics of bulk issuance, what landed in the ACDC specification and what is still open, are in [chapter 05, section 9](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#bulk-issuance); what follows is only what the decks add.

- Per-member keys. "Each member of a bulk-issued set has a unique AID with unique keys derived using an HDKey algorithm" (Bulk privacy deck, slide 18 @b47f21597), so each member can live in a separate context in the sense of [section 5](#contexts).
- Herd privacy for registry updates. "Placeholder updates provide herd privacy wrt correlation to date time of actual state changes" (Bulk privacy deck, slide 23 @b47f21597); an issuer "could randomly issue update events that do not change state, across all its registries" (slide 21). This is the registry-level form of [section 7](#covert-clandestine)'s covert traffic, and of [chapter 05, section 9](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#bulk-updates)'s bulk updates.
- A predicate proof that preserves detectability. The comparison slide states the trade directly: a ZKP has "relatively large computation, defeats detectability of issuer key compromise"; a sparse Merkle tree has "relatively large storage, relatively small computation, enables detectability of issuer key compromise" (Bulk privacy deck, slide 31 @b47f21597). This is the deck form of the proposal in [chapter 05](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#age-smt-proposal).
- An identity-assurance step ahead of issuance. An in-person session binding assurance to a cryptographic identity "enables dynamic sizing of SEDI bulk issuance sets" (Bulk privacy deck, slide 17 @b47f21597). The SEDI architecture this belongs to is in [chapter 06, section 3](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#sedi-architecture).

The talks summarize KERI's privacy stance as RAP-C3, which the 2026 bulk-issuance deck's last slide spells out in damaged extracted text as reputable autonomic pseudonymity with control over confidential contexts (Bulk privacy deck, slide 42 @b47f21597). The expansion is this chapter's reading of that slide and is not quoted.

## 9. The wire {#wire}

### The SPAC and TSP envelope in CESR {#cesr-envelope}

Both the SPAC paper's CESR appendix (added 2025-04-21) and a separate TSP CESR paper specify the same wrapper [proposal]. SPAC is "a tunneling routing protocol that is meant to be transparent to any other message protocol that is tunneled as a payload" (SPAC paper > Appendix: CESR Implementation, L1187 @b47f21597). Its native fields are fixed-field CESR, and "all tunneled payload messages (i.e. non-native SPAC payloads) are encoded as sniffable CESR streams" (L1191), so a KERI or ACDC message can ride inside unchanged.

The shape is head, body and tail. The head is plaintext and uniform by design, "so that the head contains no correlatable metadata besides the source and destination AIDs" (SPAC paper > Head, L1207 @b47f21597). The wrapper count code is `-E##` (or `--E#####` for large wrappers) and the payload group is `-Z##` (or `--Z#####`). The body is ciphertext or a plaintext payload group, and a parser tells them apart by the first character: a `-` begins a group, anything else a cipher primitive (SPAC paper > Determining if the payload field value is a ciphertext CESR primitive or a plaintext CESR group, L1549 @b47f21597). The native payload types are `CTL`, `HOP`, `RFI`, `RFA`, `RFD`, `PAD` and `SCS` (SPAC paper > SPAC Payload Types, L1246-L1254 @b47f21597). Every control payload carries a pad field "to pad the total packet size to some desired minimum length" (SPAC paper > Padding Control Message Payloads, L1265 @b47f21597), and the `PAD` type exists to send padding packets when there is nothing to say. Relationship formation needs no replay protection because "relationship formations are treated as idempotent actions" (SPAC paper > Replay Attack, L1436 @b47f21597). The CESR mechanics these rest on, count codes and sniffable streams, are in [chapter 04, section 7](04-cesr-and-the-wire.md#count-codes) and [chapter 04, section 10](04-cesr-and-the-wire.md#cold-start).

### What is in the CESR specification {#cesr-codes}

The codes are normative in CESR [spec]. The master table carries the wrapper on both branches: "| `-E##` | ESSR wrapper signable up to 4,095 quadlets/triplets |" (CESR spec > Universal Code table genus/version codes that do not allow genus/version override, L842 @bad6edd84; L850 @65fd7518b). The payload group is named for TSP: "ESSR (TSP) Payload `version+messagtype+...` up to 4,095 quadlets/triplets" (CESR spec > Master code table for genus/version `-_AAACAA` (KERI/ACDC protocol stack Version 2.00), L939 @bad6edd84; L947 @65fd7518b; "messagtype" sic). The X25519 sealed-box cipher codes (`4C` to `9AAE`) and the HPKE-Base cipher codes (`4F` to `9AAF`) are in the same table (L1028-L1051 @bad6edd84).

### Where the papers and the specifications diverge {#wire-divergences}

The sources disagree in ways an implementer must not paper over.

- Identifier encoding. The TSP CESR paper has open and closed modes; in open mode "all VIDs must be either URNs or DIDs encoded as variable-length CESR UTF-8 byte strings" (TSP CESR paper > VID fields in Open Mode, L17 @b47f21597), and in closed mode a KERI AID is used bare. The SPAC paper removed the modes on 2025-04-21 and uses bare AIDs throughout. The TSP specification has no closed mode and a syntax rule that conflicts with its own KERI example ([section 10](#tsp-vid-syntax)).
- The version field. The SPAC paper uses a ten-character field with an explicit genus version, `0OSPACAABCAA`; the TSP CESR paper uses `YTSP_AAB`, where "The CESR Genus table version is implied." (TSP CESR paper > TSP ESSR Wrapper, L102 @b47f21597); the TSP specification uses `YTSP-AAC`, version 0.2, with a hyphen (TSP spec > TSP Envelope Encoding, L1130 @5599b4556). The SPAC paper's own hop examples still show the pre-June-2025 forms `YSPACABA` and `YSPACAAB`.
- The hop-list code. The SPAC paper's prose says "The hop list group code is `-K##`." (SPAC paper > Hop Payload, L1377 @b47f21597), while its own example tables use `-J##` and use `-K##` for the indexed-signature group in the tail. The TSP paper's and the specification's examples agree with `-J##`. The prose is a defect in the paper ([open question 5](#open-questions)).
- The `4F` code. The CESR specification describes it as "HPKE Base cipher bytes of QB2 plaintext" (CESR spec > Master code table for genus/version `-_AAACAA` (KERI/ACDC protocol stack Version 2.00), L1046 @bad6edd84), while the SPAC paper's code table describes it as "HPKE Base cipher bytes of sniffable stream plaintext" (SPAC paper > Ciphertext Body, L1316 @b47f21597). Those are different payloads under one code, and which the TSP specification means is not stated ([open question 6](#open-questions)). By [chapter 04, section 12](04-cesr-and-the-wire.md#interop-risks)'s rule, this is a finding, not a wording nit.

## 10. What the TSP specification settles {#tsp-spec}

### Shape and status {#tsp-shape}

The TSP specification is the only normative text for most of this chapter, with the status caveats of [Status](#status) [spec]. Its wire version is 0.2: "The current experimental draft's version is `0.2`." (TSP spec > TSP Envelope, L245 @5599b4556). It describes itself as "the spanning layer protocol within the Trust over IP technology architecture" (TSP spec > Overview, L52 @5599b4556). It is identifier-agnostic: a KERI AID is one example of a suitable VID, alongside `did:webs`, `did:webvh` and `did:peer` (TSP spec > Examples, L206 @5599b4556), and it requires of public VIDs what KERI provides natively: "The authority to rotate a VID's keys MUST be separate from the VID's signing key" (TSP spec > VID General Requirements, L144 @5599b4556). It defines no rotation of its own, because "the resolution and verification of key state is VID type specific" (TSP spec > Handling Changes, L196 @5599b4556), and its account of recovery limits names pre-commitment to next keys, delegation, and first-seen recording with conflict treated as evidence, which are KERI's three defenses, without naming KERI (TSP spec > Security and Privacy Considerations > Limits, L1511 @5599b4556).

Metadata privacy comes from exactly the two SPAC mechanisms: "The metadata privacy protections are achieved by nested TSP messages and routed messages through intermediaries." (TSP spec > Authenticity, Confidentiality, and Metadata Privacy, L107 @5599b4556). Inner VIDs in nested relationships "do not require same level of verification as public VIDs, and do not require transport layer address resolution of their own" (TSP spec > Nested Relationships, L418 @5599b4556; source italics dropped).

### ESSR with HPKE-Base {#tsp-hpke}

Under HPKE-Base, the sender-in-ciphertext half of ESSR moves into the associated data [spec]: "the binding of the ciphertext to the sender identity required by the ESSR construction is provided by the associated data `aad`" (TSP spec > HPKE-Base Mode, L1010 @5599b4556), which includes the sender's VID from the envelope. The in-payload sender field becomes optional: "In HPKE-Base mode the VID_sndr confidential field is NULL VID by default." (L1041). Under the sealed box it remains mandatory, "as required by [[ref:ESSR]]" (TSP spec > Sealed Box, L1102 @5599b4556). Whether binding through associated data gives receiver unforgeability of plaintext in An's sense is the specification's argument, made in its binding section ([section 4](#aids-not-keys)), and it offers a fallback: an implementation that does not wish to rely on "the associated-data handling of a newer HPKE implementation obtains the same property from the payload check alone" (TSP spec > Binding of identifiers, L1482 @5599b4556). This is a refinement of SPAC's ESSR, not a contradiction of it.

### Routing and relationship forming {#tsp-routing}

Routed mode follows SPAC's non-shared-intermediary design [spec]. "A typical three hop pattern of TSP routed messages will traverse from source endpoint `A` to its intermediary `P`" (TSP spec > Metadata Privacy in Routed Mode, L464 @5599b4556), then to the destination's intermediary and the destination. Three kinds of relationship are named: direct neighbor, endpoint-to-endpoint, and "Nested private endpoint-to-endpoint relationship" (L462). The last hop is identified by a VID the destination chose, so that "an intermediary cannot oblige its clients to re-establish their routing information with every correspondent" (L469) when it changes its own VIDs. That settles the SPAC paper's open variant, labeled "Proposed Variant for Discussion" (SPAC paper > Hop Payload Encrypted to Final Destination (Proposed Variant for Discussion), L1392 @b47f21597), which would have encrypted the inner message to the final destination: the specification chose a destination-chosen exit VID instead.

Relationship forming became mandatory before application traffic, which SPAC did not require. "It is not permissible that one endpoint which has learned a VID of the other simply starts with an application level message" (TSP spec > Direct Relationship Forming, L714 @5599b4556) without exchanging control messages first. The most private reply to an unwanted invitation is none: "The most private option is to remain silent." (L734).

### What TSP says it does not do {#tsp-limits}

The specification is more candid than the papers about limits, and these sentences belong in any review [spec].

- No forward secrecy. "TSP does not provide forward secrecy: an adversary that has recorded earlier messages and later obtains the decryption key can read them." (TSP spec > Security and Privacy Considerations > Limits, L1515 @5599b4556). The cost follows from a design choice the SPAC paper presents as a simplification, that with unbounded-term identifiers many protocols can drop repeated setups using ephemeral keys (SPAC paper > Unbounded-Term AIDs instead of Long-Term Public Keys, L276 @b47f21597): rotation protects the future, not the recorded past.
- Metadata privacy is not cryptographic. "The properties that require no judgement about an operator are authenticity and confidentiality, which are cryptographic." The next sentence: "Metadata privacy and availability in routed mode are not among them." (TSP spec > What An Intermediary Is Trusted For, L1558 @5599b4556).
- Timing, size and frequency survive everything. "Timing, size, and frequency survive encryption, nesting, and routing alike." "TSP itself does not batch, reorder, or delay messages to frustrate this" (TSP spec > What Remains Observable, L1527 @5599b4556). At the 2026 conference Sam described good intermediaries as maintaining uniform traffic across their fan-out (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, SPAC & TSP, 35:45); in the specification that is an arrangement among intermediaries, not a protocol property, and an endpoint "needs to know what its intermediaries actually do" (L1527).
- Non-retention is a promise. Not storing endpoint-to-endpoint VIDs "is not enforced by any mechanism in TSP; it is a commitment by the operator." (TSP spec > What An Intermediary Is Trusted For, L1554 @5599b4556). The SPAC paper's "only ever in memory" (SPAC paper > One Shared Intermediary, L699 @b47f21597; [section 6](#five-steps)) is, in the specification, the operator's word.
- Intermediaries can drop. An intermediary that declines to relay "cannot be distinguished by the sender from one that has not yet delivered it" (TSP spec > What An Intermediary Is Trusted For, L1556 @5599b4556). Sam's conference answer is the same: if two intermediaries collude, "the only thing that they can do is DDoS this route" (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, SPAC & TSP, 37:33), with redundant routes as the remedy.
- A verified VID is not an identity. "A verified VID is an identifier that can be relied upon consistently, not an identity." (TSP spec > Introductions, L1566 @5599b4556). That is [chapter 07, section 9](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#semantic-tells)'s authenticity-veracity line restated for channels.

These limits are where a reviewer's findings about TSP-based designs belong. A design that claims metadata privacy without naming its intermediaries, their fan-out and their padding has claimed a property the specification says it cannot deliver alone.

### The VID syntax conflict {#tsp-vid-syntax}

Identifier syntax is the sharpest friction between TSP and KERI usage [spec]. The specification requires "that the VID format be either compliant DID format [[ref:DID]] or compliant URN format [[ref:RFC8141]]" (TSP spec > VID Syntax, L158 @5599b4556). Its own example says of a KERI AID: "An AID is not a DID; it is used directly" (TSP spec > Examples, L206 @5599b4556). Read together, a bare AID in an envelope violates the syntax rule unless it is carried as a URN or as `did:webs`. The TSP CESR paper anticipated this with a closed mode that the specification does not have. Sam co-authors both, so this is a question for him rather than a contest between authorities ([open question 4](#open-questions)).

## 11. What is implemented {#implementation}

### keripy: one ESSR send path {#keripy-essr}

keripy at `927c0cb87` has a `keri.spac` package containing one primitive, the payload-type tag [code]: `PayloadTypes = PayloadTypage(HOP='HOP', RFI='RFI', RFA='RFA', RFD='RFD', SCS='SCS')` (keripy spac/payloading.py, module level, L15 @927c0cb87). It omits `CTL` and `PAD`, which both papers and the specification define. The same change added an ESSR option to `StreamPoster`, the forwarder that delivers events to a recipient's mailbox. With it enabled, the plaintext is a SPAC `SCS` payload: the tag, the sender's AID, and a pad that is always empty, under the comment "bext field can be randomized to reduce correlation based on packet size, empty for now" (keripy app/forwarding.py, StreamPoster._chunk, L317 @927c0cb87). The slot for padding exists and is never filled.

`_essrWrapper` performs the ESSR [code] (keripy app/forwarding.py, StreamPoster._essrWrapper, L408-L430 @927c0cb87). It derives the receiver's X25519 key from the receiver's first current signing key, as [section 4](#aids-not-keys) permits; seals the plaintext with the libsodium sealed box; and signs an outer `exn` on route `/essr/req` whose modifiers name both ends, `modifiers=dict(src=hab.pre, dest=ctrl)`, and whose body carries a Blake3 digest of the ciphertext. The signature therefore covers the destination AID and the ciphertext, and the sender AID is inside the ciphertext. That is ESSR in shape.

Four boundaries apply to that [code], each checked at the pin.

1. It is not the SPAC or TSP wire format. The outer wrapper is a KERI v1 `exn`, not the `-E##` wrapper with a protocol version field. keripy's codex defines `ESSRWrapperGroup: str = '-E'` (keripy core/counting.py, L210 @927c0cb87), but the forwarder does not use it.
2. Multi-key receivers are not handled. Only the first verifier is used, so a multisig receiver gets one ciphertext to one key, not the per-key list of [section 6](#multisig-sketch).
3. There is no receive handler. `/essr/req` occurs in `src/` only at the sender. The `Exchanger` checks that an attached ciphertext matches the signed digest, raising "essr diger={diger.qb64} is invalid against content" (keripy peer/exchanging.py, Exchanger.processEvent, L337 @927c0cb87) otherwise, and `Manager.decrypt` (keripy app/keeping.py, Manager.decrypt, L1431 @927c0cb87) can open a sealed box with a signing-key-derived key, but nothing routes `/essr/req` to decrypt and dispatch.
4. No routing. There is no hop processing, no relationship forming (the formation types exist only as tag values), no padding message and no HPKE.

So keripy implements direct-mode ESSR from a sender to a recipient's mailbox, with SPAC's payload framing inside the ciphertext, and none of the metadata-privacy machinery of sections [5](#contexts) and [6](#nesting-routing). The keripy pin here, `927c0cb87`, is later than the `80f77b73b` pin of [chapter 10](10-presentation-registries.md#status), because that is the commit at which this code was read.

### Deployments, by status only {#deployments}

Speakers at the 2026 conference reported ESSR in use beyond keripy, and none of it was verified against code for this chapter [practice]. Veridian's fork of KERIA was said to have tunneled client-to-agent traffic through ESSR for over a year, with agent-to-agent CESR streams in basic TSP form and the KERIA part not yet upstream (Fergal O'Connor, Signify Security Architecture). A gateway for healthKERI was said to have wrapped each transaction in ESSR and not been adopted for lack of interoperability, and Phil Feairheller described ESSR as protecting plugin-to-service traffic. These are status claims from caption summaries in `raw/14` and `raw/17`; the KERIA pull request named in the talks was not located ([open question 8](#open-questions)).

### A proposed use for TSP inside KERI {#tsp-in-keri}

One in-window proposal uses TSP as the channel for confidential registry state [proposal]. In a discussion on blinded state, Sam suggests that an authorizer could answer a signed query from a downstream authorizee by "attaching the unblinded state to a TSP protected response" (keripy GitHub > #1082, body created 2025-08-17 and last edited 2026-04-27, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1082). That is the clearest written statement in the corpus of where TSP is meant to sit in the KERI stack: beside ACDC and KRAM, carrying the second-party disclosures that blinded state keeps from third parties ([chapter 05, section 9](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#blindable-state)).

## 12. Open questions {#open-questions}

1. Which form of the PAC trilemma is current, the one bounded at the highest level (2021, 2023) or the one bounded to the same degree (2021, 2025)? They make different claims ([section 1](#pac-trilemma)).
2. An's paper (IACR ePrint 2001/079) was not read. The four-property table is relayed through Sam and through the TSP specification, both interested parties. Someone should read it before the bible states the table as established ([section 4](#encrypt-then-sign)).
3. The time-value argument has no numbers. No source estimates a correlation time constant for any market ([section 7](#time-value)).
4. Does the TSP specification require a bare KERI AID to be wrapped as a URN or a DID? Its syntax rule and its KERI example point different ways ([section 10](#tsp-vid-syntax)).
5. The SPAC paper's `-K##` hop-list code contradicts its own tables and the specification. Probably a typo ([section 9](#wire-divergences)).
6. Does `4F` carry QB2 plaintext (CESR specification) or a sniffable stream (papers)? ([section 9](#wire-divergences)).
7. Is the destinationless ESSR variant sound? The birthday-style argument is sketched, not made ([section 4](#essr-variants)).
8. Where is the ESSR receive path? Until the KERIA change named in the talks is read, ESSR is implemented, in code anyone can inspect, on the send side only ([section 11](#keripy-essr)).
9. What are the seven privacies of the Seven Privacies deck? The deck does not enumerate them ([section 2](#confidential-drift)).
10. Is the loss of the as-of-anchor defense for ESSR messages ([section 4](#aids-not-keys)) acknowledged anywhere by the authors? Neither the papers nor the specification discuss anchoring an ESSR message except as a replay option.
