# 09 — KRAM and Request Authentication {#kram}

## Thesis {#thesis}

KERI signs everything, and a signature over an unordered message is a bearer token: whoever captures it can replay it. Key events are immune to this by construction — a sequence number and a prior digest give every key event its place in a total order — but the six non-key-event message types that carry all of KERI's supporting traffic (`qry`, `rpy`, `pro`, `bar`, `xip`, `exn`) have no such ordering, and therefore no built-in replay protection. KRAM is the mechanism that supplies it. Its design commitment is that replay protection must be non-interactive: no challenge, no nonce round trip, no session, because a challenge-response doubles the packet count and reintroduces a synchronous channel into a protocol family built for asynchronous public networks. In place of interaction, KRAM uses a datetime stamp read against the receiver's clock — so the sender cannot lie about time — plus, in its full form, a monotonic cache keyed per message. Those two supply the two properties every replay defence needs, timeliness and uniqueness.

## Status {#status}

Two facts about KRAM's status matter more than any detail of its design, and they pull in opposite directions. It is absent from every KERI specification; and it is substantially implemented in keripy, to a 2,319-line module with a 6,187-line test suite at 2026-09-15 (2,324 and 6,193 at `80f77b73b`, 2026-10-03). The gap between those is where every open question in this chapter lives, and in the month to 2026-09-15 it widened rather than narrowed: `kraming.py` gained 95 net lines and a new doctrinal rule about which key state a signature is checked against, while the standardizing line gained nothing.

The markers do more work in this chapter than in most, because one tier is nearly empty: for KRAM the [N] tier is empty, and [section 5](#normative-status) documents the one near-miss.

#### Status at 2026-10-03 {#status-2026-10-03}

The gap widened again, in a different direction. Between `b8f60166b` and keripy `main` @`80f77b73b` (2026-10-01) `kraming.py` moved by one commit (`790797bcd`, PR [#1693](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/pull/1693)) and now stands at 2,324 lines against a 6,193-line test file; the whitepaper and both specification lines did not move. The commit is small, but it changes what KRAM is in keripy. KRAM now records the receiver time at which it accepted a complete transactioned message, and IPEX's presentation-registry check refuses a `grant` without that record and uses the time as its cutoff ([section 8](#load-bearing)). So KRAM is no longer only a replay filter in keripy: one verification path above it depends on its acceptance timestamp. Separately, `Baser.clean()` now carries KRAM's replay caches across a database rebuild instead of discarding them ([section 7](#keripy)). Neither change reaches KERIA, which pins `keri==1.2.14` rather than following keripy `main` ([section 5](#normative-status)).

## 1. What problem KRAM solves, and why not the obvious alternatives {#problem}

#### Secure attribution is ephemeral {#ephemeral-attribution}

[P] KERI's sign-everything posture means any over-the-wire message with a source AID and an attached signature can be attributed to that AID's controller. But the attribution decays: "Should the key state change between the time of origination and the time of verification, then the verifier can no longer assume the signature as a secure form of attribution because one of the primary reasons for a given controller to change its key state is to recover from key compromise" (keripy discussion [#934](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/934) > KRAM). A signed message is therefore "at best, an ephemeral issuance given the dynamic key state of the AID" — good enough to defeat impersonation at reception, and no more.

#### The division of labor {#division-of-labor}

[P] #934 states the scope in one sentence: "KERI Key Event messages and their associated KELs have built-in ordering mechanisms that are tied to the current key state, so they are self-protecting from replay attacks. However, the generic exchange, query, reply, prod, and bare messages do not have any built-in replay attack protection. For this purpose, the KRAM (KERI Request Authentication Mechanism) was designed." KRAM is exactly the non-key-event message layer. If you find yourself asking whether KRAM applies to an `icp` or `rot`, the question is malformed.

#### Why not a session, and why not a nonce {#why-not-session-or-nonce}

[P] #934 enumerates three families of replay protection — authenticated sessions on a synchronous channel, interactive challenge-response, timeliness-ordered authentication — and eliminates the first two: sessions are "precluded for asynchronous networks", and challenge-response "is fundamentally less scalable… since it requires a minimum of two times the message traffic for each message to be authenticated." The whitepaper adds a historical argument: nonce challenge-response is an artifact of an era before network time servers, before cheap CSPRNGs, and before asymmetric signatures were affordable, carried forward by teaching habit rather than analysis. Under KERI's actual assumptions — verified key state, ubiquitous NTP, microsecond clocks, asynchronous public networks — the mechanism to beat is a signed timestamp, and eliminating half the packets is "a huge design win."

This is the same architectural instinct as the rest of KERI: prefer the non-interactive, end-verifiable construction, and pay for it in local state rather than in round trips. The KERI spec makes the parallel argument for data-at-rest in its BADA-RUN treatment — "Because non-interactive mitigations are asynchronous… they do not have the latency and scalability limitations of interactive mitigations and are therefore preferred" (KERI spec > Replay attack) — without ever naming KRAM. BADA and KRAM are siblings, not the same thing, and [section 6](#kram-and-bada) takes up where they collide.

#### The general form {#general-form}

[P] "In general replay attack protection imposes some form of timeliness to any signed request and some form of uniqueness to any signed request" (KRAM whitepaper > Replay Attack Protection). Everything below is one of those two properties or a resource bound on them.

## 2. Simple KRAM: what is deployed, and why it is not enough {#simple-kram}

#### The simple KRAM mechanism {#simple-mechanism}

[P] A message carries a `dt` stamp; the receiver accepts it only if that stamp lies inside a window around the receiver's own current time, `[t-d-m*l, t+d]`, where `d` is clock drift/skew, `l` is average network latency and `m` a small integer. Typical values given: `d = 0.01` s, `l = 1` s, `m = 3`. No cache. The security argument is bluntly stated: "This window limits the time during which a replay attack can be mounted… Therefore the protective efficacy of simple KRAM is better the smaller the window."

Because the window is anchored to the receiver's clock, "the originator of the message can't lie about time." That inversion is the load-bearing idea, and it survives into full KRAM unchanged.

#### Why this is what shipped {#why-simple-shipped}

[P] #934 is unusually candid: "due to the exigency of limited resources in implementing support for the vLEI, only the most expedient version of KRAM, called simple KRAM, was implemented. This has now become problematic and is largely the root cause of the associated issues and discussions." The surrounding section generalizes it into an account of why KERI components sit at uneven maturity levels — GLEIF delivered the vLEI under real time and budget pressure, "bare-bones features needed for the vLEI became expedient, whereas other features were not."

#### The failure: multisig cannot fit through a three-second window {#multisig-window-failure}

[P] This is the crux of the whole KRAM programme. With a multisig sender, a receiver running simple KRAM has only one workable policy: accept on any one member's signature. But then "the message is not protected by the threshold of the group multi-sig." Escrowing to collect the rest does not rescue it, because "all the to-be escrowed signatures must still arrive within the narrow time window, which means the coordination of the group members must be tight enough to fit in this window." Three humans on three devices do not sign within three seconds. So a multisig `exn` under simple KRAM either abandons its threshold or cannot be sent at all.

#### The production workaround, and its cost {#two-level-simple-kram}

[P] "Two-level simple KRAM" is named as "the current supported approach": the over-the-wire `exn` acts as a wrapper, single-signed by any one member and subject to KRAM, while an embedded payload is separately signed by the group, is not subject to KRAM, and goes into a signature-collecting escrow once the wrapper is through. The whitepaper describes the payload as "a tunneled exchange message… The tunnel is meant to cross through simple KRAM." Every member signs twice, and the escrow must hold partially signed payloads for as long as the group takes to converge. The variant that collects payload signatures before sending, via a pre-protocol, is HAMI ([keripy#911](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/issues/911)).

#### An architectural tell {#architectural-tell}

Read this as an architectural tell. A mechanism whose window must be short to be secure, meeting a signing ceremony whose duration is set by humans, produces a tunnel through the security mechanism. Full KRAM's central move is to make the window long without making it weak.

## 3. Full KRAM: monotonic caches, and why the window can be long {#full-kram}

#### The monotonic-cache move {#monotonic-cache}

[P] Replace "is this timestamp fresh?" with "is this timestamp fresh *and* strictly later than the last one I cached for this sender?" Once a monotonic cache is doing the uniqueness work, the window's job changes completely: "the monotonicity of the cache protects against a replay attack and the time window merely bounds the memory requirements." A window that is only a memory bound can be hours, days or weeks. That single reallocation of responsibility is what makes multisig workable, and it is the most important idea in the chapter.

#### Window and cache {#window-and-cache}

[P] The window is `[t-d-l, t+d]` on the receiver's clock. Redesign-era example values: `d = 100` ms, short lag `sl = 2` s, long lag `ll = 2` h, exchange lag `xl = 48` h. A cache entry records the message's datetime and the window parameters in force when it was created — so reconfiguring windows never disturbs caches already in flight.

#### Retrograde clock protection {#retrograde-clock}

[P] A rewound receiver clock would otherwise re-open the window on old messages. Cached entries already refuse them; for everything else, "the receiver persists to durable storage a single timestamp of the latest time it sees. If the network time is even older than the latest saved time, the receiver can refuse to accept messages until the clock catches up."

#### Detection, not only prevention {#detection}

[P] Under the strict policy the receiver answers a given signed request exactly once, which makes a successful interception visible to the sender: either no response arrives, or it arrives redirected from a host that is not the responder. This is duplicity-detection reasoning — KERI's signature move — applied to request authentication, and it is worth naming when someone objects that a timing window is weaker than a nonce.

#### Granularity is both isolation and parallelism {#granularity}

[P] Cache keys are drawn from the vector `[source AID, message type, route, exchange ID, message ID]`. Finer keys stop interleaved transactions from colliding, and because microsecond timestamps give ~1M monotonic slots per second per cache entry, finer keys also raise throughput. The whitepaper's worst case: with `d = 100` ms and zero network latency a sender has roughly 90,000 slots before it runs past the receiver's leading edge and must block; every millisecond of real latency adds 1,000 more. Conclusion: microsecond resolution "is more than adequate for the foreseeable future."

#### One rule with teeth {#window-per-type}

[P] "When using per-transaction ID or per-message ID caching, the window size must be per transaction type, not per transaction ID/message ID, to avoid a cache-prune replay attack." A sender that could name its own window could either exhaust the receiver's memory with an unprunable cache, or open a replay gap at prune time. Window class is always the receiver's decision. Any proposal to let a message declare its own freshness budget runs into this.

#### Out-of-order is not replay {#out-of-order}

[P] Asynchronous multipath delivery reorders messages that are not attacks. The whitepaper's answer is structural: interactive transactions self-order because the parties take turns, so use one cache entry per transaction on asynchronous transport, and only pipeline several transactions through one entry on an in-order channel or where the transaction layer retries.

## 4. The v0.7.6 redesign: three authentication types, two window levels, two gap attacks — and one rule that lives only in code {#redesign}

The whitepaper's front matter is a redesign that supersedes the material behind it, and reading the older sections as current is the easiest mistake to make with this source. Sam's stated motive: "we added a lot of flexibility to allow resource tuning that may be overkill… in hindsight, I think single-key and multi-key controlled identifiers are sufficiently different that a different logic split would benefit."

#### Authentication types, chosen by cardinality not threshold {#auth-types}

[P] Three: attached seal reference (`asr`), attached signature single-key (`assk`), attached signature multi-key (`asmk`). The split is by "the cardinality of the current key list for the sender AID, not by whether the attached signature(s) satisfy the threshold… because threshold satisfication requires verifying the attached signatures first, which is a much heavier operation than merely counting the elements in the key list." A seal-reference message needs no signature collection at all — the anchoring seal in the sender's KEL already authenticated it — so it takes the short window whatever the key list looks like. When both a seal and signatures are attached, the seal is validated first because it is cheaper; if it validates, the signatures are discarded.

#### The payoff {#payoff}

Only `asmk` ever waits. Long windows are confined to the one case that genuinely needs them, so the exposure that a long window represents is not spread across all traffic.

#### Two window levels {#two-window-levels}

[P] An inner window per message ID, `[rdt-d-l, rdt+d]`, and for transactioned exchanges an outer window per exchange ID, `[xdt, xdt+xl]`, anchored at the `dt` of the `xip` that opened the transaction. Both must be satisfied. The outer window exists because "a transaction may not advance until a message is authenticated" — without a cap on the transaction as a whole, an exchange cache could never be released.

#### Per-message-ID caching has a pleasant side effect {#per-message-id-caching}

[P] A message's SAID digests its `dt`, so two messages with different datetimes are different message IDs with different caches. Consequently a sender needs no cross-device synchronization to avoid tripping its own monotonicity, and reordered arrivals do not knock each other out. The price is storage: "the lower storage limit required for full KRAM is higher," and window size is the only remaining knob.

#### The redesign assumes KERI v2 {#assumes-v2}

[P] Transactioned `exn` requires a non-empty `x`; a v1 `exn` is treated as non-transactioned even when its `p` field chains it to a predecessor.

#### Accept lag vs prune lag, and the two gap attacks {#gap-attacks}

[P] Each cache type carries paired lags — `sl/ll/xl` to accept, `psl/pll/pxl` to prune — with prune ≥ accept and typically equal. The pair exists to make reconfiguration safe:

- Gap replay. A message is accepted, then pruned. The accept window is lengthened. The pruned message is replayed and now falls inside the window with no cache entry left to refuse it.
- Gap first-play. A message is rejected as too old and so is never cached. The accept window is lengthened. An attacker submits it as a first play. The whitepaper works the case: the sender, seeing no response, reissues identical content with a fresh `dt` and fresh message ID, and the victim suffers the effect twice from two messages that differ only in their timestamp.

The mitigation is a staged change: raise the prune lag immediately, then delay raising the accept lag by `delta = new - old` so any exploitable message ages out first. Decreases are safe and immediate. Changing the granularity of cache types is the unsolved case in the whitepaper — "This logic has yet to be worked out" — because adding a route-specific type can extend coverage to messages that a shorter-windowed type would have refused.

#### Configuration, including a firewall {#configuration-denials}

[P] A `"kram"` dictionary in the HJSON config carries `enabled`, `caches` (prepopulating the cache-type table) and `denials` — a list of `(version, ilk, route-prefix)` triples that behave "like a set of explicit firewall denial rules," where a match disables KRAM for that message. Its stated purpose is backward compatibility for pre-KRAM applications and for "message-type-route combinations, such as BADA-RUN endpoints, that conflict with KRAM."

#### KEL availability: drop and cue, don't escrow {#kel-availability}

[P] If the sender's KEL or the specific event is missing, the whitepaper says drop the message and cue a retrieval rather than escrow it, because "the time required to notify and then retrieve the KEL exceeds the KRAM message window, causing the message to be dropped, even after the KEL is retrieved. Which makes moot the use of the escrow." It notes in passing that the existing escrow logic has "a bug… that can cause a loop that repeatedly reescrows."

#### DDoS surface {#ddos-surface}

[P] Stripping attachments to force a drop is a weak DoS; stripping and reattaching invalid signatures is stronger; attaching a bogus seal alongside valid signatures is an amplification attempt against the receiver's processing budget. The answer is ordering — accept if either authenticator validates, check the cheapest first — with transport encryption as a complementary mitigation.

#### Which key state authenticates a signature: the currency rule, and the one place it is written down {#currency-rule}

[K] Everything above this paragraph is the whitepaper's. This is not: it is a rule about what a signature means that exists in keripy's code and in no specification, and it changed this month. A transferable signature arrives as a `tsg`, a quad `(prefixer, number, sdiger, sigers)` whose `(number, sdiger)` names an event in the signer's KEL, and before checking the signature a receiver must decide whether that reference is current. Two answers are available, and they agree right up until a non-establishment event intervenes: the sender's latest KEL event of any kind, or the sender's last establishment event. keripy at `b8f60166b` takes the second, and states the reasoning in the module's own prose: "Signing keys change only at establishment events, so the key state ref names the last establishment event rather than the last event of any kind" (keripy `core/kraming.py`, module docstring above `class Kramer`, L70-75). The gate that enforces it is `if (number.sn != kever.lastEst.s or sdiger.qb64 != kever.lastEst.d): continue` (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `Kramer._verifyAttachedSigs`, L706-707), and ten sites across the module now carry that same reference — [section 7](#keripy) lists them.

#### Superseded 2026-09-15: the old rule, and why its failure mode is the point {#currency-rule-superseded}

At `fe1617090` the same gate compared against the sender's latest event of any kind — `if (number.sn != kever.sner.num or sdiger.qb64 != kever.serder.said): continue` (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `Kramer._verifyAttachedSigs`, L697-698 @`fe1617090`) — and every one of the ten sites read that way. The substitution landed in `8870ea6a0` (PR [#1658](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/pull/1658)) and, for the partial-multisig escrow, in `72b034073` (PR [#1662](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/pull/1662), closing [#1661](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/issues/1661)). The two rules diverge exactly on an AID that has anchored something without rotating — a registry inception, a credential issuance, a revocation — which is to say on every credential issuer. Under the old rule such a sender's `tsg` named the KEL tip while the verifier compared against a reference that no longer matched, so correctly formed signatures were refused; and because the partial-multisig escrow pinned the same reference when collection began, with nothing refreshing it, a multisig sender that anchored anything mid-collection could never again match and so could never reach threshold. The message class KRAM governs is exactly the class issuers use, so the mis-stated rule failed on the population the mechanism exists to serve rather than on an edge case. That is why this is doctrine and not a defect note: a rule this consequential was recorded in a docstring and nowhere else, and drifted out of the code beneath it without anything catching it. The docstring on `_normalizeCurrentSenderTsgs` had stated the establishment-event rule correctly the whole time, at both pins — "When prefixer.qb64 == senderId and (number, sdiger) references the sender's current establishment event, those sigers are also added to ``sigers``" (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `_normalizeCurrentSenderTsgs`, L442-445 @`fe1617090`, L444-447 @`b8f60166b`). The prose was right and the code was wrong, which is what an unspecified interface looks like from the inside.

The rule loosens what counts as current without loosening what counts as stale: a signature made under a key state that a rotation has superseded is still refused, and the test that pins that behavior survives (keripy `tests/core/test_kraming.py`, `test_stale_tsgs`, L2598 @`b8f60166b`), alongside two new tests naming the interaction case (`test_tsgs_current_when_latest_event_is_non_establishment`, L6000; `test_partial_multisig_survives_an_anchor_during_collection`, L6093). What the rule does for delegated establishment, and for superseding or recovery events, was not examined and is not claimed here.

## 5. Normative status: the tier that is empty {#normative-status}

#### KRAM is in no KERI specification {#no-spec}

KRAM is in no KERI specification [N-absent]. Re-verified at `trustoverip/kswg-keri-specification` `main` @`fbdd4a615` (2026-09-15) and unchanged. A case-insensitive search for `kram` across the repository at that commit returns exactly one file, `docs/versions/v1/index.html`, and zero hits anywhere under `spec/`. The hit is not spec text. It is an external cross-reference pulled from `trustoverip/kerisuite-glossary` (declared `external_spec: "keri1"`, KERI spec `specs.json`, L26), and the whole of it is: "All requests from a web client must use KRAM (KERI Request Authentication Method) for replay attack protection. The method is essentially based on each request body needing to include a date time string field in ISO-8601 format that must be within an acceptable time window relative to the server's date time," plus a link to the `WebOfTrust/kram` repo. `spec/spec-body.md` does not mention KRAM. The ACDC and CESR specs do not mention it either — but that second negative rests on a search run on 2026-09-01 and carries no commit pin, because neither specification is available to re-search here; treat it as unverified rather than as re-confirmed.

Three things follow, and they are all worth saying out loud.

#### The one published definition is the inadequate version {#glossary-definition}

The one published definition describes the version that is known to be inadequate. The glossary defines simple KRAM — a window, no cache — which is precisely the variant #934 says cannot support multisig. An implementer reading the specs and the glossary and nothing else would build the thing the designer has already superseded.

#### The name is unstable {#name-unstable}

Whitepaper and repo say "Mechanism"; the glossary says "Method." Both circulate. Use "Mechanism."

#### Everything load-bearing lives in a personal repo {#personal-repo}

`SmithSamuelM/Papers/whitepapers/kram.md` is versioned (v0.7.6), maintained (substantive commits through March 2026) and directive down to Python module names — but it is one person's repository on a `master` branch, with no change-control process, no review gate, and no stable citation. The keripy implementation issue [#937](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/issues/937) names it as the target of implementation. A protocol layer that every non-key-event message depends on is specified nowhere that a standards process can reach. That is the finding an adversarial reviewer will lead with, and it is correct.

#### The gap widened this month {#gap-widened}

The gap widened this month, and it is measurable. The whitepaper has not moved since March 2026 — the most recent commit touching `whitepapers/kram.md` is the merge `6ed1ceff4` (2026-03-06), behind the substantive `67550b477` of the same day — while `kraming.py` gained 95 net lines and the key-state currency rule of [section 4](#currency-rule), which no specification states anywhere. A residual that is re-verified at a new pin and found larger is worth more than one merely re-asserted.

#### The same correction at both ends of the wire {#convergent-correction}

The sharpest illustration of what that residual costs is the same correction, made independently at both ends of the wire, six days apart. keripy fixed the receiver's currency test in `kraming.py` on 2026-09-01. KERIA fixed the sender's construction of the same quad on 2026-09-07 — at `7e685edaa` the `/end/role` handler builds `tsg = (hab.kever.prefixer, coring.Seqner(sn=hab.kever.lastEst.s), coring.Saider(qb64=hab.kever.lastEst.d), rsigers)` (KERIA `src/keria/app/aiding.py`, end-role handler, L1663-1668), where immediately before commit `b57780a8b` the same two positions read `coring.Seqner(sn=hab.kever.sn), coring.Saider(qb64=hab.kever.serder.said),` (KERIA `src/keria/app/aiding.py`, L1665-1666 @`b57780a8b^`) [K]. Two codebases, two authors, opposite ends of one wire, the same substitution in the same month — and both arrived at it from failures rather than from a document. Independent convergent error is evidence about the specification, not about either implementer, and this one governs what a signature means. The reading is strengthened, not weakened, by the fact that fourteen sites elsewhere in KERIA already constructed the quad correctly (`ipexing.py`, `grouping.py`, `peer/exchanging.py`); the two `rpy` handlers were stragglers against a convention the rest of the codebase already followed, which is precisely what an unwritten convention looks like when it fails. Whether a third class of site still seals to the latest event was not audited at either pin. One coincidence is worth noticing and not worth explaining: the two KERIA handlers that were wrong serve `/end/role` and `/loc/scheme`, which are exactly the two routes keripy hard-codes as KRAM denials because they belong to BADA ([section 6](#kram-and-bada)). Whether being outside KRAM's path is why they lagged the convention, or whether the overlap is chance, is not something this corpus can settle from the code. This section's residual is usually argued as a governance risk; this is the concrete form it takes — a rule everyone agrees on, nowhere written, discovered twice by breakage.

#### The two fixes share no code {#fixes-independent}

Added 2026-10-03: the two fixes are independent in a stronger sense than "two authors". KERIA does not consume keripy `main`. Its dependency is pinned exactly — `"keri==1.2.14",` (KERIA `pyproject.toml`, L30 @`928ce26a4`), tightened from `"keri>=1.2.13,<1.3",` at `7e685edaa` [K] — while keripy `main`, where `kraming.py`'s establishment-event rule and everything else in this chapter's [section 7](#keripy) landed, is on the 2.1.0-dev line (`raw/10-keria-signify.md` section 11a). So KERIA's `/end/role` and `/loc/scheme` fix was a reimplementation in KERIA's own code, not an inheritance from keripy's, and the convergence above is between two codebases that do not share the code in question. The same pin bounds every other `[K]` claim here: "keripy does X" at `b8f60166b` or `80f77b73b` is not evidence that a KERIA deployment does X, and that includes the KRAM implementation of [section 7](#keripy), the currency rule of [section 4](#currency-rule), and the acceptance timestamp of [section 8](#load-bearing). Whether `keri==1.2.14` contains any part of `kraming.py` was not checked and is not claimed either way.

## 6. KRAM and BADA-RUN: siblings that cannot both govern a message {#kram-and-bada}

Both are non-interactive replay defences; they are not the same mechanism and do not compose silently.

#### BADA {#bada}

[N] BADA (Best-Available-Data-Acceptance) is in the KERI spec, and governs data at rest: it guarantees monotonicity of updates to signed data. For KEL-anchored updates, accept if the update's anchor is later in the KEL than the prior's. For signed-but-not-anchored updates, compare key states, later key state wins, and where the key-state location is equal, later datetime wins — with datetimes relative to the controller's clock (KERI spec, ~L2887-2918; `raw/01-keri-spec.md` section 10).

#### KRAM {#kram-in-flight}

[P] KRAM governs messages in flight, with datetimes relative to the receiver's clock.

#### Whose clock is authoritative {#whose-clock}

They disagree on whose clock is authoritative, which is why a single message cannot be subject to both. The whitepaper resolves it by exclusion rather than reconciliation: BADA-RUN endpoints go in the denials list. [K] keripy hard-codes that resolution — `Kramer.OobiDenials` merges denials for `rpy` routes `/end/role` and `/loc/scheme` whenever KRAM is enabled, with the comment that "OOBI endpoint discovery replies rely on BADA acceptance rather than KRAM replay protection" (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `Kramer.OobiDenials`, L98-147).

The clean statement: BADA orders what a host stores; KRAM orders what a host accepts off the wire. OOBI and service-endpoint discovery are BADA's, everything else in the non-key-event set is KRAM's, and the boundary is drawn by configuration rather than by the message shape — which means it is a thing an operator can get wrong.

## 7. What keripy actually does {#keripy}

#### The redesign is built {#redesign-built}

[K] The redesign is built, and it tracks v0.7.6 rather than the older strata. `src/keri/core/kraming.py` is 2,319 lines against a 6,187-line test file at `b8f60166b`, and 2,324 against 6,193 at `80f77b73b`. Line hints below are at `b8f60166b` unless stamped otherwise; past about L1460 they run 2 to 5 lines short at `80f77b73b`. Specifics worth knowing:

#### A database clean keeps the replay markers (new 2026-10-03) {#clean-keeps-replay-markers}

New 2026-10-03, at `80f77b73b`: a database clean keeps the replay markers. `Baser.clean()` rebuilds the database from a re-verified clone, and before PR [#1684](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/pull/1684) (`87ba6a56d`, `56c43fd2b`) it copied none of KRAM's stores, so a clean discarded every timeliness cache. That reopened exactly the window a replay cache exists to close, in the same shape as [section 4](#gap-attacks)'s gap replay: a message accepted and then forgotten can be played again. The docstring now states the rule — "Copy KRAM replay caches without reverification. Discard cache types and partial state; Kramer rebuilds cache types from configuration." (keripy `db/basing.py`, `Baser.clean`, L1594-1595 @`80f77b73b`) and "Retained replay markers require retries to use a new datetime and new signatures." (L1596-1597) — and the three stores copied, `"kramMSGC", "kramTMSC", "kramXDT"` (L1639), sit in the list the code calls `unsecured`, copied as-is rather than re-verified [K]. Two things are lost on purpose. Cache types come back from configuration, so a window change made at runtime and never written to the config file does not survive a clean; the staged reconfiguration that defends against [section 4](#gap-attacks)'s gap attacks is therefore only as durable as the config file. And partial multisig state is dropped, so a group message mid-collection must be re-sent with a new `dt` — one more site where keripy discards partials earlier than the whitepaper's "until prune time" (see the divergence below).

#### Where the currency rule bites {#currency-rule-sites}

[K] Where the currency rule of [section 4](#currency-rule) actually bites. Ten sites in `kraming.py` compare a `tsg`'s `(number, sdiger)` against a key-state reference, and at `b8f60166b` all ten read `kever.lastEst`: `_normalizeCurrentSenderTsgs` (L460-461), `_scrubSameSenderPriorEventTsgs` (L553-554), `_scrubFailedVerification` (L609-610), `_setVerifiedSenderTsg` (L632-633), `_verifyAttachedSigs` (L706-707), `_storeNonAuthAttachments` (L854-855), and the four partial-multisig key-state pins at L1159-1160, L1331-1332, L1407-1408 and L1669-1670. The escrow pin is the consequential one — `currentKeyState = (Number(num=kever.lastEst.s), Diger(qb64=kever.lastEst.d))` (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `Kramer.kramit`, non-transactioned multi-key accumulation, L1159-1160) — because the value is stored when collection opens and compared on every later delivery, and nothing refreshes it.

#### A third application: endorser last-sig groups {#endorser-groups}

[K] A third application shows the rule generalizes. When a partially-signed multisig message carries a last-sig group from someone other than the sender — an endorser whose signature does not authenticate this message but must travel with it — keripy resolves that group to the endorser's last establishment event at the moment of receipt and stores it in explicit `(prefixer, number, diger, siger)` form, because this "freezes the establishment reference so a later endorser rotation before the sender reaches its signature threshold does not reinterpret the escrowed signatures" (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `Kramer._storeNonAuthAttachments` docstring, L828-830). The principle worth carrying out of the chapter is that an escrow that holds signatures across time must record the key state each signature was made under, not consult the current one at release. Otherwise the meaning of a signature changes while it sits in the escrow — and a long window, which is full KRAM's whole contribution, is exactly what gives it time to change.

#### The `processMsg` consolidation {#processmsg}

[K] The `processMsg` consolidation the whitepaper asks for exists. `Kevery.processMsg` (keripy `core/eventing.py`, `Kevery.processMsg`, L4683) is the single entry point for `qry, rpy, pro, bar, xip, exn`, documented with the whitepaper's three steps in order: AID allow/deny, `self.kramer.intake()` (L4741), then message-specific dispatch. `processXip`, `processPro` and `processBar` are still stubs — `"""Stub: KERI v2 exchange transaction; no processing yet."""` (keripy `core/eventing.py`, `Kevery.processXip`, L4818-4819), with `processPro` (L4822-4823) and `processBar` (L4826-4827) identical in form. KRAM will authenticate a `xip` and then hand it to a `pass`, and a month of concentrated KRAM work did not change that. Re-read at `80f77b73b`, 2026-10-03, and unchanged: `processMsg` is now at keripy `core/eventing.py` L4645 with `self.kramer.intake` at L4703, and all three handlers are still `pass` stubs, `processXip` carrying the same docstring (L4780-4781) and `processPro` (L4784-4786) and `processBar` (L4788-4790) theirs.

#### Default-disabled, and where it is enabled {#enable-kram}

[K] "Default-disabled" is true of the class and misleading about deployments. Unchanged at `b8f60166b`. `Kevery.__init__` takes `enableKram=False` (keripy `core/eventing.py`, `Kevery.__init__`, L4136), so a bare `Kevery` does no KRAM. But `directing.py` (L470) and `indirecting.py` (L76) both pass `enableKram=True` — the runtimes that actually run agents and witnesses turn it on. Those three are the only `enableKram` occurrences in `src/keri`. Re-read at `80f77b73b`, 2026-10-03: still true of the sites that set a value, with the `Kevery.__init__` default now at `eventing.py` L4100 and the two runtime sites unmoved. A literal search also hits L4115 and L4139, the parameter's docstring and the `if kramer is None and enableKram:` test that reads it, so "only occurrences" should be read as "only settings". Anyone repeating "KRAM is disabled by default" should say which of those two claims they mean.

#### Window classes have no defaults {#no-default-windows}

[K] Window classes have no defaults and no guidance, and that did not change either. `kraming.py` ships no default window values at all. Every `(d, sl, ll, xl, psl, pll, pxl)` septuple comes from the `"kram"` config's `"caches"` dictionary via `_populateCtyp` (L196-208), and `_parseValidateCtyp` (L151-188) enforces only ordering and sign — `d >= 0`, `0 < sl <= ll <= xl`, `psl >= sl`, `pll >= ll`, `pxl >= xl` (L170-185). There is no ceiling, no non-trivial floor, and no warning at any magnitude: a deployer may configure a one-millisecond short lag or a thirty-day one and the code accepts both silently. A receiver that configures nothing does not get a safe default either — `_fetchCacheType` raises `KramError` when nothing matches and no `"~"` catchall exists (L312-313). The whitepaper's example numbers stay examples in prose with no counterpart in code, so the security parameter that decides how wide the replay surface is remains the deployer's to choose unaided. Both gap attacks of [section 4](#gap-attacks) are likewise unaffected by anything that changed this month.

#### Implementation history {#implementation-history}

[P/K] [#1302](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/issues/1302) (closed 2026-03-25) records that PR #1288 landed the core "aligned with the v0.7.5 whitepaper" and lists as remaining gaps the attachment databases, the pruning doer, keystate-retrieval cueing, AID allow/deny, and continued signature collection until the prune window. The first four are present. Amended 2026-09-15: the fifth no longer holds as written — see the divergence below. The implementation is not a sketch; it is close behind a moving specification, and this month it moved past the specification in one place.

#### Do partials survive threshold? {#partials-divergence}

A divergence the corpus is not going to reconcile: do partials survive threshold? [P] versus [K]. The whitepaper says yes, deliberately. Its processing pseudocode states three times over that "the partially signed database entries will remain until the prune window closes, upon which all the partially signed database entries will be deleted" (KRAM whitepaper > Full KRAM processing steps, L306, L353, L427 @`6ed1ceff4`), and says why in the same breath: "This allows signatures to continue to be collected even after the threshold is reached", because "It is desirable to know all who signed, not just the first who meet the threshold." The commit that introduced the wording is titled as a clarification, so it is a considered position rather than incidental phrasing. keripy at `b8f60166b` says no. On threshold satisfaction it rehydrates the accumulated state onto the outgoing message and then removes the three partial-multisig stores and every non-auth attachment store, with a stated rationale of its own — "The cache remains as the replay marker. Partial state is no longer retryable after the accepted handoff." (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `Kramer.kramit`, non-transactioned multi-key threshold branch, L1215-1220; the transactioned branch does the same at L1463-1468, and a third site clears them on key-state change at L1166-1171 and L1414-1418). Superseded, 2026-09-15: this chapter previously relayed the whitepaper's policy as implemented, which was true at `fe1617090` — the only clearing calls in the whole file were the two pruner bodies — and is false at `b8f60166b`, where four earlier clearing sites exist. The change is `d70f670c7` (2026-09-02). The two rationales answer different questions and both are coherent: the whitepaper's is evidentiary, the code's is replay-safety. The observable consequence of keripy's rule is worth stating plainly, because it is the thing the whitepaper wanted to avoid — a late co-signer's endorsement of an already-accepted multisig message is silently discarded. Which policy governs is unsettled, and this chapter leaves it standing as an open tension rather than picking a winner; the whitepaper is the design of record, the implementation is what runs. Added 2026-10-03: at `80f77b73b` the transactioned threshold branch cited above has drifted to about L1465-1471 (`raw/15-kram.md` coverage note; the other line hints in this paragraph were not re-verified and keep their `b8f60166b` stamp), and `Baser.clean()` is a further site that drops partial state, earlier than prune time and on a different trigger (above). The divergence did not narrow.

#### Three divergences worth checking [K] {#three-divergences}

1. Cache-type route matching is exact; denial route matching is a prefix. `_fetchCacheType` compares `key == f"{msgType}.R.{route}"` (L294, L299) while `intake` uses `md.startswith(d)` (L1031). So a cache-type configured for `/end` does not govern a message routed `/end/role`, though a denial configured for `/end` does deny it. The whitepaper's "most specific matching" language does not settle which was intended, and the asymmetry is the kind that produces a window silently defaulting to the catchall. Unchanged at the new pin.
2. The gap-attack defences assume windows change only through `changeConfig`. Cache entries store their own parameters at creation precisely so in-flight caches are immune to reconfiguration. That invariant is the hinge of the whole gap-attack argument, and it is now partly closed: `test_existing_caches_unchanged_on_config_update` (keripy `tests/core/test_kraming.py`, L3628) exercises exactly it. Whether any path other than `changeConfig` can mutate a live window is still not established.
3. A design rationale in the source cites a pull request that does not exist. `_scrubSameSenderPriorEventTsgs` justifies its policy by quoting "KRAM PR #1788" (keripy `core/kraming.py`, `_scrubSameSenderPriorEventTsgs`, L525-528); `WebOfTrust/keripy` has no PR #1788, the API returns 404, and the repository's PR numbers are in the 1600s. The policy is implemented and coherent — prior-event `tsg`s from the sender cannot authenticate, so they are scrubbed — and only its provenance is unresolvable. It is a small thing, recorded because a module that documents itself this carefully is the only place several of KRAM's rules are written down at all, which makes an unfollowable citation inside it a different kind of problem than it would be elsewhere.

## 8. Where KRAM is load-bearing for other designs {#load-bearing}

#### Multiply-endorsed presentation rests on KRAM {#multiply-endorsed}

Multiply-endorsed presentation rests on it entirely — see [chapter 08, sections 7 to 9](08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md#sec-7). The proposed IPEX design uses KRAM's escrow as the signature-collection mechanism itself: a replayed copy of the same message inside the window is not an attack but a contribution, so "This elegantly solves the multi-sig problem without requiring a pre-protocol to collect signatures. The receiver's KRAM escrow does the signature collection" (keripy discussion [#1613](https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1613)). That is an elegant reuse, and it means a freshness mechanism is also an availability mechanism: if KRAM's escrow is off or its window is short, group presentation does not merely lose replay protection, it stops working.

#### KRAM's acceptance time as evidence {#acceptance-time}

[K] KRAM's acceptance time is now evidence for another check, at `80f77b73b`. The previous paragraph is a design argument from #1613. Since 2026-10-03 there is a coupling in code as well, and it runs the other way: not IPEX reusing KRAM's escrow, but IPEX reading KRAM's record. The transactioned-message cache record gained a field documented as `rdt (str): receiver datetime when KRAM accepted the complete message` (keripy `recording.py`, `TxnMsgCacheRecord` docstring, L505). It is set on single-key acceptance and, for a multi-key message, only once threshold is met — the partial record stores an empty string, under the comment "A partial cache is only a replay marker. Receiver time is recorded once the complete threshold is accepted." (keripy `core/kraming.py`, L1658-1659 @`80f77b73b`) — and when accumulated partials later satisfy threshold the cache is re-pinned with `cache.rdt = helping.nowIso8601()` (L1463). The consumer is IPEX's presentation-registry check, which refuses a `grant` declaring a presentation registry unless that record exists — "A presentation requirement needs a completed KRAM acceptance." (keripy `acdc/ipexing.py`, L1524 @`80f77b73b`) — and then cuts off registry history at the acceptance time: "Require a valid binding anchored before the KRAM-accepted send." (L1543). `raw/16-presentation-registries.md` section 2.2 carries the IPEX side, including that `vetBinds` drops registry updates whose `dt` is later than that time.

Three consequences, at different strengths. First, and established in code: KRAM is no longer only a replay filter in keripy. One verification path above it depends on its acceptance timestamp, so KRAM's bookkeeping has become an input to whether a credential presentation is valid, not merely to whether a message is fresh. Second, and an inference rather than a statement in the code: a deployment that disables KRAM (`"enabled": false`, [section 4](#configuration-denials)) should be unable to accept any `grant` that carries a presentation-registry declaration, because no transactioned cache record will exist for the check to read. `raw/15-kram.md` section 4.8 draws this from the two code sites and did not look for a test exercising KRAM-disabled presentation; treat it as a likely consequence, not a verified one. Third, the boundary on the coupling: `kraming.py` still contains no presentation-registry logic, so #1613's statement that KRAM does not process a registry-based authentication factor still holds; KRAM records a time, and the registry check lives above it (`raw/16` section 2.6). Neither the whitepaper nor any KERI or ACDC specification line mentions a receiver acceptance time used by another protocol — this is implementation-first, which is the pattern of this whole chapter. It also reaches only keripy `main`; KERIA, on `keri==1.2.14`, has none of it ([section 5](#normative-status)).

#### The `ax` anchoring field {#ax-field}

The `ax` anchoring field exists because KRAM's guarantee expires. #1613's argument for `ax` is that KRAM proves timeliness but "gives neither party a way to signal to the IPEX that the relevant messages MUST be perpetually verifiable and hence anchored." KRAM is a freshness mechanism, not an evidence mechanism; anchoring is what converts a timely exchange into a durable one.

#### Tethering {#tethering}

Tethering is defined in terms of KRAM's guarantee. An AID appearing anywhere in the disclosed DAG is "tethered" to a grant when KRAM has established fresh proof of control over it — "Tethering implies no other relationship besides fresh (timely) proof-of-control over an AID so tethered."

#### A correction to chapter 08 on nonces {#nonce-correction}

A correction to [chapter 08, section 7](08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md#sec-7), on the evidence of the primary source. That chapter describes KRAM, following #1613, as using "a message SAID `d` field, a sender AID `i` field, a receiver AID `ri` field, a salty nonce `u` field, and a datetime stamp," and then flags a problem: `u` is not a field of an `exn`. The whitepaper does not build KRAM on a nonce. Its uniqueness comes from monotonic ordering of receiver-clock datetimes, and its Background section is an extended argument that nonce-based mechanisms are the thing KRAM replaces. A salty nonce appears once, in a different role: placed in the `q` modifier block of a transaction's first message to make the transaction ID universally unique — and the whitepaper immediately says a unique transaction ID cannot substitute for a timestamp, "because a timestamp is still needed in order to know when any given transaction can be pruned." So the `u`-on-`exn` puzzle is most likely an artifact of #1613's paraphrase rather than a design gap. The keripy implementation reads `msg.stamp` and never touches `u` (keripy `core/kraming.py`, L1071, re-checked at `b8f60166b`; `u` does not appear as a field reference anywhere in the module), which is consistent with the whitepaper and not with the paraphrase.

## 9. Open questions {#open-questions}

1. Where does KRAM get a normative home? It is required for every non-key-event message and specified only in a personal repository. The KERI spec is the obvious candidate; a standalone specification is the alternative.
2. What is the migration path from simple to full KRAM? The glossary defines simple; deployments run simple; keripy now implements full; multiply-endorsed presentation needs full. Nothing states what a mixed network does, or how a receiver signals which it enforces.
3. Is `Signify-Timestamp` enforced anywhere? [K] No, at either end, and the negative is stronger and more symmetric than when this chapter first recorded it. Re-run at KERIA `7e685edaa`: the auth path has been refactored into an abstract `Authenticator` with two concrete subclasses, and both bind a timestamp without checking it. `SignedHeaderAuthenticator` still lists `Signify-Timestamp` among its `DefaultFields` (KERIA `src/keria/core/authing.py`, `SignedHeaderAuthenticator`, L81); its `inbound` (L95-167) reconstructs the RFC-9421 signature base and verifies it, and `inputage.expires` appears only as a value folded into that base string (L143-144), never compared to a clock. `ESSRAuthenticator.inbound` reads the header (L233) and places it in the signed payload as `dt=dt` (L253) before verifying — bound, never checked. `fromIso8601` does not appear in `authing.py` at all; the only two time calls in the file are outbound stamps, one of them `response.set_header("Signify-Timestamp", helping.nowIso8601())` (L180). `inputage.nonce` is signed and unexamined too, and there is no replay cache anywhere in `src/keria`. New this month: the client half does not enforce it either — signify-ts at `ffba8406e` folds `input.expires` into the params string (signify-ts `src/keri/core/authing.ts`, L136-137) and never compares it, and its `EssrAuthenticator` checks only that a timestamp is present. So the property this buys is request integrity and origin authentication, not replay protection. State the boundary whenever the finding is used, because it is narrower than it sounds in both directions. The signature covers `@method` and `@path`, so a captured request cannot be replayed against a different method and path — that much is real, and is more than a bearer token offers. But against the same method and path it replays indefinitely, because nothing checks the time and nothing remembers the request. What retires a stolen header set is key rotation, not expiry. This remains a negative search result over a bounded set of files rather than a maintainer's statement, and the fields needed to enforce freshness are present and already signed, so enforcement would be a verifier change with no wire change. Recorded in `raw/10-keria-signify.md` section 2, which owns the KERIA material.
4. Who owns the granularity-change gap analysis? The whitepaper marks it unfinished; keripy implements it. Which is authoritative when they differ?
5. What happens downstream of `xip`, `pro` and `bar`? All three pass through KRAM into stubs. Still open at `b8f60166b`, after the busiest month `kraming.py` has had, and still open at `80f77b73b` ([section 7](#processmsg)).
6. How should an operator choose window classes? The lag values are security parameters — too long widens the replay surface, too short breaks multisig — and there is no policy guidance anywhere, only example numbers. Still open at `b8f60166b`, and there is not even an implicit recommendation to read off the code, since `kraming.py` ships no defaults and validates only ordering and sign ([section 7](#no-default-windows)).
7. Does the `Exchanger` gap in [chapter 08, section 7](08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md#sec-7) close here? KRAM deliberately preserves non-sender endorsements for downstream handlers (`_normalizeSenderSeals`, "leaves only non-sender triples in ssts for non-auth forwarding / escrow"), and `Exchanger.processEvent` still rejects any message carrying a signature not from the sender (keripy `peer/exchanging.py`, `Exchanger.processEvent`, L88-95 @`4df8e4a86`, verified at `upstream/main` and not re-read at `b8f60166b` — `exchanging.py` was outside this month's mining). Two halves of one code path, still pulling in opposite directions as far as the evidence goes.
8. Do partially-signed entries survive threshold satisfaction? The whitepaper says yes, on evidentiary grounds; keripy at `b8f60166b` says no, on replay-safety grounds; both are quoted at their pins in [section 7](#partials-divergence). This is the sharpest code-versus-design divergence the chapter holds, and it is not a matter of vocabulary — the two policies differ in observable behavior for a late co-signer. Which governs, and was the whitepaper's rationale weighed and rejected or simply not seen?
9. Where is the rule that a `tsg`'s `(sn, said)` names the last establishment event written down normatively? It is stated in a keripy docstring and, as far as this pass could find, in no KERI specification, no ACDC specification, and not in the KRAM whitepaper either. Two implementations got it wrong in the same direction and corrected it independently within six days, each from breakage rather than from a document ([section 5](#convergent-correction)). The rule decides which key state a signature is checked against, which is to say what a signature means, and it is the clearest case the corpus has of [section 5](#normative-status)'s residual costing something concrete. Its behavior under delegated establishment, superseding and recovery events is also unexamined — flagged as unexamined, not as a problem.
10. Added 2026-10-03: should KRAM's acceptance time be something other protocols may rely on? At `80f77b73b` IPEX's presentation-registry check requires a completed KRAM acceptance and uses its `rdt` as a cutoff ([section 8](#acceptance-time)). That makes a receiver-local bookkeeping value into evidence for a credential-level verdict, and it couples presentation validity to whether KRAM is enabled — on this chapter's inference, not on a test. The whitepaper does not contemplate it, and neither does #1613, whose own coupling runs through the escrow rather than the timestamp. If the dependency is intended, it is a second reason KRAM needs a normative home (question 1); if it is not, the presentation check needs a time source that does not disappear when an operator sets `"enabled": false`.
11. Added 2026-10-03: is the config file the durable record of window classes? `Baser.clean()` now rebuilds cache types from configuration rather than copying them ([section 7](#clean-keeps-replay-markers)). A runtime `changeConfig` that is not also written to the config file would be lost on a clean, and the staged two-step reconfiguration that defends against the gap attacks of [section 4](#gap-attacks) could then be half-applied across one. Whether `changeConfig` persists to the file was not checked.
